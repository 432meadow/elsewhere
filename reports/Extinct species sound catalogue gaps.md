# Filling the silences between eighty-three voices

The lost cast of [ what can no longer be heard ] is missing about sixty voices that the evidence can support, and roughly thirty of them can be added now with firm dates, usable acoustic detail and no open cultural question. The strongest gaps are not more obscure island birds. They are a **Javan lapwing whose 1920s call transcriptions can be synthesized almost syllable by syllable**, two birds with real recordings (the **Bahama nuthatch, 6.1/6.4 kHz, lost around 2019**, and the **purple-winged ground dove, a 0.5 s rising coo under 500 Hz**), a set of frogs with full published call tables (the **Kihansi spray toad at 4,071 Hz**, the golden coquí at 5 kHz, Poynton's forest toad, three Andean harlequin toads, the northern Darwin's frog), and a group of "kin" voices that still live elsewhere but have fallen silent over huge mapped areas: **gibbon song across central and southern China, the Sumatran rhino's whale-like song in Malaysia, the saiga's 44.5 Hz nasal roar on the Pontic steppe, the corncrake over lowland England, the prairie-chicken's 299 Hz boom across the Canadian prairies, and the Siberian crane's 1.4 kHz whistle at Keoladeo**. Underwater, the large yellow croaker's "gu-gu" spawning chorus of the East China Sea is the natural partner to the existing cod chorus; among non-animal sounds, drowned waterfalls (Guaíra 1982, Celilo 1957, Kettle Falls 1940, the Dnieper Rapids 1932) have exact coordinates, exact dates and described sound. The same sweep shows where the map must stay quiet: Amazonia, the Congo basin, most of Siberia and Mongolia, Canada north of 60° and continental Antarctica have almost nothing that is both firmly dated and still gone. Roughly twenty strong candidates, including Celilo and Kettle Falls, sturgeon thunder at Keshena Falls, the Onondaga cicada brood, Lake Suwa's ice ridges and the red wolf, should wait for consultation. Everything below carries the researchers' own limits: **every bounding box is their estimate from place names, many numbers reached them only through search-engine summaries, and several primary papers could not be opened.**

## How the gap list is ranked, and what the researchers could not verify

Six research files swept the standard extinction lists against the 83 implemented voices and the earlier catalogue: Wikipedia's lists of recently extinct birds (451 entries), mammals, amphibians (about 185 taxa) and insects, the Search for Lost Birds list (144 taxa), and around 320 individual species pages, plus primary nineteenth-century texts read directly from archive.org and journal PDFs ([List of extinct bird species since 1500](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_extinct_bird_species_since_1500); [Search for Lost Birds](https://www.searchforlostbirds.org/birds); [List of recently extinct mammals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_recently_extinct_mammals); [List of recently extinct amphibians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_recently_extinct_amphibians); [List of recently extinct insects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_recently_extinct_insects)). The headline count is sobering. About **185 lost bird taxa** in the swept regions are in neither the implemented cast nor the earlier catalogue, yet only around 35 have any voice evidence. Of some 185 lost amphibians, only about twenty have an acoustic record at all. No extinct reptile has a recorded voice. Hawaiʻi and Aotearoa New Zealand were skipped throughout and nothing from them appears below.

Each candidate carries one of four provenance labels, which tell the synthesis how literal it may be. **RECORDED** means a real recording of the lost taxon or the lost population exists. **KIN** means the same species is alive elsewhere or in captivity, so the voice is fully known while its presence on the lost range is what has ended. **DESCRIBED** means someone who heard it put the sound into words. **INFERRED** means only a living relative remains. The rank inside each table follows the researchers' own grades, harmonised: **A** is a recorded, measured or richly described voice with a firm date and place that is still gone; **B** is a thin description, a kin voice without published numbers, or a voice that has returned or been refound; **C** is inference only, or a poorly dated loss. Rows are ordered strongest first inside each region and kind.

Four caveats apply to every row and are not repeated each time. First, **all bounding boxes are `[lon west, lat south, lon east, lat north]` and every one was estimated by the researchers from place names**; none comes from a published range polygon, and where two researchers boxed the same place their boxes differ by up to a degree. Where the notes give only a point, it is shown as `pt lon, lat` and a box of a few tenths of a degree around it is a reasonable placeholder. Second, facts marked **[ss]** reached the researchers only as a search-engine summary of the linked page and need checking against the page before any public text quotes them. Third, the main archives were unreachable in every pass: Macaulay Library, xeno-canto, the IUCN Red List, BirdLife Data Zone, AmphibiaWeb and DOSITS all refused automated access, so catalogue numbers and status labels are second-hand, and "kin, recordable" means only that the species is alive and studied, not that an archive number was verified. Fourth, these primary papers were not opened and hold numbers the report could not get: Jaslow 1979 and Cocroft et al. 1990 (harlequin toads), Penna & Veloso 1990 (Darwin's frog), Drewry & Rand 1983 and Drewry & Jones 1976 (coquís), the "Vocalizations" section of Olson & Rauzon 2011 (Wake rail), Prŷs-Jones 1979 (Aldabra warbler), the full text of Hume 2023 (Mascarene booby), Turvey et al. 2015 (gibbon gazetteers, HTTP 403), Kershenbaum et al. 2016 (red wolf howl), Liu et al. 2016 and Yen et al. 2013 (deer ruts), McShane et al. 1995 (sea otter), Burney & Ramilisonina 1998 (kidoky), Schärer et al. 2012 (Nassau grouper), Cronwright-Schreiner 1925 (springbok treks) and the Scheele et al. 2019 supplement listing the 90 chytrid extinctions. "No voice found" therefore means not found in these sessions, not proven absent.

## Birds: 185 uncovered taxa, about 35 with any voice, and the strongest are kin

Birds give the longest list and the widest spread of evidence. The pattern across both hemispheres is the same: the long-extinct island birds are almost all voiceless in the record, a small number of recently lost birds were actually recorded, and the largest untapped group is regional extinctions of species that can still be recorded elsewhere.

### The Americas and Atlantic islands: two recorded losses and one island heard by one man in one season

About 75 lost bird taxa in this region were uncovered by neither list, and roughly fifteen have a recording or a usable first-hand description. The richest single cluster is **Guadalupe Island, Mexico**, where Walter Bryant, between December 1885 and April 1886, wrote voice notes for six taxa that are all now gone ([Bryant 1887](https://archive.org/details/bulletinofcalifo021887cali)). Two of the six (caracara, storm petrel) were catalogued earlier without their primary sources; they are listed here because the whole island is best built as one scene from one notebook. No measured frequency, duration or spectrogram exists for any Guadalupe bird, the Grand Cayman thrush, Semper's warbler, the kinglet calyptura, the Jamaican wood rail or the Cuban ivory-bill; only the nuthatch and the ground dove have numbers.

## Mammals: the loud ones are silenced by region, not extinct

About 25 mammal voices are missing and usable, and the strongest are not globally extinct species. The truly extinct small mammals on the lists (hutias, *Nesophontes*, rice rats, island woodrats, Australian hopping mice, gazelles, the sea mink) have **no recorded or described voice at all**, so the implemented "something small in the cay grass" treatment already says what can honestly be said about them. What the cast lacks is the set of large, loud animals that are extinct in the wild or silenced across a mapped region while their voices are recorded elsewhere. The existing "wolves", "lions", "a tiger", "elk bugling" and "the bison herds" absorb most lost carnivore and ungulate subspecies; the red wolf is the one canid whose howl differs enough to stand alone.

## Frogs and reptiles: nine published call tables and one alligator chorus

The acoustic record of lost amphibians sits in three clusters: Neotropical harlequin toads, Puerto Rican coquís and Queensland stream frogs. Africa and Asia contribute only the Kihansi spray toad, Poynton's forest toad and the Hula painted frog. Vocalisations are known for only 23 of more than 100 *Atelopus* species, a gap the authors link to the declines themselves ([Lötters et al. 2019](https://herpetozoa.pensoft.net/article/39192/)). Three of the best candidates are barely audible, which matters for design: with the implemented golden toad they form a set in which the habitat carries the scene and the animal is a trace. Year "last heard" is rarely separated from "last seen"; only the Kihansi toad (two males heard, January 2004) and the golden coquí (one unconfirmed call, 1986) have explicit heard dates.

## Insects: a 14 kHz cicada and three dated cicada silences

Three whole-soundscape findings give the mode a quantitative floor without naming a species. Reconstructed soundscapes at more than 200,000 sites show a pervasive loss of acoustic diversity and intensity in the spring dawn chorus across North America and Europe over 25 years, by a method (synthesis from survey data plus species recordings) close to what "elsewhere" itself does ([Morrison et al. 2021](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/id/eprint/81972)). Human noise has doubled background sound in 63% of US protected areas ([Buxton et al. 2017](https://angelonilab.colostate.edu/Site/Publications_files/Buxton_et_al_2017.pdf)). Off California, ocean noise at 30–50 Hz rose 10–12 dB between the 1960s and 2003–04 ([McDonald et al. 2006](https://www.cetus.ucsd.edu/docs/publications/McDonaldJASA2006.pdf)).

## Ten regions the cast leaves quiet, and what could honestly fill them

One researcher was asked to test ten thin regions directly. The result divides them cleanly. Western Europe, China and mainland Southeast Asia can be filled well. Scandinavia has modest, firmly dated candidates, all in the far south. Siberia, Mongolia, tropical Africa, South America beyond the Atlantic Forest and pampas, Canada's north and Antarctica have very little that is both firmly dated and still gone, and there the honest choice is one or two kin voices or continued wind. **Gibbons in China and tigers in Indochina fill the largest empty areas with one well-documented voice each.**

## Checked and not recommended

**Invalid or doubtful taxa.** Giving these a voice risks sounding something that was never a distinct animal: the hooded and Tumaco seedeaters (both now lumped), the Rio de Janeiro antwren (treated as a colour morph), the Texas Henslow's sparrow and Amak song sparrow (subspecies no longer accepted; frame them as lost local populations if the Houston or Amak story is wanted), Townsend's dickcissel, Bartram's "painted vulture", the Cabo San Lucas ani, Vaurie's nightjar, the Cape Verde kite, and the Okinawa flying fox (moved to Data Deficient) ([Yellow-bellied seedeater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellow-bellied_seedeater); [Henslow's sparrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henslow%27s_sparrow); [Rio de Janeiro antwren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rio_de_Janeiro_antwren); [Okinawa flying fox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Okinawa_flying_fox)). The earlier report's **"Borodino thrush" does not exist**: it conflates the Bonin thrush with the Borodino (Daitō) bush warbler on adjacent lines of a widely copied list, and the Daitō bush warbler itself is contradictory (called extinct, yet seen in 1984 and nesting in 2008) ([Facts and Details](https://factsanddetails.com/japan/cat26/sub164/item891.html); [ja.wikipedia](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ダイトウウグイス)). The Siau scops owl's "Ook-grrrrah" sits oddly with a species known from one 1866 skin, and the buff-breasted buttonquail's booming may have been another species, since targeted surveys in 2018–22 found none ([Siau scops owl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siau_scops_owl); [Buff-breasted buttonquail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buff-breasted_buttonquail)).

**Lost before about 1500, or undated.** The Caribbean ground sloths (latest date about 4,700 years ago), the Sardinian pika (extinct in antiquity; the "1774" date rests on probable brown rats), the mainland Tasmanian devil (about 3,000 years ago), the Atlas bear (youngest confirmed remains 8th century), the São Miguel and Madeiran scops owls (15th-century settlement), the Puerto Rican flower bat (subfossil only), and the St Helena hoopoe, cuckoo and rails, which sit at 1502–1600s with no sound evidence ([Megalocnus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megalocnus); [Sardinian pika](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sardinian_pika); [Atlas bear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlas_bear); [Puerto Rican flower bat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puerto_Rican_flower_bat); [Saint Helena hoopoe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Helena_hoopoe)).

**Still alive, refound or recovered.** The cherry-throated tanager, blue-eyed ground dove, Táchira antpitta, Santa Marta sabrewing, Belem curassow, Azores bullfinch and Cebu flowerpecker are extant; Stresemann's bristlefront (last seen 2020) is not yet declared lost. The Guadalupe fur seal, Juan Fernández fur seal, northern elephant seal and Antarctic fur seal are recovered. Brood X on Long Island, the English field cricket and the British mole cricket persist. The yellow-spotted bell frog and longnose stubfoot toad were refound. Several "lost" tepui and New Guinea birds are merely unvisited ([Cherry-throated tanager](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cherry-throated_tanager); [Guadalupe fur seal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guadalupe_fur_seal); [Atelopus longirostris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atelopus_longirostris); [Search for Lost Birds](https://www.searchforlostbirds.org/birds)).

**Voice wholly unknown.** Among birds: the Guanacaste hummingbird, Brace's and Gould's emeralds, coppery thorntail, Cayenne nightjar, Vilcabamba brushfinch, Zapata rail (the calls attributed to it may belong to the spotted rail), white-eyed river martin (silent in winter, breeding voice unknown), Sulu bleeding-heart, Negros fruit dove, spectacled cormorant, and almost every extinct bird of Tonga, the Marquesas, the Mascarenes and Australia's subspecies list. Among frogs: all 17 extinct Sri Lankan *Pseudophilautus*, Günther's streamlined frog, the spiny-knee leaf frog, the peppered tree frog (call never recorded), the Maracay harlequin frog and the Vegas Valley leopard frog; the salamanders on the list are treated as silent (the researcher's inference). Among mammals: the sea mink (no description of its voice exists), every extinct hutia, rice rat and hopping mouse, the oryxes and the bubal hartebeest. Among insects: the Central Valley grasshopper. Among reptiles: the Rodrigues and Jamaican giant geckos ([White-eyed river martin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White-eyed_river_martin); [Zapata rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zapata_rail); [Sea mink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_mink); [list of amphibians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_recently_extinct_amphibians); [Rodrigues giant day gecko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rodrigues_giant_day_gecko)).

**Near-silent, or known only as held knowledge.** The great bustard, bearded vulture, California and Andean condors and harpy eagle are firmly dated but give almost nothing to synthesize. The Marshall Islands "annañ" exists only as Marshallese oral tradition and should be treated like the Choiseul pigeon the project already declined ([Nauru reed warbler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nauru_reed_warbler)).

## Conclusion

The gap in the lost world is geographic more than taxonomic, and it is filled by a different kind of evidence than the first 83. The earlier cast was built from the last recordings and the best eyewitness prose of animals that are gone everywhere. The continents it leaves quiet have few such animals with a voice on record. What they have instead is range loss: the same song, measured in a living population, missing from a province, a steppe or a river since a known decade. That shifts the mode's centre of gravity from "this sound no longer exists" to "this sound no longer exists *here*", and it makes two data fields essential rather than optional: a `lost` year range and a `returned` year. At least fifteen of the candidates above are returning as this is written, among them the buttonquail (2026), the New Forest cicada (2025), the giant otter (2025), the takhi, the milu, the toki and the Kihansi toad. A lost world that could not show a voice coming back would misreport them.

Two further things follow. The best-measured new voices are disproportionately quiet ones: a toad audible at ten centimetres under a hundred-metre waterfall, a frog that chuckled once every five minutes, a cicada at 14 kHz. They extend the register the golden toad opened, in which the place carries the scene. And the strongest non-animal sounds are the ones where the sound was itself the thing taken, which is exactly why they cannot simply be put back. Four unopened papers (Jaslow 1979, Cocroft et al. 1990, Penna & Veloso 1990, Drewry & Rand 1983), two public recordings that nobody measured (the Wyoming toad at Acoustic Atlas, the Fonoteca Zoológica frogs) and a gazetteer check of every box would turn most of the remaining "search summary" flags into numbers.
