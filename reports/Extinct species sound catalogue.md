# Tuning a world of vanished voices

Across eight research files, the evidence supports a [ what can no longer be heard ] world of roughly three hundred candidate taxa and sound sources. Only about two dozen of the lost ones survive as real audio of the animal itself. These include the Kauaʻi ʻōʻō, kāmaʻo and ʻōʻū (Alakaʻi Swamp, 1975), the ivory-billed woodpecker (Singer Tract, 1935), Bachman's warbler (1950s), the baiji (Wuhan, 1980–2002), the Christmas Island pipistrelle's last echolocation call (26 Aug 2009), the two gastric-brooding frogs (Keith McDonald), the Atitlán grebe (LaBastille), and the last wild Spix's macaw (1993, 2000). Everything else rests on words, on human imitations, or on inference from living relatives. The world can still be built at depth, because the DESCRIBED tier is unexpectedly rich. Wallace Craig gave the passenger pigeon's cries in metronome tempi and at a pitch near B5. Perkins (1903) says when and in what weather each Hawaiian bird called, and Hull (1909) set a Lord Howe warbler's song to "Pop Goes the Weasel". Leguat heard a Rodrigues solitaire's wings at 200 paces, and Hornaday heard rutting bison "like distant thunder" 1–3 miles off. The IMITATION tier is small but carries the most cultural weight. It includes Hēnare Hāmana's 1948/49 whistled huia duet, the Hawaiian kia manu lure calls, and names that are themselves calls (quagga, kākāwahie, ʻōʻō). It needs consent before it is voiced. The "secret traces" in the ordinary world are strongest where a recorded or vividly described last voice meets a dated last place. Examples are the unanswered ʻōʻō at Halepaʻakai Stream (1987), Booming Ben on his Martha's Vineyard lek (1932), the pipistrelle's fern clearing on Christmas Island (2009), and Martha and Incas at Cincinnati Zoo (1914, 1918). Every species that may still survive or is being searched for needs a blurred location. Treat the catalogue as a working reference, not a finished gazetteer. Every research pass ran out of web-search budget. Macaulay Library and xeno-canto pages were bot-blocked, so archive IDs come from search snippets. Almost all coordinates are the researchers' own approximations from place names, and only one recording (ML6050) was measured spectrally.

## How to read the catalogue: two labels, one date, and approximate pins

The artwork needs two independent tags per voice, which the research converged on. **Axis A, provenance**, says how the sound is known. **RECORDED** means real audio of the taxon exists, and this catalogue splits it into wild, captive, conspecific (a living population of the same species) and unconfirmed. **DESCRIBED** means a named observer wrote down the sound. **IMITATION** means a documented human imitation, lure device or onomatopoeic name. **INFERRED** means nothing survives beyond anatomy and living relatives. **Axis B, status**, is a verdict with its date and authority. A third field, **"last certain voice"**, records the year and place of the last accepted recording or record, kept apart from the verdict year ([Wikipedia: Ivory-billed woodpecker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivory-billed_woodpecker); [reginfo.gov RIN 1018-BC98](https://www.reginfo.gov/public/do/eAgendaViewRule?pubId=202504&RIN=1018-BC98)). Status should come from IUCN/BirdLife first and the national body (USFWS, DOC NZTCS, EPBC) second. Where the two disagree, show both. The ivory-bill is the test case: **IUCN CR(PE), USFWS Endangered with a delisting decision pending since 2021, NatureServe "Presumed Extinct"** ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivory-billed_woodpecker)).

Two sets of conventions apply throughout. First, every lat/lon below is approximate. Unless marked "sourced", it is the note-taker's own geocode of a named place or an island centroid, not a figure from the cited source, and each must be checked against a gazetteer before shipping. Second, the regional notes flag their own research limits, and this report carries those through. The North America, Hawaiʻi, South Pacific, NZ/Australia, Latin America, Old World, marine/insect and ethics passes all record exhausted search budgets or blocked archives. So "no voice description found" often means *not found this session*, not *none exists*.

## Strongest candidates by provenance tier

The four tiers call for different synthesis strategies. A RECORDED voice can be modelled from real spectra, which fits the artwork's rule to synthesize from measurement and never play the file. A DESCRIBED voice gives contour, rhythm, timing and scale, and needs a living model for timbre. IMITATION gives a human's memory of contour and should carry the imitator's name. INFERRED should sound either restrained or honestly absent. One tested rule from the North America pass is worth adopting: **reserve literal-sounding traces for RECORDED taxa, and give DESCRIBED or INFERRED taxa a more abstract, "remembered" timbre**.

Unconfirmed recordings form a separate subclass: *Mystacina robusta* "mystacinid-like" echolocation in 1999, a South Island kōkako candidate in 2021, Maui ʻākepa audio in 1994–95, and the Project Principalis ivory-bill audio, which Michalak (2024) judged closer to great horned owl ([Wikipedia: NZ greater short-tailed bat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_greater_short-tailed_bat); [Wikipedia: Maui ʻākepa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maui_%CA%BB%C4%81kepa)). Conspecific recordings supply voices for extinct subpopulations with high fidelity. Examples are greater prairie-chicken for the heath hen, living *Magicicada septendecim* for lost Brood XI, living wolves, cranes and bitterns for extirpations, plains zebra for the quagga, and Tuamotu sandpiper for its lost congeners. Extinct-in-the-wild and refound species remain recordable: Socorro dove, ʻalalā, sihek, koʻkoʻ, Alagoas curassow, Panamanian golden frog, night parrot and Jerdon's courser.

### DESCRIBED: where words are good enough to build from

The richest written voices are the **passenger pigeon** (Craig 1911: keck at "b above the treble staff", chatter at ♩=120–180, nest-call ♩=192, falling a semitone) ([Craig 1911](https://archive.org/details/sim_auk_1911-10_28_4)). Next come the **Hawaiian forest birds in Perkins 1903**, which specifies dawn and dusk choruses, weather gating and bark-rattling ([Perkins 1903](https://archive.org/details/agk2408.0001.004.umich.edu)), and **Hull 1909 for Lord Howe and Norfolk** ([Hull 1909](https://archive.org/details/biostor-54912)). The **Mascarene explorers** (Leguat, Tafforet, van West-Zanen, Vosmaer) are collected by Hume ([Hume 2007](https://julianhume.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2010/07/Hume-Mascarene-Parrots.pdf)). Further strong accounts are the **laughing owl** ([NZ Birds Online](https://www.nzbirdsonline.org.nz/species/laughing-owl)), the **piopio's five-bar song** ([NZ Birds Online](https://www.nzbirdsonline.org.nz/species/south-island-piopio)), the **thylacine's double yap** ([Tasmania Parks](https://parks.tas.gov.au/discovery-and-learning/wildlife/tasmanian-tiger)) and **Steller's sea cow snorting every 4–5 minutes** ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steller%27s_sea_cow)). At the scale of whole landscapes, **Hornaday's bison rut** ([Hornaday 1889](https://gutenberg.org/cache/epub/17748/pg17748-images.html)) and the **Rocky Mountain locust swarm** ([Wagner 2008](https://history.nebraska.gov/wp-content/uploads/2017/11/doc_publications_NH2008Grasshoppered.pdf)) give the densest descriptions.

### IMITATION: human memory of a bird, which needs human credit

The strongest items are Hēnare Hāmana's whistled male and female huia calls (1948/49, Ngā Taonga 26325) ([Ngā Taonga](https://ngataonga.org.nz/search-use-collection/search/26325)); the Hawaiian kia manu lure call for the mamo and Molokaʻi elders' imitation of the moho (Hawaiian rail) ([Perkins 1903](https://archive.org/details/agk2408.0001.004.umich.edu)); and passenger-pigeon hunters' throat calls and silk-band callers ([Craig 1911](https://archive.org/details/sim_auk_1911-10_28_4)). Many names are also imitations of the call. They include quagga ("kwa-ha-ha") ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quagga)), Guaraní *gua'a hovy* for the glaucous macaw ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glaucous_macaw)), Lord Howe's "cudgimeruk" starling and "Morepork" boobook ([Hull 1909](https://archive.org/details/biostor-54912)), kākāwahie ("wood-chopping") ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kakawahie)), Tuamotuan *kivi-kivi* ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuamotu_sandpiper)), and the Choiseul pigeon's "c-r-r-ooo" from Indigenous informants ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choiseul_pigeon)).

### INFERRED: the honest majority

Most lost mammals, rodents, island rails, parakeets and Caribbean birds fall here. The research gives three principled options for them. The first is a timbre from the nearest living relative, clearly labelled. The second is movement sound instead of voice: hopping cadence for the desert rat-kangaroo, digging for the bilby, wing-whirr. The third is silence, which the notes argue is the truthful rendering for **Lyall's wren**, the "voiceless" **golden toad** and the "nearly mute" **Steller's sea cow** ([NZ Birds Online](https://www.nzbirdsonline.org.nz/species/lyalls-wren); [Savage 1966](https://tropicalstudies.org/rbt/attachments/volumes/vol14-2/02-Savage-Bufo.pdf)).

## The regional catalogue

Each region opens with a short synthesis and then gives a table. The columns are: names (common / Indigenous / scientific); historical range, habitat, season and abundance; last record with an approximate pin; status; provenance tier with who, when and archive; synthesis notes with a living model; and culture, flags and sources. "Model" means the researchers' suggested living proxy. Unless a source is attached, treat it as an inference.

### North America and the North Atlantic: one recorded woodpecker, one metronome-marked pigeon

This region holds the catalogue's two great abundance voices, the passenger pigeon and the Carolina parakeet. Both last individuals died at the same zoo, which makes **Cincinnati Zoo (~39.145 N, 84.508 W) a double secret trace**: Martha on 1 Sep 1914 and Incas on 21 Feb 1918 ([Wikipedia: Passenger pigeon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passenger_pigeon); [Feathered Friends](https://featheredfriends.substack.com/p/carolina-parakeet-portrait-of-a-vanished)). Two famous last birds survive only on silent film: the heath hen (Gross, 1928/1931, Bowdoin) and the imperial woodpecker (Rhein, 1956) ([Bowdoin ArchivesSpace](https://archivesspace.bowdoin.edu/repositories/2/archival_objects/48321); [Lammertink et al. 2011](https://www.bioone.org/journals/the-auk/volume-128/issue-4/auk.2011.10271/Film-Documentation-of-the-Probably-Extinct-Imperial-Woodpecker-Campephilus-imperlalis/10.1525/auk.2011.10271.full)). The 2008 *Séance Vocibus Avium* broadcast, in which musicians voiced 11 extinct birds from descriptions, is artistic precedent, not evidence ([Earbirding](https://earbirding.com/blog/?p=316)).

### Hawaiʻi: the densest lost dawn chorus on Earth, with Perkins as its score

Hawaiʻi gives the mode its richest combination of real audio, first-hand description and Indigenous imitation. The USFWS rule of 17 Oct 2023 (88 FR 71644, effective 16 Nov 2023) delisted **eight Hawaiian birds**: Kauaʻi ʻakialoa, Kauaʻi nukupuʻu, Kauaʻi ʻōʻō, kāmaʻo, Maui ʻākepa, Maui nukupuʻu, kākāwahie and poʻouli. It left the ʻōʻū and olomaʻo listed ([GovInfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-10-17/html/2023-22377.htm); [USFWS](https://www.fws.gov/species-publication-action/removal-21-species-list-endangered-and-threatened-wildlife)). Perkins's *Fauna Hawaiiensis* (1903) doubles as a timing engine. The kāmaʻo "is of all birds the first to make itself heard in the morning… while the stars were still shining". ʻŌʻō are silent in hot sun but "vociferous" in fog and rain. On Kauaʻi "in the early morning and again towards dark the woods fairly resound" with ʻōʻō and kāmaʻo ([Perkins 1903](https://archive.org/details/agk2408.0001.004.umich.edu)).

**Conflict: who made the "last male" Kauaʻi ʻōʻō recording?** EarthSky credits **Jim Jacobi, 1986**, with a remix posted by Robert Davis in 2009 ([EarthSky](https://earthsky.org/earth/extinct-bird-songs-woodpecker-warbler-kauai-oo/)). Wikipedia credits **David Boynton, 1987**, and says the recording is in Macaulay ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaua%CA%BBi_%CA%BB%C5%8D%CA%BB%C5%8D)). Hawaiʻi Public Radio's "last Kauaʻi ʻōʻō" episode actually plays **Zeillemaker's 1975 ML6049**, made before the female was lost ([HPR](https://www.hawaiipublicradio.org/podcast/manu-minute/2020-12-16/manu-minute-the-last-kauaˊi-ˊoˊo)). The widely circulated "lonely male" audio may therefore be a 1975 duet-era recording. The 1986 and 1987 recordings could not be matched to public ML catalogue numbers. The USFWS last record, **28 Apr 1987 at Halepaʻakai Stream**, was a vocal answer to playback, and it can anchor the trace whoever recorded the famous clip. Hurricane Iwa struck on **23 Nov 1982**. HPR's date of 1983 is an error ([Wikipedia: Hurricane Iwa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_Iwa)). A separate Wikipedia attribution of a "Boynton 1987" recording to the ʻōʻū looks like a conflation with the ʻōʻō.

Perkins lists ʻalalā, ʻelepaio, ʻōʻō and kioea as onomatopoeic names, and ʻapapane, ʻakikeke and kākāwahie as named from their sounds. Hawaiian names are therefore part of the acoustic record, not a translation layer ([Perkins 1903](https://archive.org/details/agk2408.0001.004.umich.edu)). Featherwork is the central cultural context. ʻAhu ʻula used red ʻiʻiwi and ʻapapane, yellow ʻōʻō and mamo, and occasionally green ʻōʻū feathers, and Kamehameha I placed a kapu requiring black-and-yellow birds to be released after plucking ([Wikipedia: ʻAhu ʻula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%CA%BBAhu_%CA%BBula); [Hawaiʻi Alive](https://www.hawaiialive.org/resource/bird-catching)). The "450,000 feathers from 80,000 birds" figure is a popular estimate, not verified. The Oʻahu deceptor bush cricket (*Leptogryllus deceptor*, last recorded 1910 or earlier) is the only Hawaiian insect candidate, and its song is unknown ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leptogryllus_deceptor)). Not covered: ʻōlelo noʻeau (Pukui 1983), oli and mele that imitate birds, and Munro 1944.

### Micronesia, Bonin/Ogasawara and the Ryukyus: a forest silenced by one snake

Guam gives the mode its clearest "before and after". The **sihek (~3,000 in the early 1980s, ~30 by 1986)** and the **koʻkoʻ (60,000–80,000 in the late 1960s)** collapsed within about two decades under the brown tree snake. Both survive in captivity or reintroduction, so their voices are recordable ([Smithsonian](https://nationalzoo.si.edu/animals/guam-kingfisher); [Wikipedia: Guam rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guam_rail)). The sihek's first call comes "at about the same time every day, usually at dawn", which gives a precise diel anchor ([Tracy Aviary](https://tracyaviary.org/liberty-park/birds-nature/guam-kingfisher)). The Bonin voices depend on Kittlitz (1827–28), whose monograph was not accessible. No Hz values were found for any bird in this region. The Mariana swiftlet's paired 3–10 kHz echolocation clicks are the one quantitative sound ([Wikipedia: Swiftlet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiftlet)).

### Lord Howe, Norfolk and Christmas Island: Hull's 1909 notebook, then the rats

**Black rats landed from the SS *Makambo* in June 1918, and "within two years 40% of Lord Howe's endemic bird species were extinct"** ([Wikipedia: Tasman starling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tasman_starling)). A. F. Basset Hull's 1909 paper was written in the austral spring and summer, just before this collapse (Lord Howe from 3 Oct 1907; Norfolk Oct 1908–Feb 1909). It is effectively a field recording in prose, and several islanders' names for birds are imitations of their calls ([Hull 1909, OCR text](https://archive.org/download/biostor-54912/biostor-54912_djvu.txt)). Island centroids are about 31.55 S, 159.08 E (Lord Howe), 29.03 S, 167.95 E (Norfolk), 29.12 S, 167.95 E (Phillip Island) and 10.49 S, 105.62 E (Christmas Island). These are general geography, not sourced.

**Attribution warning.** The widely quoted phrase "harsh and disagreeable in the extreme" belongs to the **New Zealand** kākā (Yate, via Gould), not the Norfolk bird. Gould's own words for *Nestor productus*, from a captive, are "a hoarse, quacking, inharmonious noise, sometimes resembling the barking of a dog" ([Gould OCR 13874](https://digital.lib.ku.edu/islandora/object/ku-gould%3A13874/datastream/OCR/download); [Gould OCR 17825](https://digital.lib.ku.edu/islandora/object/ku-gould%3A17825/datastream/OCR/download)).

### Polynesia and Melanesia: painted by Cook's artists, voiced by informants

Most Society Islands losses are known from Forster, Ellis and Webber paintings made on Cook's voyages. So the birds have images and Tahitian names but almost no voices ([Tahiti sandpiper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tahiti_sandpiper); [Jansen et al. 2021, BBOC](https://bioone.org/journals/bulletin-of-the-british-ornithologists-club/volume-141/issue-2/bboc.v141i2.2021.a4/Taxonomic-implications-of-the-original-illustrations-of-Prosobonia-from-Tahiti/10.25226/bboc.v141i2.2021.a4.full)). The single strongest Melanesian account comes from Indigenous informants, not Europeans. The Choiseul pigeon gave "a beautiful rising and falling whistling call… from the roost site every evening" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choiseul_pigeon)). Tonga, Pitcairn and Vanuatu produced no candidates, but searches were cut short, so this is not a confirmed absence.

### Aotearoa New Zealand: a tapu imitation, a laughing owl, and a kōkako that may still sing

Status here comes from the DOC NZ Threat Classification System. NZTCS 2021 lists **62 extinct bird taxa**, and only two are Data Deficient: South Island kōkako and South Island brown teal ([DOC NZTCS 36](https://www.doc.govt.nz/globalassets/documents/science-and-technical/nztcs36entire.pdf)). The region's defining sound is not a recording. It is the huia, known only through **Hēnare Hāmana's 1948/49 whistled imitation**, which comes out of the Māori practice of luring huia to snares by imitating their call ([Evans, "Echoes of the past"](https://www.thomvandooren.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/Evans-Echoes-of-the-Past.pdf)). Sources conflict on the recording's details. Evans/NZ Geographic give 2YA studios, a 10-inch acetate, 1949. Ngā Taonga's blog gives Columbus Recording Studios, Wakefield Street, 1948 "(or 1949)", 78 rpm. The catalogue gives 1949, Wellington, 3:50, RNZ Collection ([Ngā Taonga blog](https://www.ngataonga.org.nz/blog/nz-history/the-call-of-the-huia/); [Ngā Taonga 26325](https://ngataonga.org.nz/search-use-collection/search/26325)). No source was found for a "Manakau 1954" recording mentioned in the brief. Treat it as a probable conflation until Ngā Taonga confirms. The pre-1500 taxa (moa, Haast's eagle, owlet-nightjar) are borderline for a post-1500 world. If used, flag them as "pre-contact memory".

### Australia: recorded frogs, a filmed-but-silent thylacine, and a mammal fauna best heard as footfalls

Australia's recorded dead are frogs and a bat. The **southern and northern gastric-brooding frogs** were recorded by Keith McDonald and called in October–November from rock crevices by rainforest streams ([FrogID R. silus](https://www.frogid.net.au/frogs/rheobatrachus-silus/); [FrogID R. vitellinus](https://www.frogid.net.au/frogs/rheobatrachus-vitellinus/)). The thylacine was filmed in 1933 (Fleay, silent) and 1935 (*Tasmania the Wonderland*, with a soundtrack of narration or music), but "few thylacine vocalisations were reported and unfortunately none recorded" ([NFSA](https://www.nfsa.gov.au/latest/tasmanian-tiger-last-known-footage-thylacine); [NFSA colourised](https://www.nfsa.gov.au/latest/colourised-footage-last-tasmanian-tiger)). The lost mammals are almost entirely voiceless in the record. The notes argue they should be heard as movement at historical density: hop cadence, galloping patter, digging, rustle ([List of extinct animals of Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_extinct_animals_of_Australia); Woinarski et al. 2015, [PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1417301112), not accessed).

### Latin America, the Caribbean, Bermuda, Galápagos and the Falklands: recent recorded losses and the chytrid silence

The region's recorded losses are recent Brazilian Atlantic Forest and Caatinga birds. Butchart et al. (2018) formalised their status, recommending Extinct for the cryptic treehunter and Alagoas foliage-gleaner and Extinct in the Wild for Spix's macaw ([Mongabay 2018](https://news.mongabay.com/2018/09/8-species-of-birds-have-possibly-gone-extinct-over-past-few-decades/); [NRDC](https://www.nrdc.org/stories/what-eight-latest-bird-extinctions-can-tell-us); [Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/study-says-last-decade-4-birds-went-extinct-and-4-more-are-likely-gone-180970226/)). The second group is Central American and Puerto Rican frogs lost to chytrid. Scheele et al. (2019) count declines in **at least 501 amphibian species and 90 presumed extinctions**, worst in large, range-restricted anurans of the wet Americas and Australia ([NESP](https://www.nespthreatenedspecies.edu.au/publications-and-tools/deadly-frog-fungus-has-wiped-out-90-species-and-threatens-hundreds-more); [FAPESP](https://agencia.fapesp.br/fungus-has-decimated-the-populations-of-501-amphibian-species-worldwide/30127/)). The species list of the 90 was not retrieved. Several famous lost frogs were near-silent. The golden toad was described as "voiceless" and gave at most a low release chirp, and Holdridge's toad is mute. The honest render is therefore the surrounding community in fog and rain, not an invented advertisement call ([Savage 1966](https://tropicalstudies.org/rbt/attachments/volumes/vol14-2/02-Savage-Bufo.pdf)).

**Spix's macaw status is unstable and should be labelled "EW → reintroduced 2022 → uncertain 2025–26".** Wikipedia says 52 birds had been released by June 2022, with more in December 2022. By Nov 2025 ICMBio confirmed all **11 surviving wild birds** had circovirus, and another source said only ~20 were "initially freed". On 27 May 2026 authorities moved 69 macaws and described the species as having no wild population ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spix%27s_macaw); [BirdGuides](https://www.birdguides.com/news/lethal-virus-strikes-wild-population-of-spixs-macaw-in-brazil/); [Mongabay 2025](https://news.mongabay.com/2025/10/virus-outbreak-deepens-rift-over-return-of-spixs-macaw-to-brazil/); [Malay Mail](https://malaymail.com/news/life/2025/11/29/all-endangered-wild-spixs-macaws-in-brazil-test-positive-for-deadly-and-incurable-circovirus/199989); [Emol](https://www.emol.com/noticias/Tendencias/2026/05/27/1201173/rescatan-guacamayos-azules-brasil.html)).

### Europe, Asia, Africa and the Middle East: a recorded river dolphin and a quagga that said its own name

The region holds two of the strongest RECORDED marine and terrestrial voices, the baiji and the northern white rhino, and one striking IMITATION. The quagga's "shrill, barking neigh" is the source of its Khoikhoi name, "kwa-ha-ha" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quagga); [New World Encyclopedia](https://www.newworldencyclopedia.org/entry/Equus_quagga_quagga)). Most large mammals (aurochs, tarpan, bluebuck, bucardo, tigers, Barbary lion, kouprey) have no described voices and must be modelled from living relatives. Their power lies in dated last places.

### Mascarenes, Seychelles and Madagascar: sailors' ears on paradise

These islands have almost no recordings but some of the most vivid first-hand sound in the catalogue, collected by Julian Hume ([Hume 2007, Zootaxa 1513](https://julianhume.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2010/07/Hume-Mascarene-Parrots.pdf)). ### Oceans and rivers: a sea cow that barely breathed aloud, and a chorus six to twenty times fuller

Only the **baiji** gives true recordings of a now-extinct marine mammal (see Europe/Asia). Every other lost marine voice is DESCRIBED or INFERRED. At the soundscape scale, however, the ocean section holds the catalogue's best quantitative evidence for a louder past. The two key facts are **~240,000 humpback and ~360,000 fin whales in the pre-whaling North Atlantic, 6–20× today** ([Roman & Palumbi 2003](https://fdn.montana.edu/kalinowski/teaching/documents-and-images/14%20CONGEN%20-%20READING%20-%20Whales%20before%20whaling%20-%20Science.pdf); [Stanford](https://news.stanford.edu/pr/03/whales86.html)), and up to **12 dB** more shipping noise at 10–500 Hz than a few decades ago ([Hildebrand 2009](https://int-res.com/abstracts/meps/v395/p5-20)). A pre-industrial vs modern degradation of >15 dB at 50 Hz is reported only via a search summary ([Frontiers 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fmars.2021.689860/pdf)).

### Insects and swarms: a locust cloud larger than California, and cicada broods that simply did not come back

The **Rocky Mountain locust** is the single largest lost sound source on Earth. **Albert's swarm (June 1875)** is estimated at ~198,000 sq mi and ~12.5 trillion insects. Albert Child timed it from Plattsmouth, Nebraska (~41.0 N, 95.9 W) as it streamed past for five days. Wikipedia calls him "a physician interested in meteorology" and HistoryNet "a county judge and sometime meteorologist" ([Wikipedia: Albert's swarm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert%27s_swarm); [HistoryNet](https://historynet.com/1874-the-year-of-the-locust/)). Eyewitnesses give three separable layers. There was a wing roar "like a rushing storm" (Sheldon, July 1874). There was a near-field "continuous cracking and snapping sound, which increased to a perfect roar" at ~100 yards (Finch, 1876), and jaws that "crackled like a deadly fire". Finally, the insects hit "the ground and the house with the noise of a hailstorm", a phrase quoted only via search summary ([Wagner 2008](https://history.nebraska.gov/wp-content/uploads/2017/11/doc_publications_NH2008Grasshoppered.pdf); [Outdoor Nebraska](https://magazine.outdoornebraska.gov/stories/history-and-culture/clouds-of-grasshoppers-in-1874/)). **The last specimens were collected by Hebard in August 1904 in Colorado.** The Manitoba records of 1901–02 are the last Canadian ones, not the last overall ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocky_Mountain_locust)). No wing-beat or crepitation frequency exists for *M. spretus*.

### Bats: the extinction that was literally heard

The **Christmas Island pipistrelle** is the emblem here, because acoustic monitoring was how its decline was measured. During the August 2009 rescue attempt, "night after night, the devices recorded only a single individual". Lindy Lumsden gave the extinction date as 26 August 2009 ([artensterben.de](https://www.artensterben.de/en/christmas-island-pipistrelle/), citing Lumsden 2009, *Australasian Bat Society Newsletter* 33:21–25, and Woinarski 2018, *A Bat's End*). It fits the work's existing bat-detector framing (the moth variation). Other lost bats are known only from specimens: the Lord Howe long-eared bat and the greater short-tailed bat (tabled above); the **Guam flying fox** (last killed 1968); the **small Mauritian flying fox** (Mauritius 1859, Réunion 1862); the **large Palau flying fox** (*Pteropus pilosus*, two specimens before 1874); and the **Percy Island flying fox** (1874) ([Guam flying fox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guam_flying_fox); [Large Palau flying fox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large_Palau_flying_fox)). Flying-fox camps would have been audible squabbling at dawn and dusk returns. No *Pteropus* call parameters were retrieved. The Puerto Rican flower bat and Palau dusky flying fox were not researched.

### Abundance soundscapes: bison thunder, lek chorus and wolf howl as measurable scale

Hornaday (1889) is the richest abundance source. In the August–September "running season", herds of "many thousand" massed, and bulls "roar like a fog-horn". "The mingled roaring… form[s] a great volume of sound like distant thunder, which has often been heard at a distance of from 1 to 3 miles", and up to five miles in favourable air. A night stampede grew from "a low rumble" to "a thundering noise". At Nashville in 1770, "their bellowings sounded from the hills and forest". The great southern herd was ~4 million, and Dodge saw the Arkansas herd at ≥25 × 50 miles, 15–20 per acre ([Hornaday 1889](https://gutenberg.org/cache/epub/17748/pg17748-images.html)). Hornaday's 1889 total of ~1,091 comprised 541 captive, 256 wild plains and ~294 wood bison. "541" is often misquoted as the wild count ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Extermination_of_the_American_Bison); [Univ. Arizona](https://repository.arizona.edu/bitstream/handle/10150/639069/11258-10800-1-PB.pdf?sequence=1&isAllowed=y)). Pre-Columbian estimates of 30–60/65 million are contested. The modern rut bellow resembles "the roar of a lion" ([NPS](https://www.nps.gov/articles/bison-bellows-6-23-16.htm)).

Other abundance layers with usable parameters follow. **Prairie-chicken and heath-hen leks** boom at 270–325 Hz for ~1.9 s at spring dawn ([Acoustics.org](https://acoustics.org/category/meetings/169th-meeting/page/2)). **Wolf choruses** have F0 of ~150 Hz to >1 kHz and howls of 0.5–15.5 s (Iberian 270–720 Hz, 1.1–12.8 s), audible ≥10 km in open terrain ([Wikipedia: Howling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howling); [Palacios et al. 2007](https://bioone.org/journals/Journal-of-Mammalogy/volume-88/issue-3/06-MAMM-A-151R1.1/Iberian-Wolf-Howls--Acoustic-Structure-Individual-Variation-and-a/10.1644/06-MAMM-A-151R1.1.full)). **Red knots at Delaware Bay** fell ~75% from the 1980s to the 2000s, with a flock "knutt" and a migration "knuup-knuup" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_knot)). **Autumn salmon runs** are largely non-vocal: splashes, leaps, and gravel moved while digging redds, detectable by hydrophone ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salmon_run); [OSTI](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/962011); [USGS](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70026384)). Historic Columbia run sizes were not sourced. Bison, salmon and wolves carry deep Indigenous relationships (Buffalo Treaty, InterTribal Buffalo Council, First Salmon ceremonies), and these were *not* verified this session. Consult Indigenous-led sources before voicing them.

### Regional extirpations worth restoring: fully known voices, uncertain presence

Extirpated species with living conspecifics are the best-documented class. They are **RECORDED (conspecific)**, placed with a bounded loss date and often a return date. Their faintness should express *presence probability at that date*, not uncertainty about the voice. A workable data model stores a historical-range polygon, a `lost` year range (earliest to latest plausible) and an optional `returned` year.

## Secret traces: a shortlist of last places for the ordinary world

## Density: how much quieter the ordinary world has become

Density figures serve two jobs. They scale historical abundance inside the lost world, and they drive an optional density setting for the present. The most rigorous figures are these. **North America lost 2.9 billion birds (−28.8%) between 1970 and 2017**, and grassland birds lost **−53.3%** ([Rosenberg et al. 2019](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10133018)). **Europe lost 560–620 million birds between 1980 and 2017 (−17 to −19%)**, including ~247 million house sparrows ([NHM](https://nhm.ac.uk/discover/news/2021/november/600-million-birds-lost-across-europe-since-1980.html); [BirdLife](https://birdlife.org/news/2021/11/16/press-release-huge-declines-in-europe-birds-eurobirds); [RSPB](https://community.rspb.org.uk/ourwork/b/science/posts/dramatic-declines-in-eu-birds)). Rigal et al. find **−25% overall and nearly −60% for farmland** across continental Europe, which the CNRS gives as "800 million fewer birds". That total exceeds Burns because it covers different species and countries ([CNRS](https://www.cnrs.fr/en/node/7688); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10235941); [CREAF](https://www.creaf.cat/en/articles/intensive-farming-main-cause-bird-population-decline-europe)). Flying-insect biomass in German reserves fell **−76% seasonally and −82% in midsummer** between 1989 and 2016 ([Hallmann et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29045418/); [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2017-10-percent-decrease-total-insect-biomass.html); [Sussex](https://sro.sussex.ac.uk/id/eprint/70873/)).

The multipliers are the ethics researcher's derivation from Rosenberg's Table 1. The same researcher gives Europe farmland ×0.43 and all species ×0.75 (Rigal), or EU/UK all species ×0.82 (Burns). Europe's multiplier for insects would be ~×0.24, or ×0.18 in midsummer. Biomass is only a proxy for acoustic output. Four refinements matter. First, NEXRAD radar shows spring nocturnal migration biomass down **13.6 ± 9.1%** over 2007–2017 ([Rosenberg](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10133018)). Second, the **2025 US State of the Birds** lists 112 "Tipping Point" species that have lost >50% in 50 years, and "ducks, previously the bright spot, are now trending downward", so the waterfowl ×1.56 should decay after 2017 ([AOS](https://americanornithology.org/us-state-of-the-birds-2025-report/); [ABC Birds](https://abcbirds.org/news/2025-state-of-the-birds/); [Ducks Unlimited](https://www.ducks.org/newsroom/us-bird-populations-continue-alarming-decline-new-report-finds)). Third, **Morrison et al. 2021** reconstructed soundscapes at >200,000 sites and found "a pervasive loss of acoustic diversity and intensity". Losing skylark, nightingale or willow warbler thins complexity more than losing corvids or gulls, so the multiplier should thin songbird complexity before loud simple callers. Quote no percentage without the paper ([UEA](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/id/eprint/81972); [EBCC](https://www.ebcc.info/not-silent-yet-the-shifting-sounds-of-spring/); [BTO](https://www.bto.org/our-work/science/publications/papers/bird-population-declines-and-species-turnover-are); [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2021/11/211102125447.htm)). Fourth, **Bernie Krause's Lincoln Meadow** recordings (June 1988 vs June 1989, before and after "selective logging") are the audible precedent for a collapse within a single year ([TED](https://ideas.ted.com/listen-to-the-haunting-impact-that-humans-have-had-on-the-natural-world/); [MIT Press Reader](https://thereader.mitpress.mit.edu/everything-is-wrong-bernie-krauses-concept-of-biophony); [NPR](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/216100454)). **Before 1970 there is no systematic baseline.** A slider reaching back to 1500 needs an explicitly INFERRED ramp tied to documented collapses: passenger pigeon, bison, whooping crane, Eskimo curlew, and market hunting in 1870–1918. The marine equivalents are the pre-whaling 6–20× multiplier, cod at ~1% of peak, oyster reefs at −85%, and reefs 15 dB quieter.

## Archives and precedents: where the real voices live and who has already tried this

The **Macaulay Library** (Cornell) holds more than 1 million recordings, some from 1929. It is the main archive for lost-bird audio: the ivory-bill 1935 "crown jewel", Bachman's warbler, Kauaʻi ʻōʻō, kāmaʻo, poʻouli, bridled white-eye and the Atitlán grebe. Its pages blocked automated fetching, but its CDN served ML6050 for measurement ([The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/digital-sound-archives-can-bring-extinct-birds-briefly-back-to-life-176115); [EarthSky](https://earthsky.org/earth/extinct-bird-songs-woodpecker-warbler-kauai-oo/)). **xeno-canto** hosts the cryptic treehunter, Pernambuco pygmy-owl and (restricted) Spix's macaw. The **NFSA** holds the pipistrelle, **Ngā Taonga** the huia imitation (reuse enquiries: sound@ngataonga.org.nz), and **FrogID/Australian Museum** the McDonald frog calls. **Dryad** has the *A. zeteki* dataset, **UConn** the Magicicada sonograms, **Bowdoin** the heath hen film, and the **Wuhan IHB** the baiji. The Tierstimmenarchiv Berlin, British Library Sound Archive, FNJV (Unicamp) and DOSITS files were not verified. Reusing ML6050-style *measurement* rather than playback keeps the artwork within its "synthesize, never sample" rule. The huia and restricted-access files are the exception: pitch data there should not be extracted without permission.

Precedents range from scientific to lyrical. **Te Papa** commissioned five extinct NZ bird calls (Coubrough and Smith, 2005–06). The moa used emu and cassowary analogues, tracheal anatomy, a ceramic fish and a Nerf gun, and drew on Herries Beattie's interviews with kaumātua. The makers concluded "we will likely never know", and the article does not mention iwi consultation ([The Spinoff](https://thespinoff.co.nz/pop-culture/22-04-2025/how-a-nerf-gun-and-a-ceramic-fish-helped-bring-to-life-the-lost-call-of-the-moa); [Te Papa](https://collections.tepapa.govt.nz/topic/1444)). Other precedents are Sandia's *Parasaurolophus* call (1997) ([Sandia](https://newsreleases.sandia.gov/scientists-use-digital-paleontology-to-produce-voice-of-parasaurolophus-dinosaur/); [Lab News](https://www.sandia.gov/labnews/1997/12/19/dinosaur-story/)); Marguerite Humeau's vocal tracts for Lucy and the mammoth ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2011/07/23/138644143/helping-mammoths-roar-again); [Dezeen](https://www.dezeen.com/?p=226206)); and *Séance Vocibus Avium* (2008). Recorded-voice precedents include *Songs of Disappearance* (2021), 53 threatened Australian birds that reached #2 on ARIA with proceeds to BirdLife Australia ([The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/how-our-album-of-birdsong-recordings-rocketed-to-2-on-the-aria-charts-177070); [The Music Network](https://themusicnetwork.com/songs-of-disappearance-aria-top-five/)), and Julianne Warren's and David Hindley's huia works. Two listening-at-a-distance precedents fit "elsewhere" closely. Katie Paterson's *Vatnajökull (the sound of)* drew 3,200 calls from 47 countries in a week ([Katie Paterson](https://katiepaterson.org/?p=198); [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2007/07/29/12317438/artist-records-glaciers-sounds-from-the-deep)), and Marcus Coates's *Dawn Chorus* slowed birdsong for human singers ([Marcus Coates](https://marcuscoates.co.uk/projects/68-dawn-chorus); [Arts Council Collection](https://artscouncilcollection.org.uk/artwork/dawn-chorus)). The "lost birds" framework (126–144 species with no media for 10+ years, from ~42 million records) links archives to status directly ([ABC 2023](https://abcbirds.org/news/abc-results-2023-preventing-extinctions)). Butchart et al. 2006 estimated that conservation prevented 16 bird extinctions in 1994–2004 ([Cambridge PDF](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/6011F6BE0D5BF3A7A91DDF3F7DB2A8DA/S0030605306000950a.pdf/how-many-bird-extinctions-have-we-prevented.pdf); [BirdLife](https://datazone.birdlife.org/sowb/casestudy/over-ten-years-action-by-birdlife-partners-and-others-prevented-the-extinction-of-16-bird-species)). Lazarus precedents include the takahē (20 Nov 1948), cahow (Jan 1951), black-browed babbler (2020), dusky tetraka (2022–23) and Santa Marta sabrewing (2022) ([DOC](https://doc.govt.nz/our-work/takahe-recovery-programme/the-takahe-story); [Te Ara](https://teara.govt.nz/en/photograph/20091/takahe-rediscovered); [Birds Outside My Window](https://www.birdsoutsidemywindow.org/2010/09/07/rare-bird-rediscovering-the-cahow/); [babbler](https://searchforlostbirds.org/birds/1688); [BirdLife 2023](https://www.birdlife.org/news/2023/03/01/found-mysterious-songbird-rediscovered-in-madagascar-after-eluding-scientists-for-24-years/); [ABC Birds](https://abcbirds.org/?p=34392)). A refound species could sound faintly in the lost world only for its gap years: the takahē from ~1898 to 1948, then graduating to the living world, perhaps with a "returned" shimmer. Its tag also changes over time, because rediscovery often produced the first recording.

## Ethics and Indigenous data: consent before voice

Two frameworks apply. **Te Mana Raraunga** (Māori Data Sovereignty Network, founded 2015) sets out rangatiratanga, whakapapa, whanaungatanga, kotahitanga, manaakitanga and kaitiakitanga. It defines Māori data to include information "about or from Māori people, our language, culture, resources or environments", which covers the huia imitation and arguably all taonga species ([Te Mana Raraunga](https://www.temanararaunga.maori.nz/principles-of-maori-data-sovereignty); [TMR 2018 PDF](https://online.op.ac.nz/assets/LearningTeachingDevelopment/TMR+Maori+Data+Sovereignty+Principles+Oct+2018.pdf)). The **CARE Principles** (Collective benefit, Authority to control, Responsibility, Ethics) generalise this globally ([Carroll et al. 2020](https://account.datascience.codata.org/index.php/up-j-dsj/article/view/dsj-2020-043)). Neither Te Papa nor Bishop Museum has published guidance on voicing extinct species that the research could find. The recommendations for respectful practice follow.

**Huia and Ngā Taonga.** Treat Hāmana's whistles as human knowledge of the bird, not the bird. Credit "after Hēnare Hāmana, 1948/49, introduced by R. A. L. Batley". Contact Ngā Taonga, which notes online content "may not be available for supply". Seek guidance from Mōkai Pātea / Moawhango-area iwi and hapū connected to Hāmana, whose iwi affiliation the sources do not state, and from Ngāti Huia–linked iwi. Consider opening with silence or a karakia-respectful framing, and do not derive pitch data from the Ngā Taonga copy without permission. If consent is not obtained, the Tararua trace should be a **silent or non-voiced marker**. The same consultation applies to kōkako, whēkau, piopio and moa. The whakataukī "kua ngaro i te ngaro o te moa" signals how the moa is held ([Wikipedia: Moa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moa)).

**Hawaiian taxa.** Kia manu imitations (mamo lure call, moho cry), onomatopoeic names, featherwork birds (ʻōʻō, mamo, ʻiʻiwi, ʻōʻū) and ʻaumakua birds (ʻalalā, and others by family) are culturally owned knowledge. Consult Kanaka Maoli practitioners and Bishop Museum Cultural Collections. Record who was asked and on what conditions, and credit "voiced with the guidance of…" ([Bishop Museum](https://www.bishopmuseum.org/online-learning-center/wings/)).

**palawa kani and Aboriginal names.** *kaparunina* belongs to a revived language managed by the Tasmanian Aboriginal community. The researcher believes the Tasmanian Aboriginal Centre controls its use, but did not verify this. Seek permission before displaying it. Pair Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander names (Maizab Kaur Mukeis, yallara, oolacunta, moda) with community guidance. Indigenous names should otherwise come first or paired, with correct diacritics (ʻōʻō, kōkako, kāmaʻo).

**Location protection.** Blur possibly-extant, refound or recovering species to ~50–100 km or bioregion, following the four-year embargo on night parrot recordings. Never place ivory-bill traces at contested 2004–23 sites, because rendering the Project Principalis audio as ivory-bill would take a side in a live dispute.

**Labelling and reciprocity.** Label anything beyond evidence as "imagined" and name the analogue ("moa, inferred from cassowary and emu"). Link each voice to its archive and recordist, and consider directing listeners to conservation or cultural organisations on the *Songs of Disappearance* model. Avoid "resurrection" language. These sounds are memory and research, not de-extinction, which matters where firms market ivory-bill genetic engineering ([ABC News](https://abcnews.com/US/ivory-billed-woodpecker-extinct-genetic-engineering-company-aims/story?id=114352833)). Politically, keep "Liancourt Rocks" neutral, and note the Onondaga Nation's stewardship of Brood VII. The baiji and Japanese wolf voices carry religious meaning (the "Goddess of the Yangtze"; Ōkuchi-no-Makami shrines and the Obon requiem).

## What remains uncertain

The gaps below are shared across the whole catalogue and are worth closing before build. **Spectral data**: only ML6050 was measured. Kāmaʻo ML6027/6029, ʻōʻū ML5897, Bachman's ML10717, Atitlán ML943 and the poʻouli need the user's go-ahead to download for the same analysis. Measure the Macaulay files first, since they are the most valuable next step. **Archive IDs**: few Macaulay or xeno-canto numbers were verified, including the dusky seaside sparrow, poʻouli, Maui ʻākepa 1994–95 and the Jacobi/Boynton ʻōʻō cuts. **Frequencies**: missing for the pipistrelle, *Mystacina*, Hawaiian and Caribbean monk seals, the Japanese sea lion, bison bellows, locust wings, the golden toad chirp, Rabbs' treefrog and all model species for the Pacific. **Primary texts not read**: Schorger 1955, Tanner 1942, Gross 1928, Hindwood 1938/1940, Jenkins 1983, Kittlitz 1858, Buller, Steller's Latin, Leguat in full, Hume and Walters, Riley's locust reports, Lockwood 2004, Rivero, and Woinarski 2015. **Indigenous names and knowledge not found**: Beothuk/Mi'kmaq (great auk), Cherokee/Choctaw (parakeet, ivory-bill), Inuit (Eskimo curlew), Nahuatl (grackle), Taíno (monk seal, macaw), Kanak (New Caledonian birds), Moriori (Chathams), Marquesan (monarchs) and Māori-authored sources on whēkau and pouakai. **Status checks**: the IUCN pages were not fetched directly. The ʻōʻū 2024 verdict, poʻouli and treehunter reclassification, Lord Howe EPBC statuses and the ivory-bill's final USFWS action all need confirmation. **Unresearched areas**: Tonga, Pitcairn, Vanuatu, Palau/Yap/Chuuk, Caribbean hutias and bats, Latin American and Mascarene insects, and the species list of the 90 chytrid extinctions.

## Conclusion

The research changes what [ what can no longer be heard ] can honestly be. It cannot be a library of resurrected recordings, because only about two dozen of some three hundred candidates survive as audio of the animal itself. It can be a **score assembled from witnesses**: Craig's metronome marks, Perkins's weather-gated choruses, Hull's islanders humming "Pop Goes the Weasel", Leguat counting wing-whirls. Each is layered over real spectra where they exist and over living relatives where they do not. The provenance tag is therefore not metadata. It is the instrument's timbre control, running from literal (RECORDED) through remembered (DESCRIBED) and borrowed human (IMITATION) to restrained or silent (INFERRED). Several of the most truthful renderings are near-silences: the voiceless golden toad, a sea cow breathing every five minutes, Lyall's wren. Several of the strongest secret traces are *absences of an answer*: the ʻōʻō's unreturned duet, the lone boom on a lek, the pipistrelle's last pass.

Two design implications follow from the evidence rather than from taste. First, **density is a better-documented lever than voice**. Hornaday's thunder at 1–3 miles, Albert's 12.5 trillion locusts, 6–20× pre-whaling whale numbers and Rosenberg's biome multipliers let the lost world be demonstrably *fuller* even where individual voices are inferred. The move from full to thin is itself what the present has lost. Second, **the most culturally powerful material is gated by consent**, and the catalogue should be built so that a silent marker is a first-class outcome, not a failure. The huia, the kia manu lure calls and *kaparunina* are the clearest cases. A world that plays silence where permission was not given is more faithful to what can no longer be heard than one that fills every gap.
