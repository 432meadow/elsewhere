# Extinct and "not heard since" birds of North America: a sound catalogue

Scope: Canada, mainland USA, Mexico (including its Pacific islands) and the North Atlantic, including the great auk. Species or subspecies that went extinct, or were last reliably recorded, after about 1500. The emphasis is on sound (call types, pitch, rhythm, chorus behaviour, wing noise), plus range, timing and abundance, for synthesis in "elsewhere".

Provenance tiers used below:
- **RECORDED**: real audio of the taxon exists.
- **DESCRIBED**: only written accounts exist.
- **IMITATION**: a human imitation or calling device is documented.
- **INFERRED**: nothing beyond anatomy or living relatives.

Coordinates in the "map pin" lines are my own approximations from the named places. They are not taken from any source, so verify them before use.

## Overview: which lost North American birds have real sound, and how good is the rest?

### Takeaway
Only a handful of these birds have real recordings:
- ivory-billed woodpecker (1935)
- Bachman's warbler (1950s)
- dusky seaside sparrow (to 1980s)
- Socorro dove (still alive in captivity)

Two famous "last birds" survive only on silent film: the heath hen (1928 and 1931) and the imperial woodpecker (1956). The passenger pigeon has by far the best written acoustic description: Wallace Craig's 1911 notations, which give tempi and pitch. Almost everything else is DESCRIBED or INFERRED.

### Cited Findings
- The 1935 Cornell–American Museum expedition recorded sound, photos and motion pictures of a nesting pair of ivory-billed woodpeckers in the Singer Tract, Louisiana, in early April 1935. Arthur A. Allen and Peter Paul Kellogg are listed as contributors — [Macaulay Library ML6784](https://macaulaylibrary.org/asset/6784); [To the Best of Our Knowledge, "Recording America's Rarest Bird"](https://www.ttbook.org/node/54781)
- These are described as "the only universally accepted audio and motion picture recordings" of the species — [Wikipedia: Ivory-billed woodpecker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivory-billed_woodpecker)
- A 1968 Texas recording attributed to John Dennis is "contentious" — [Earbirding, "Sounds of Extinct Birds"](https://earbirding.com/blog/?p=316)
- Earbirding lists Macaulay/Borror Lab Bachman's warbler recordings from 1954 and 1959, and Macaulay dusky seaside sparrow recordings, as genuine — [Earbirding](https://earbirding.com/blog/?p=316)
- The 2008 *Séance Vocibus Avium* (The Wire broadcast) had avant-garde musicians "voice" 11 extinct birds, including the great auk, from written descriptions only. It is an artistic reconstruction, not evidence — [Earbirding](https://earbirding.com/blog/?p=316)
- The heath hen film is one reel of silent black-and-white 35 mm film (14:03 min), shot on Martha's Vineyard in 1928 and 1931 by Alfred O. Gross. It is held by Bowdoin College Special Collections and was digitized in 2017 — [Bowdoin ArchivesSpace](https://archivesspace.bowdoin.edu/repositories/2/archival_objects/48321); [Wikipedia: Heath hen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heath_hen)
- The only imperial woodpecker footage is a 16 mm colour film made in 1956 by William L. Rhein in Durango. It has no sound — [Lammertink et al. 2011, The Auk 128(4)](https://www.bioone.org/journals/the-auk/volume-128/issue-4/auk.2011.10271/Film-Documentation-of-the-Probably-Extinct-Imperial-Woodpecker-Campephilus-imperlalis/10.1525/auk.2011.10271.full); [Cornell Chronicle](https://news.cornell.edu/node/270868)
- On 17 Oct 2023, USFWS delisted 21 species as extinct, including Bachman's warbler. The ivory-billed woodpecker was pulled from that rule for a separate future decision — [USFWS final rule 2023-22377](https://www.fws.gov/policy/library/2023/2023-22377.html); [Federal Register public inspection PDF](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2023-22377.pdf)

### Inferences
- For synthesis, only the ivory-bill kent calls, Bachman's warbler song, dusky seaside sparrow song and Socorro dove coos can be modelled directly from real spectrograms. Everything else should be built from written descriptions plus the closest living relative, and labelled as such in the artwork.
- A fair rule for the "rare secret traces" is to reserve literal-sounding traces for RECORDED taxa, and give DESCRIBED or INFERRED taxa a more abstract, "remembered" timbre.

### Gaps
- Macaulay Library asset pages block automated fetching (bot check), so I could not verify catalogue metadata (duration, exact coordinates) beyond what search snippets gave. Check ML6784, ML10717 and the dusky seaside sparrow assets by hand.
- My web search budget ran out before I could check the Birds of the World species accounts (paywalled) or BirdLife data zone pages for each species.

## Passenger pigeon (*Ectopistes migratorius*)

### Takeaway
Tier: DESCRIBED plus IMITATION; no recording exists.

This was the loudest, most gregarious bird of the eastern forests. Its voice was harsh shrieks, chatter and clucks rather than coos, with a wing-noise roar at flock scale. Craig (1911) gives musical notations, tempi and one pitch reference: the "keck" at about B5, roughly 988 Hz.

### Cited Findings

**Range and habitat**
- Eastern deciduous forests from the Great Plains to the Atlantic, breeding mainly around the Great Lakes.
- Wintered from Arkansas and Tennessee south to the Gulf Coast and northern Florida.
- Source: [Wikipedia: Passenger pigeon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passenger_pigeon)

**Abundance** (all from [Wikipedia: Passenger pigeon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passenger_pigeon))
- Estimated 3–5 billion birds, possibly 25–40% of all North American land birds.
- Audubon (Kentucky, 1813) counted 163 flocks in 21 minutes over a 55-mile journey.
- A flock in Ontario in 1866 was reportedly 1.5 km wide and 500 km long.
- The largest nesting (Wisconsin, 1871) covered about 2,200 km², with an estimated 136 million birds.

**Audubon's sound descriptions**
Source: Audubon's *Ornithological Biography* vol. 1 account, quoted via [HackerNoon reprint "A Story from Audubon"](https://hackernoon.com/a-story-from-audubon).
- A "continued buzz of wings" that lulled the senses.
- A "noise like thunder" when the flock rushed into a compact mass.
- At the Green River, Kentucky roost, the approaching noise "reminded me of a hard gale at sea, passing through the rigging of a close-reefed vessel."
- "The uproar continued the whole night."
- Flights passed "for three days in succession."
- Birds arrived at the roost after sunset and left at sunrise.
- Wikipedia summarises the colony noise as "deafening" and audible for miles — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passenger_pigeon)

**Craig 1911 (primary source): observation base**
Source for this and the next three blocks: [Craig 1911, "The expressions of emotion in the pigeons. III. The Passenger Pigeon," Auk 28(4):408–427 — full text via Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/sim_auk_1911-10_28_4); [USF Digital Commons landing](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/auk/vol28/iss4/3); [BHL](https://biodiversitylibrary.org/part/87486)
- Craig's notes come from Professor C. O. Whitman's aviary, chiefly in 1903.
- He saw a wild bird only once, in Chicago in 1891.
- He says the voice was "more distinctive than that of any other species" in Whitman's worldwide pigeon collection.
- Birds were "Especially active and noisy in early morning."

**Craig 1911: call types**
- **Keck.** The first *k* is aspirated like German *ch*. It is "loud, sometimes very loud, harsh, and rather high-pitched (b above the treble staff) so far as it can be said to have any pitch at all."
  - Usually single, sometimes two or more with short pauses.
  - Often paired with one full wing-flap; a wingtip can strike the perch audibly.
  - Used to "overpower" a female or a rival.
- **Scolding / chattering / clucking.** The commonest call.
  - Loud and high in excitement, "very rapid" in fighting, "a soft, low-pitched adagio" in gentle mood.
  - The tone is "never musical."
  - Craig gives 20 notations with metronome marks from ♩=120 to 180. For example, No. 1 "cuck ck-uck cuck" at ♩=120 has a "chest-tone, much like the low clucking of a hen."
- **Vestigial coo, "keeho."** A "weak, somewhat musical" disyllable given after kecks.
  - One notation reads "keck kee-ho … choo": the first tone "hard like squeak of violin," the second "softer, a head-tone."
  - Craig's explanation: in so noisy a community the soft coo degenerated and the loud cry developed.
- **Nest-call** (notation No. 27, ♩=192).
  - "Timbre very much burred … A great mixture of high and low tones. Only moderately loud, and half musical."
  - Series of at least about 8 notes; long series fall "about a semitone."
  - Always ends with a keeho.
- **Female.** Comparatively quiet.
  - "krk krk krk" clucks.
  - "an almost toneless croaking sound, like the rolling of an r in one's throat," repeated 5–6 times.
- **Copulation.** The male "clucks in a soft toneless voice." One such observation was at 7:20 pm, dusk.

**Craig 1911: earlier descriptions he collected**
- Audubon: "kee-kee-kee-kee."
- Others: "Tete! Tete! Tete!", "chattering," "twittering," "chirping."
- Wm. Brewster: "a sound resembling the croaking of wood-frogs."

**Craig 1911: wing noise and alarm (Pokagon)**
Chief Simon Pokagon (Potawatomi), quoted by Craig from Mershon:
- The watch-bird's alarm is made by "beating its wings together in quick succession, sounding like the rolling beat of a snare drum."
- Each bird then repeats it "with a thundering sound, as the flock struggles to rise."

**IMITATION tier** (from Craig, quoting H. T. Blodgett via Mershon)
Hunters called flocks down in two ways:
- a throat imitation of the call ("liable to make the throat sore");
- a silk band stretched between two wood blocks, blown between lips and teeth, with pitch raised or lowered by biting pressure.

**Last records**
- Last confirmed wild specimen: shot at Laurel, Indiana, 1902 — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passenger_pigeon)
- **Martha**, the last individual, died 1 Sept 1914 at Cincinnati Zoo — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passenger_pigeon)

**Indigenous names** (all from [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passenger_pigeon))
- Potawatomi "O-me-me-wog"
- Seneca "jahgowa" ("big bread")
- Lenape "amimi"
- Ojibwe "omiimii"

### Inferences
- "b above the treble staff" is B5, about 988 Hz in modern tuning (my conversion). Treat it as the perceived pitch centre of a noisy, harsh keck, not as a pure fundamental.
- Synthesis sketch:
  - Keck: a broadband, harsh burst around 1 kHz.
  - Chatter: clucks at 2–3 per second, from Craig's 120–180 quarter-note tempi with a note per beat or half-beat.
  - Keeho: two tones, a hard high squeak then a softer lower head-tone.
  - Nest-call: burred pulse series of 8 or more notes, falling a semitone.
  - Flock scale: continuous wing "buzz" and "gale in rigging" roar, with thunderous take-off bursts; roost uproar all night, dusk to dawn.
- The closest living relatives (*Patagioenas*, e.g. band-tailed pigeon) coo in a way the passenger pigeon did not. Use them for wing-clap and flight noise, not for voice.
- Pin: Martha at Cincinnati Zoo, about 39.145 N, 84.508 W. Last wild specimen at Laurel, Indiana, about 39.50 N, 85.19 W.

### Gaps
- Schorger 1955 (*The Passenger Pigeon*) was not accessed directly; its compiled roost-noise accounts and exact last-wild-record circumstances (e.g. the 1900 Sargents, Ohio bird) should be checked.
- Pokagon's own 1895 essay was not accessed.
- I found no measured frequency data, since no recording exists.

## Carolina parakeet (*Conuropsis carolinensis*)

### Takeaway
Tier: DESCRIBED; no recording.

This was the only parrot native to the eastern US. It was noisy in flocks, with shrill rising "qui" cries in flight and low chatter while feeding. It roosted communally in hollow sycamores and cypresses.

### Cited Findings
- **Range:** from southern New York and Wisconsin to Kentucky, Tennessee and the Gulf.
- **Subspecies:** eastern *carolinensis* and Louisiana *ludovicianus*, separated by the Appalachians.
- **Habitat:** old-growth riverine forest and swamps, with cypress and sycamore cavities.
- **Numbers:** "huge, noisy flocks of as many as 300 birds"; population estimates range from tens of thousands to a few million.
- **End:** last wild specimen Florida, 1904; declared extinct 1939.
- **Relatives:** closest living ones are the nanday parakeet, sun conure and golden-capped parakeet.
- **Names:** Seminole "puzzi la née" ("head of yellow"); Chickasaw "kelinky."
- Source for all of the above: [Wikipedia: Carolina parakeet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carolina_parakeet)
- **Voice (Myron Swenk's description):** a "shrill series of rapidly uttered, discordant cries, given incessantly when the birds were in flight, resembling qui-qui, qui, qui, qui, qui-i-i-i, with a rising inflection on each i and the last cry drawn out." Also loud and raucous in flight, with low continuous chatter while feeding — [NCpedia](https://www.ncpedia.org/carolina-parakeet); [Feathered Friends](https://featheredfriends.substack.com/p/carolina-parakeet-portrait-of-a-vanished)
- **Butler 1892 (Auk 9:49–56)** — [Auk Jan 1892 full text via Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/sim_auk_1892-01_9_1)
  - Flocks of "thirty to fifty" on a Vermilion County, Indiana farm, 1834–1844.
  - In winter 1842, a hollow sycamore about 4 ft in diameter cut at Lodi, Indiana held "hundreds of Parrakeets in a quiescent or semi-torpid condition."
  - Audubon (1831) already noted rapid decline near Cincinnati.
- **Incas**, the last known individual, died 21 Feb 1918 at Cincinnati Zoo. His mate Lady Jane died in summer 1917. They are reported as a pair for 32 years, a figure from a popular source — [Feathered Friends](https://featheredfriends.substack.com/p/carolina-parakeet-portrait-of-a-vanished)

### Inferences
- Model on *Aratinga* (sun conure or nanday): harsh, rising, repeated screeches in flight, and a low chatter bed at feeding or roost sites.
- Rhythm: rapid repeated "qui" notes, each rising, with a drawn-out final note.
- The flight-call chorus should scale with flocks of up to 300. Winter roosts are quiet cavity clusters.
- Pin: Incas at Cincinnati Zoo, about 39.145 N, 84.508 W (same zoo as Martha).

### Gaps
- No frequency data. Sun conure and nanday call spectra were not researched here.
- The exact place of the 1904 Florida specimen, and later sight claims (e.g. Santee River 1930s), were not verified.
- The Cherokee name was not found.

## Heath hen (*Tympanuchus cupido cupido*)

### Takeaway
Tier: DESCRIBED, plus silent film; no known audio.

This was the eastern form of the greater prairie-chicken. It boomed on spring leks, and its last lek was on Martha's Vineyard. The booming of greater prairie-chickens (peak about 296–297 Hz) is the obvious model.

### Cited Findings
- **Range and habitat:** scrubby heathland barrens from southernmost New Hampshire to northern Virginia, including Long Island and the Pennsylvania barrens — [Wikipedia: Heath hen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heath_hen)
- **Colonial abundance:** "extremely common"; became "poor man's food" — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heath_hen)
- **Martha's Vineyard decline** — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heath_hen)
  - 1908: about 100 birds, when a reserve was set up.
  - 1916: about 2,000, then a fire killed about 80%, leaving about 150.
  - 1927: 13 birds (11 males, 2 females).
  - December 1928: a single male.
- **Booming Ben** was last seen 11 March 1932 "on his traditional lekking ground between West Tisbury and today's Martha's Vineyard Airport" — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heath_hen); [Wikipedia: Booming Ben](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Booming_Ben)
- **Film:** Gross filmed three males in 1928. In 1931, with Thornton Burgess, he trapped, banded, filmed and released the last bird. The film is silent — [Bowdoin ArchivesSpace](https://archivesspace.bowdoin.edu/repositories/2/archival_objects/48321)
- **Model species acoustics:** greater prairie-chicken boom average peak frequency is 296 Hz; the boom chorus peaks at 297 ± 13 Hz. Chorus signal-to-noise falls from 15.7 dB at 50 m to 2.6 dB at 800 m. Booms were 3% shorter near wind farms — [Whalen et al., Condor 120(1)](https://bioone.org/journals/the-condor/volume-120/issue-1/CONDOR-17-56.1/Male-Greater-Prairie-Chickens-adjust-their-vocalizations-in-the-presence/10.1650/CONDOR-17-56.1.full); [Tethys summary](https://tethys.pnnl.gov/publications/wind-turbine-noise-limits-propagation-greater-prairiechicken-boom-chorus-does-it)

### Inferences
- Synthesis: a low hooting three-part boom centred near 300 Hz, from a spring dawn lek chorus that thins over the years.
  - Historical: a dense chorus across coastal barrens.
  - 1929–1932: a single male booming alone. This is a powerful "last voice" trace.
- Pin: Booming Ben's lek, West Tisbury / Martha's Vineyard Airport area, about 41.39 N, 70.61 W.

### Gaps
- No period description of heath hen booming was accessed (e.g. Gross 1928 monograph, Brewster).
- Whether heath hen booms differed from prairie-chicken booms is unknown.
- Boom duration for the living species was not captured here. It is commonly cited as about 2 s, but I did not verify that.

## Great auk (*Pinguinus impennis*)

### Takeaway
Tier: DESCRIBED (very thin); no recording.

This was a flightless North Atlantic alcid with dense colonies in May–June. Its voice is recorded only as "low croaking and a hoarse scream," plus a captive's anxious gurgling. Model on the razorbill, its closest relative, but louder and deeper.

### Cited Findings
All from [Wikipedia: Great auk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_auk) unless noted.
- **Voice:** "low croaking and a hoarse scream." A captive bird made "a gurgling noise when anxious." Its vocalizations were probably like a razorbill's but "louder and deeper."
- **Breeding sites:** Papa Westray (Orkney), St Kilda, Grimsey, Eldey, Geirfuglasker (Iceland), Funk Island (Newfoundland), Bird Rocks (Gulf of St Lawrence).
- **Winter range:** Canada and Greenland to Massachusetts Bay; occasionally Florida and northern Spain.
- **Timing and numbers:** pairing from May, eggs late May to early June. Colonies held about one bird per square metre. The population reached "in the millions."
- **Last birds:**
  - Eldey, 3 June 1844: the last confirmed pair was killed by Jón Brandsson and Sigurður Ísleifsson ("It walked like a man…").
  - St Kilda, July 1840: a bird was captured, kept three days, then killed as a "witch" blamed for a storm.
  - A possible sighting on the Grand Banks in 1852.
- **Cultural:** Maritime Archaic burials at Port au Choix, Newfoundland (about 2000 BC) held "more than 200 great auk beaks." Nearly half the bird bones at the site were great auk.
- **Closest living relative:** the razorbill.

### Inferences
- Synthesis: low guttural croaks and hoarse screams from a dense colony at summer dawn, over surf, based on razorbill and murre colony textures pitched down to match a much larger body.
- No wing noise in flight: it was flightless. Instead, splashes and waddling on rock.
- Pins (approximate):
  - Eldey: 63.74 N, 22.96 W
  - Funk Island: 49.76 N, 53.18 W
  - St Kilda (Hirta): 57.81 N, 8.58 W
  - Papa Westray: 59.35 N, 2.90 W

### Gaps
- Beothuk and Mi'kmaq names and relationships to the great auk were not found.
- Fuller's *The Great Auk* (1999) was not accessed.
- Primary sources for the voice description (e.g. the St Kilda account by Martin Martin, or the captive bird's observer) were not traced.

## Labrador duck (*Camptorhynchus labradorius*)

### Takeaway
Tier: INFERRED. No description of its voice is known.

It was a sea duck wintering on sandy bays from New England to New Jersey, and was already rare when first documented. Steller's eider, its closest relative, is the best acoustic model.

### Cited Findings
All from [Wikipedia: Labrador duck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labrador_duck).
- **Voice:** no information on voice.
- **Breeding:** thought to be Labrador and northern Quebec (largely unknown).
- **Winter:** coasts of New Jersey and New England, on "southern sandy coasts, sheltered bays, harbors, and inlets."
- **Bill:** a soft, flattened tip with many lamellae, suited to probing sediment.
- **Abundance:** "already a rare duck before European settlers arrived."
- **Last records:** a specimen shot on Long Island in autumn 1875; last reported sighting at Elmira, NY in 1878.
- **Closest relative:** Steller's eider, per mitogenomics.
- **Other names:** pied duck, skunk duck, sand shoal duck.

### Inferences
- Treat as near-silent in winter: soft grunts and croons modelled on Steller's eider, plus wing whistle and water noise. Label it clearly as INFERRED.
- Pin: Long Island (approx. 40.8 N, 73.0 W); Elmira (42.09 N, 76.81 W, a doubtful record).

### Gaps
- There are no acoustic accounts at all.
- Whether the 1878 Elmira record is reliable is disputed in the literature but was not verified here.

## Ivory-billed woodpecker (*Campephilus principalis*)

### Takeaway
Tier: RECORDED, 1935. Status is contested: "possibly extinct."

Its sound is a nasal, tin-horn "kent" repeated in series, plus a double-rap drum. The 1935 Singer Tract recordings give measured harmonics: a fundamental of about 550–630 Hz with harmonics spaced about 550–700 Hz.

### Cited Findings

**Voice and recordings**
- "Kent" or "hant" calls, "likened to a toy trumpet," frequently repeated in series, plus a "double rap" — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivory-billed_woodpecker)
- ML6784 was recorded 9 and 14 April 1935 by the Cornell team in Louisiana. It has almost constant "kent" or "kint" calls with the quality of a somewhat nasal horn — [Macaulay ML6784](https://macaulaylibrary.org/asset/6784)
- **Acoustic measurements**, as summarised in the search snippet; I could not open the underlying pages:
  - Known kent calls have frequency peaks spaced about 650–700 Hz, starting at about 630 Hz.
  - Louder Singer Tract notes have a base of about 550 Hz with about 550 Hz harmonic spacing, and at least 9 peaks.
  - Sources: [BirdForum thread on Hill et al. acoustic evidence](https://www.birdforum.net/threads/ivory-billed-woodpecker-formerly-updates.33968/post-693770); [PMC figure, Ecology & Evolution 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10194015/figure/ece310017-fig-0001)

**Population and last records**
- Tanner (1937–39) estimated 22–24 birds in the US, 6–8 of them in the Singer Tract — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivory-billed_woodpecker)
- Last universally accepted US sighting: April 1944, Don Eckelberry, a female in the Singer Tract — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivory-billed_woodpecker)
- The Cuban subspecies was last documented in 1987 in eastern Cuba — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivory-billed_woodpecker)
- Evidence since is disputed:
  - Arkansas video and audio, 2004–05.
  - Project Principalis trail-camera and drone footage from Louisiana, 2023.
  - Critics suspect misidentification.
  - Source: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivory-billed_woodpecker)
- A 1968 Texas recording (John Dennis) is contentious — [Earbirding](https://earbirding.com/blog/?p=316)

**Status**
- IUCN: Critically Endangered (Possibly Extinct).
- USFWS proposed delisting in 2021 but removed the species from the final 2023 rule. According to Wikipedia, there was still no final decision as of Sept 2026.
- Sources: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivory-billed_woodpecker); [USFWS 2023 rule](https://www.fws.gov/policy/library/2023/2023-22377.html)

**Cultural**
- Indigenous peoples traded ivory-bill bills far beyond the bird's range; bills turn up in burials 300+ miles away.
- Catesby reported a bill was worth "two or three deerskins."
- Source: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivory-billed_woodpecker)

### Inferences
- Synthesis:
  - Kent: a nasal harmonic stack on about 550–630 Hz, repeated in irregular series.
  - Double knock: two loud raps about 0.1 s apart. The spacing is typical of *Campephilus*; my estimate, not sourced.
  - Setting: mature bottomland hardwood swamp, morning.
- This is the one extinct North American bird where a "trace" can be modelled closely on real audio.
- Pin: Singer Tract, now roughly the Tensas River NWR area, Madison Parish, Louisiana (about 32.25 N, 91.40 W, approximate).

### Gaps
- Tanner 1942 monograph text was not accessed.
- The exact duration of each kent and the tempo of series were not captured.
- Choctaw and Cherokee names were not found.

## Bachman's warbler (*Vermivora bachmanii*)

### Takeaway
Tier: RECORDED, 1950s (2–4 recordings). Status: extinct (USFWS, Oct 2023).

Its song was a fast buzzy trill of "zeeps," easily mistaken for a northern parula. It bred in southeastern canebrake swamps, arrived very early (February to March) and wintered in Cuba.

### Cited Findings

**Song**
- "A rapid series of six to twenty-five buzz notes, sometimes ending in a sharp, slurred zip note"; like a northern parula but more monotone — [Wikipedia: Bachman's warbler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachman%27s_warbler)
- "A fast series of buzzy 'zeeps' usually ending with a short, downward whistled note given by both sexes" — [USFWS final rule 2023](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2023-22377.pdf)

**Recordings (sources conflict on the number)**
- USFWS says "only four recordings exist, all from the 1950s … all four in Cornell Lab of Ornithology, Macaulay Library" — [USFWS 2023](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2023-22377.pdf)
- Other sources say there are only two extant recordings, 1954 (Virginia) and 1959 (South Carolina), documenting two different songs — [search summary citing Carolina Bird Club / Macaulay](https://macaulaylibrary.org/audio/10717)
- ML10717: G. Stuart Keith, 15 May 1959, near Charleston, SC — [Macaulay ML10717](https://macaulaylibrary.org/audio/10717)

**History and range** (all from [USFWS 2023](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2023-22377.pdf))
- Named in 1833 from a bird in a swamp near Charleston, SC.
- Bred in low, wet forest with permanent water; nests low in shrubs from late March to June.
- Most of the population wintered in Cuba and the Isle of Pines.
- Found in "some numbers" 1890–1920, including several singing males in Missouri and Arkansas in 1897, and migrant flocks on the lower Suwannee River.
- Last confirmed nest: 1937.
- Unconfirmed later reports:
  - Melbourne, FL, 1977.
  - Tensas NWR, LA, 1989: six possible birds.
  - Congaree NP, SC, 2000–01: traced to parulas.
  - Cuba: two Zapata Swamp sightings in the 1960s; a single bird reported in 1981.
  - Claims from 1988 onwards are rejected.

**Migration timing:** spring from late February to early April; fall in July–August, mainly through Florida — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachman%27s_warbler)

**Status:** delisted as extinct by USFWS. The rule was published 17 Oct 2023 and effective 30 days later — [USFWS 2023](https://www.fws.gov/policy/library/2023/2023-22377.html); [news coverage](https://www.wjhg.com/2023/10/17/songbird-once-found-southeast-us-among-21-species-delisted-endangered-species-act/)

### Inferences
- Synthesis: a buzzy, flat-pitched trill of 6–25 notes, sometimes with a final downslurred zip. The frequency band is likely high (about 4–8 kHz, as in parula-like *Vermivora* songs), but that is my estimate; measure it from ML10717.
- Season: February to June in canebrake swamps of the coastal plain.
- Pin: I'On Swamp / Charleston area, SC (about 33.0 N, 79.7 W, approximate).

### Gaps
- The identities of the 1954 Virginia recordist and the other two Macaulay cuts are not confirmed.
- The last "accepted" US record is given loosely as the 1960s (I'On Swamp, 1962 is often cited). The 2023 rule does not state a single final confirmed date in the parts I read.
- IUCN's current category (CR(PE) or EX) was not verified. My search budget was exhausted.

## Eskimo curlew (*Numenius borealis*)

### Takeaway
Tier: DESCRIBED; no recording. Status: possibly extinct (IUCN CR).

It was a small curlew on a 7,000-mile loop migration. Its voice is described as soft, fine whistles and a tern-like squeak, finer than a Hudsonian curlew's. It arrived in New England in late August and September on storm winds, in flocks of dozens.

### Cited Findings

**Voice**
- Mackay (Nantucket, 1892):
  - "They are said to make a whistle somewhat similar to the Hudsonian Curlew's … only very much finer in tone."
  - "The only note I have ever heard them make is a kind of squeak, very much like one of the cries of Wilson's Tern … only finer in tone."
  - Source: [Mackay 1892, "Habits of the Eskimo Curlew in New England," Auk 9:16–21, full text via Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/sim_auk_1892-01_9_1)
- Wikipedia calls the call "poorly understood" but including "clear whistling sounds" — [Wikipedia: Eskimo curlew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eskimo_curlew)
- Later summaries describe soft, clear, whistled notes, higher and weaker than other curlews'. One observer likened them to an American golden-plover or eastern bluebird — [search summary incl. Nature Conservancy of Canada](https://natureconservancy.ca/en/blog/archive/obituary-of-a-curlew.html); [Lost Birds / Search for Lost Birds](https://searchforlostbirds.org/birds/1620)

**Flocks and timing (Mackay 1892)**
- Flocks of 50–70 landed on Nantucket and Cape Cod from late August to mid-September, often after rain and southeast or northeast storms (e.g. "1872, Aug. 29 … Saw one flock of fifty").
- The birds were hard to see in withered grass once landed.
- Mackay calls the migration "a journey of seven thousand miles" from Arctic breeding grounds to Patagonia.

**Range and abundance**
- Bred on the tundra of the Yukon and NWT.
- Fall route east along the Arctic coast, then over the Atlantic to South America.
- "As many as 2 million birds per year were killed near the end of the nineteenth century."
- Other names: "doughbird," "prairie pigeon."
- Source: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eskimo_curlew)
- In fall they fattened on crowberries on the Labrador coast — [search summary](https://www.ontario.ca/page/eskimo-curlew)

**Last records**
- Photographed on Galveston Island, TX in 1962; shot on Barbados, 4 Sept 1963. Later reports are unconfirmed, with the most recent "reliable" sighting in 1981 — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eskimo_curlew); [BirdWatching](https://www.birdwatchingdaily.com/?p=2385)

### Inferences
- Synthesis: high, fine, soft whistles and tern-like squeaks from compact flocks of about 50.
  - Fall (August–September): Labrador and New England coastal plains and moors.
  - Spring: Great Plains.
- Models: whimbrel and Hudsonian curlew calls pitched higher and softer, or American golden-plover (its historical flocking companion).
- Pins: Galveston Island (about 29.25 N, 94.87 W); Barbados (about 13.1 N, 59.6 W, outside North America); Nantucket (about 41.28 N, 70.10 W).

### Gaps
- The "bee-bee" rendering sometimes attributed to historical accounts was not found in Mackay's text. Its source is unverified.
- Hearne's and other Arctic breeding-ground voice accounts were not accessed.
- The Inuit/Inuvialuit name was not found.

## Dusky seaside sparrow (*Ammospiza maritima nigrescens*), subspecies

### Takeaway
Tier: RECORDED (Macaulay Library). Status: subspecies extinct, 1987 (declared December 1990).

It was a sedentary salt-marsh sparrow of Merritt Island and the St Johns River marshes, Florida. The last bird, **Orange Band**, died 17 June 1987.

### Cited Findings
- **Range:** non-migratory; Merritt Island and the St Johns River marshes — [Wikipedia: Dusky seaside sparrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dusky_seaside_sparrow)
- **Causes of loss:** DDT mosquito spraying (1940s–60s), mosquito impoundments after 1956, and highway and industrial drainage — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dusky_seaside_sparrow)
- **Last birds:** by 1979 only six remained, all males; a female was last seen in 1975 — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dusky_seaside_sparrow)
- **Orange Band** died 17 June 1987 on Discovery Island, Walt Disney World. Tissue was cryopreserved — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dusky_seaside_sparrow); [USFWS species page](https://www.fws.gov/species/dusky-seaside-sparrow-ammodramus-maritimus-nigrescens)
- **Recordings:** the Macaulay Library holds genuine dusky seaside sparrow recordings — [Earbirding](https://earbirding.com/blog/?p=316)
- **Living model:** seaside sparrow song audio is on All About Birds — [All About Birds](https://www.allaboutbirds.org/guide/Seaside_Sparrow/sounds)

### Inferences
- Synthesis: seaside-sparrow-type buzzy song (a few clicks, then a buzz) over *Spartina bakeri* marsh from spring through summer, at dawn. Macaulay audio lets it be modelled closely.
- Pins:
  - Discovery Island, Bay Lake (about 28.41 N, 81.57 W): where the last bird died, in captivity.
  - St Johns marshes near Titusville (about 28.5 N, 80.9 W): the last wild range.

### Gaps
- Recordists, dates and ML catalogue numbers were not confirmed (Macaulay pages blocked automated fetching).
- Song structure and frequency of the subspecies were not measured here.

## Imperial woodpecker (*Campephilus imperialis*), Mexico

### Takeaway
Tier: DESCRIBED (voice) plus silent film (1956). Status: CR (possibly extinct).

It was the largest woodpecker in the world, found in the high pine-oak forests of the Sierra Madre Occidental. Its voice is described as toy-trumpet-like, like its ivory-billed cousin.

### Cited Findings
- **Size and status:** 56–60 cm. IUCN Critically Endangered, possibly extinct — [Wikipedia: Imperial woodpecker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_woodpecker)
- **Range and habitat** — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_woodpecker)
  - Sonora and Chihuahua south to Jalisco and Michoacán, at 1,920–3,050 m.
  - Fed on larvae under bark of dead pines, in small groups.
  - About 25 km² of mature forest per pair.
- **Decline:** foresters reportedly gave locals poison to smear on trees — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_woodpecker)
- **Voice and name:** called "cumecócari" by the Tarahumara (Rarámuri); voice reportedly "toy trumpet-like" — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_woodpecker)
- **The 1956 film** — [Lammertink et al. 2011, Auk](https://www.bioone.org/journals/the-auk/volume-128/issue-4/auk.2011.10271/Film-Documentation-of-the-Probably-Extinct-Imperial-Woodpecker-Campephilus-imperlalis/10.1525/auk.2011.10271.full); [Cornell Chronicle](https://news.cornell.edu/node/270868)
  - Shot by dentist William Rhein, hand-held from the back of a mule, in Durango.
  - 2,041 frames showing one female.
  - The analysis found slow climbing strides and a "fast wingflap rate compared with other woodpeckers."

### Inferences
- Synthesis: kent-like trumpet notes modelled on the ivory-bill 1935 spectra but lower, to scale with the larger body; my inference. Plus *Campephilus* double knocks.
- Wing noise: rapid flaps, which could drive a flight-sound model.
- Setting: high pine-oak forest, small family groups.
- Pin: Durango, Sierra Madre Occidental (very approximate: 24 N, 105.5 W). Lammertink located the exact filming site, but I did not extract it.

### Gaps
- The precise Rhein 1956 location, and Nelson's and Lumholtz's original voice descriptions, were not accessed.
- Tepehuan and other Indigenous names were not found.
- Local interview-based reports (Lammertink et al. 1996) of voice and last sightings in the 1990s were not reviewed.

## Guadalupe caracara (*Caracara lutosa*), Mexico

### Takeaway
Tier: INFERRED (voice). Status: extinct, about 1900–1906.

This raptor was deliberately exterminated by goatherds on Guadalupe Island.

### Cited Findings
All from [Wikipedia: Guadalupe caracara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guadalupe_caracara).
- **Timeline:**
  - 1876: common across the island.
  - 1897: one bird seen.
  - 1900: Rollo Beck found 11 and collected 9.
  - 1903–1906: last unconfirmed sightings; certainly gone by 1906.
- **Cause:** goatherds shot and poisoned the birds as supposed goat-kid predators; the predation was "much exaggerated."
- About 35 specimens survive.
- **Relative:** crested caracara, which it resembled in plumage.

### Inferences
- Model on the crested caracara: dry rattles and head-thrown-back cackles, a low-density island soundscape of rocky slopes and surf.
- Pin: Guadalupe Island (about 29.05 N, 118.28 W).

### Gaps
- No written voice description was found. Palmer's (1875) and Beck's field notes were not accessed.

## Guadalupe storm petrel (*Hydrobates macrodactylus*), Mexico

### Takeaway
Tier: DESCRIBED. Status: CR (possibly extinct).

It was a nocturnal burrow-nesting petrel under the pine-oak forest of Guadalupe Island, bred in winter and spring, and was last recorded breeding in 1912.

### Cited Findings
All from [Wikipedia: Guadalupe storm petrel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guadalupe_storm_petrel).
- Nearly identical to Leach's storm petrel.
- Nested in burrows under pine-oak cloud forest on Mount Augusta, with young fed only at night.
- Bred in winter to spring, between the seasons of Ainley's (winter) and Townsend's (summer) storm petrels.
- **Call:** rendered as "here's a letter, here's a letter" with interjections of "For you, for you."
- **Decline:** "abundant" in 1906; last breeding record 1912; cats and goats blamed; June 2000 surveys found nothing.

### Inferences
- Synthesis: nocturnal burrow and flight chatter modelled on Leach's storm petrel (purring burrow trills and rhythmic flight calls), with the rhythm of the "here's-a-letter" phrase. Winter-spring nights only.
- Pin: north end of Guadalupe Island (about 29.1 N, 118.3 W).

### Gaps
- The original author of the "here's a letter" rendering (likely A. W. Anthony, 1890s) was not verified.

## Slender-billed grackle (*Quiscalus palustris*), Mexico

### Takeaway
Tier: INFERRED (voice). Status: extinct (IUCN; last recorded 1910).

It lived in marshes of the upper Río Lerma and the Valley of Mexico, and was ritually hunted by the Aztecs.

### Cited Findings
All from [Wikipedia: Slender-billed grackle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slender-billed_grackle).
- **Range and habitat:** Río Lerma and the marshes of the Valley of Mexico and Toluca Valley; nested in emergent aquatic vegetation.
- **Status:** no records after 1910.
- **Relatives:** close to the western great-tailed grackle clade, having diverged about 1.2 million years ago.
- **Cultural:** the Aztecs hunted it for the Izcalli fire-god ceremony, using its black tail feathers.

### Inferences
- Model on the great-tailed grackle (whistles, rattles, "clack" notes) in a reed-marsh colony setting.
- Pin: Lerma marshes, Estado de México (about 19.28 N, 99.51 W).

### Gaps
- No voice description and no Nahuatl name were found. Haemig's papers on Aztec grackle ethno-ornithology were not accessed.

## Socorro dove (*Zenaida graysoni*), Mexico

### Takeaway
Tier: RECORDED is possible (captive birds exist). Status: extinct in the wild since 1972.

It was endemic to Socorro Island in the Revillagigedo group. About 200 survive in captivity, so real sound is available.

### Cited Findings
- Endemic to Socorro Island; last wild sighting 1972; about 200 in captivity. Causes were overhunting, sheep grazing and cats. Reintroduction is planned — [Wikipedia: Socorro dove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socorro_dove); [Louisville Zoo](https://louisvillezoo.org/animalsandplants/dove-socorro/)

### Inferences
- Coos can be modelled from captive recordings (a gap to confirm). This is a rare case of "extinct in the wild" where the voice still exists.
- Pin: Socorro Island (about 18.78 N, 110.95 W).

### Gaps
- Specific recordings (xeno-canto or Macaulay) were not identified.

## Island subspecies of California and Baja California (wrens, song sparrow, and others)

### Takeaway
Tier: INFERRED from living conspecifics.

Goats, sheep and cats removed several island subspecies. Their songs can be modelled on mainland Bewick's wrens and song sparrows, though island dialects are lost.

### Cited Findings
- **San Clemente Bewick's wren** (*Thryomanes bewickii leucophrys*): San Clemente Island, CA; last sighting 1941; habitat destroyed by feral goats — [Wikipedia: San Clemente wren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Clemente_wren)
- **Guadalupe Bewick's wren** (*T. b. brevicauda*): last collected (3 specimens) by Anthony and Streator in May 1892; seen but "nearly extinct" on 22 March 1897; lost to goats and cats — [recentlyextinctspecies.com](https://recentlyextinctspecies.com/149-passeriformes/5731-thryomanes-bewickii-leucophrys); [Wikipedia: Bewick's wren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bewick%27s_wren)
- **Santa Barbara song sparrow** (*Melospiza melodia graminea*): "Extirpated on Santa Barbara (and possibly San Clemente) by feral cats, c. 1967–1970" — [Wikipedia: Song sparrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Song_sparrow)

### Inferences
- Model on the living subspecies: Bewick's wren trilled songs; song sparrow "repeated notes, quickly passing isolated notes, and trills" — [Wikipedia: Song sparrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Song_sparrow)
- Pins: San Clemente Island (about 32.9 N, 118.5 W); Santa Barbara Island (about 33.48 N, 119.03 W); Guadalupe Island (about 29.05 N, 118.28 W).

### Gaps
- Conflicting accounts of the Santa Barbara song sparrow were not reconciled: the brief's "1959 fire" versus Wikipedia's "cats, c. 1967–1970". The 1983 USFWS delisting was not confirmed; the Wikipedia page for the subspecies returned 404.
- Other Guadalupe Island losses (Guadalupe flicker *Colaptes auratus rufipileus*, Guadalupe spotted towhee *Pipilo maculatus consobrinus*) were not researched. My search budget was exhausted.
- **Townsend's bunting** (*Emberiza townsendi*, a single 1833 Pennsylvania specimen) was not researched. It is generally regarded as a doubtful or aberrant specimen rather than a valid lost species, but I did not verify that here. Treat it as excluded or uncertain.
- **Texas Henslow's sparrow** (*Centronyx henslowii houstonensis*) was not researched.

### Cited Findings

### Inferences
- If included, model on related *Toxostoma* thrashers (long, varied, repetitive songs), and flag it as "possibly extant."

### Gaps
- Post-2004 records and current IUCN status were not verified.
- No Cozumel thrasher recordings were identified.
