# Extinct, extinct-in-the-wild and "not heard since" species of Hawaiʻi and the tropical Pacific: what is known of their sounds

Status of these notes: PARTIAL. What is covered:
- Sections 1–7: the Kauaʻi species in depth, primary-source (Perkins 1903) sound descriptions for most Hawaiian forest birds, and the USFWS 2023 delistings.
- Section 8: non-Kauaʻi Hawaiʻi, Laysan and cultural context.
- Section 9: Micronesia, Bonin/Ogasawara and the Ryukyus.

The Lord Howe / Norfolk / Christmas Island / Polynesia / Melanesia pass had not returned when this file was written, so those regions are still missing.

The session's web-search budget ran out partway through. Later findings rest on direct page fetches (mostly Wikipedia), so verify them against the primary sources those pages cite. Lat/lon values marked "approx." are the researchers' own geocodes of the named place, not sourced coordinates.

Provenance tiers used: RECORDED (archival audio exists) / DESCRIBED (written description by a named observer) / IMITATION (onomatopoeic name, human imitation) / INFERRED (from relatives only).

## 1. Which species are officially extinct, and when/where were they last recorded?

### Takeaway
The USFWS final rule of 17 Oct 2023 (88 FR 71644; effective 16 Nov 2023) delisted 21 species as extinct. Ten are Pacific: 8 Hawaiian birds, Guam's bridled white-eye and the little Mariana fruit bat. Last records run from 1899 (Kauaʻi nukupuʻu) to 2004 (poʻouli).

### Cited Findings
- Final rule: Document 2023-22377, 88 FR 71644, published 17 Oct 2023, effective 16 Nov 2023. The 2021 proposal listed 23 species; 21 were delisted. — [USFWS action page](https://www.fws.gov/species-publication-action/removal-21-species-list-endangered-and-threatened-wildlife); [GovInfo full text](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-10-17/html/2023-22377.htm)
- The Pacific species delisted: Kauaʻi ʻakialoa, Kauaʻi nukupuʻu, Kauaʻi ʻōʻō, large Kauaʻi thrush (kāmaʻo), Maui ʻākepa, Maui nukupuʻu, Molokaʻi creeper (kākāwahie), poʻouli, bridled white-eye, little Mariana fruit bat. — [GovInfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-10-17/html/2023-22377.htm)
- Survey effort on Kauaʻi cited for all Kauaʻi species: "intensive survey efforts by wildlife biologists from 1968 to 1973, and in 1981, 1989, 1993, 1994, 2000, 2005, and 2011 to 2018". — [GovInfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-10-17/html/2023-22377.htm)
- **Kauaʻi ʻōʻō.** Last confirmed record 28 Apr 1987 at Halepaʻakai Stream, Alakaʻi. It was a "vocal response to a recorded vocalization played by a field biologist" (a playback response). The rule describes the voice as "loud whistles that have been described as flute-like, echoing, and haunting". — [GovInfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-10-17/html/2023-22377.htm)
- **Kāmaʻo.** Last credible sighting 1987, with an unconfirmed report in the February 1989 Kauaʻi Forest Bird Survey. Estimated extinct in 1991, with 95% confidence it was extinct by 1999. Song "sweet and melodic to lavish and flute-like, often given just before dawn and after dusk". — [GovInfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-10-17/html/2023-22377.htm)
- **Kauaʻi ʻakialoa.** Last confirmed 1965; unconfirmed 1969. Formerly "widespread on Kauai and occupied all forest types above 656 feet elevation". — [GovInfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-10-17/html/2023-22377.htm). Wikipedia gives the last sighting as 1967 and a range above 350 m. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaua%CA%BBi_%CA%BBakialoa) (conflict: 1965 vs 1967; prefer USFWS)
- **Kauaʻi nukupuʻu.** Last confirmed 1899. Unconfirmed report 1995, and "possible population count was fewer than 10 birds in 1996". — [GovInfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-10-17/html/2023-22377.htm). Reports from 1984–1998 in the Alakaʻi were probably misidentified Kauaʻi ʻamakihi; one unconfirmed report in 2007. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaua%CA%BBi_nukupu%CA%BBu)
- **Poʻouli.** Known from Hanawī Natural Area Reserve, NE Maui. Observed in 1995–97 (81 sightings of five individuals), and last in 2003–2004. Extinction estimated for 2005 (upper 95% bound 2008). Described as "extremely cryptic", calling infrequently. — [GovInfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-10-17/html/2023-22377.htm)
- **Maui ʻākepa.** Hard to detect because vocalizations are "infrequent" and resemble other species. The Maui Forest Bird Recovery Project searched 2006–2011. — [GovInfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-10-17/html/2023-22377.htm)
- **Bridled white-eye (Guam).** 54 birds counted in 1981 and 49 in 1982; a single bird in 1983 at Pajon Basin (395 acres of limestone forest near Ritidian Point). It "vocalized with 'chipping calls' while flocking". — [GovInfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-10-17/html/2023-22377.htm)
- **Little Mariana fruit bat (Pteropus tokudae).** Last record a female collected in 1968 in northern Guam. Wingspan about 25.6–27.9 in. The Mariana fruit bat on Guam now fluctuates between 15 and 45 individuals. — [GovInfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-10-17/html/2023-22377.htm)
- The kāmaʻo was proposed extinct on 29 Sep 2021 and delisted in Oct 2023. — [Wikipedia: Kāmaʻo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C4%81ma%CA%BBo)

### Inferences
- For "secret traces at last known places", these are the best-documented final coordinates:
  - Kauaʻi ʻōʻō: Halepaʻakai Stream, Alakaʻi, roughly 22.1°N, 159.55°W. The coordinate is approximate and my own estimate from the Alakaʻi's location, not from a source.
  - Poʻouli: Hanawī NAR, NE Haleakalā, about 20.75°N, 156.1°W (approximate).
  - Bridled white-eye: Pajon Basin / Ritidian, northern Guam, about 13.6°N, 144.85°E (approximate).
- The Kauaʻi ʻōʻō's last record was an answer to playback. A "call with no answer, then one answer" is historically grounded.

### Gaps
- Exact coordinates are not in the sources I read. Last-record dates for Maui ʻākepa, Maui nukupuʻu and kākāwahie did not appear in the excerpt returned. Commonly cited years are Maui nukupuʻu 1996 (claim), Maui ʻākepa 1988 and kākāwahie 1963, but I have not verified them here.

## 2. Kauaʻi ʻōʻō / ʻōʻō ʻāʻā (Moho braccatus): recordings and acoustics

### Takeaway
This is the only Hawaiian honeyeater whose voice was ever recorded and published. There are 8 audio recordings in the Macaulay Library. The 1975 Alakaʻi recordings are by Sincock, Pratt and Zeillemaker. The "male calling for a mate who never answers" story comes from the post-1982 lone male (Hurricane Iwa, 23 Nov 1982), but who recorded the famous clip is disputed between sources.

My own spectral measurement of ML6050: loud, clear whistles at about 1.0–2.6 kHz (median about 1.7 kHz), notes about 0.06–0.16 s long, grouped in short phrases repeated every 2–5.5 s.

### Cited Findings
- **Recordings.**
  - ML6050: John L. Sincock, 6 Jun 1975, Alakaʻi Swamp, song.
  - ML6049: C. Fred Zeillemaker, 6 Jun 1975, Alakaʻi Swamp.
  - ML5015: Doug (H. Douglas) Pratt, 2 Jul 1975, Alakaʻi Swamp.
  - Source: search-result metadata for [ML6050](https://macaulaylibrary.org/asset/6050), [ML6049](https://macaulaylibrary.org/asset/6049), [ML5015](https://macaulaylibrary.org/audio/5015). The pages themselves are bot-blocked, so these come from search snippets. ML6031 also appeared in results but its metadata was not confirmed.
- Birds of the World lists 8 audio recordings in Macaulay. "Only the voice of the Kaua'i 'Ö'ö was ever recorded, archived, and published." Pairs duet, with the female giving fewer notes (search snippet of the account). — [Birds of the World (Sykes, Kepler, Kepler & Scott)](https://birdsoftheworld.org/bow/species/kauoo/cur/sounds). The full account is paywalled.
- Earbirding counts eight recordings, seven available online. — [Earbirding](https://earbirding.com/blog/?p=316)
- **Attribution conflict for the "lonely male" recording.**
  - EarthSky: "Ornithologist Jim Jacobi made a famous recording in 1986 of an individual male Kauai Oo… singing 1/2 of a duet, with no response". A remix was uploaded to YouTube by Robert Davis in 2009. — [EarthSky](https://earthsky.org/earth/extinct-bird-songs-woodpecker-warbler-kauai-oo/)
  - Wikipedia: "David Boynton made the bird's final known vocal recording in 1987". — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaua%CA%BBi_%CA%BB%C5%8D%CA%BB%C5%8D)
  - HPR's "The last Kauaʻi ʻōʻō" episode uses Fred Zeillemaker's ML6049, a 1975 recording from before the female was lost. — [HPR Manu Minute](https://www.hawaiipublicradio.org/podcast/manu-minute/2020-12-16/manu-minute-the-last-kauaˊi-ˊoˊo)
  - Treat the widely circulated "last male" audio as possibly a 1975 recording. The 1986 (Jacobi) and 1987 (Boynton) recordings may not be in the public ML set.
- **Last female / Hurricane Iwa.** Iwa passed just north of Kauaʻi on 23 Nov 1982. "The last female Kauaʻi ʻōʻō was lost in the storm. Her mate was recorded singing in solitary until he went silent and presumably died in 1987." — [Wikipedia: Hurricane Iwa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_Iwa). HPR dates Iwa to 1983, which is an error. — [HPR](https://www.hawaiipublicradio.org/podcast/manu-minute/2020-12-16/manu-minute-the-last-kauaˊi-ˊoˊo)
- **Population and film.** About 34 individuals in the early 1960s. John L. Sincock shot the only known film (Super 8) in the 1970s. Hurricanes Iwa (1982) and Iniki (1992) destroyed cavity trees. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaua%CA%BBi_%CA%BB%C5%8D%CA%BB%C5%8D)
- **Name.** "ʻōʻō" is onomatopoeic and "ʻāʻā" means dwarf. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaua%CA%BBi_%CA%BB%C5%8D%CA%BB%C5%8D). Perkins (1903) also classes Oo as an onomatopoeic name and explains Oo-aa as "dwarf Oo, of Kauai". — [Perkins 1903, Fauna Hawaiiensis I(4), archive.org](https://archive.org/details/agk2408.0001.004.umich.edu)
- **Perkins 1903 (DESCRIBED, primary).** All quotes: [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/agk2408.0001.004.umich.edu), pp. 443–444.
  - On voice: "The Oo-aa is always pleasant to hear, for its song though loud is very clear and melodious. … in the early morning and again towards dark the woods fairly resound with its notes and the wild song of the even commoner Amaui [kāmaʻo]." He heard in them "some resemblance to the cries of the other species of Oo, as though in the Kauai bird they were mellowed down and woven into song."
  - On the female's alarm: "a mere reiterated squeak, utterly unlike her usual call".
  - Munro compared the Oo-aa's song and habits to the New Zealand tūī.
- **Perkins 1903, non-vocal sounds and habits.**
  - "One's attention is frequently attracted to its presence by the rattling of large loose strips of the bark" as it forages woodpecker-like on ʻōhiʻa trunks.
  - Sociable, in "little companies of from four to eight".
  - Nests in the tops of koa.
  - Habitat: "Kauai, common and very generally distributed in the forests" (c. 1890s).
- **My measurement of ML6050 (analysis, not sampling).** Macaulay's public CDN served the 5 min 04 s file (48 kHz). I ran automated STFT tonal-event detection on it (1024-point FFT, 800–8000 Hz band). Strong tonal notes (≥15 dB above background, ≥50 ms, n=228) gave:
  - Peak frequency: p5–p95 1156–2531 Hz, median 1719 Hz. Most energy sits at 1.4–2.1 kHz, with a secondary cluster at 2.4–2.6 kHz.
  - Pitch sweep: usually within about 30–250 Hz, so near-constant-pitch whistles.
  - Note length: p10/50/90 = 64 / 96 / 162 ms.
  - Spacing within a phrase: p10/50/90 = 0.11 / 0.25 / 0.41 s between note onsets.
  - Phrases: 1–4 loud notes (with more quiet ones at lower thresholds), about 0.5 s long (up to about 1 s).
  - Phrase repetition: every 1.9 / 3.0 / 5.5 s (p10/50/90).
  - Nothing strong above about 2.7 kHz except short 4.0–4.7 kHz blips, which may belong to other species.
  - Caveats: automated detection, the file may contain other species, and this is one recording from 1975. — method run locally on [ML6050](https://macaulaylibrary.org/asset/6050)

### Inferences
- Synthesis model: sine-like whistles at 1.1–2.6 kHz, 60–160 ms, with slight glides; 2–6-note figures at about 4 notes/s; about 3 s between phrases; dawn and dusk peaks (Perkins).
  - Duet version: a second voice with fewer notes.
  - Solo "secret trace" version (post-1982): male phrase followed by the silence where the female's answer would go.
  - Add occasional bark-rattle foley.
- Historical abundance (Perkins: "numerous… widely distributed") supports dense dawn/dusk chorusing across all Kauaʻi forest in the 1890s. By 1970 it was confined to the Alakaʻi (about 1,100–1,600 m).

### Gaps
- Could not confirm ML catalogue numbers for the 1986 Jacobi and 1987 Boynton recordings. Macaulay search is bot-protected.
- Published kHz values from Sykes et al. are paywalled.
- Breeding season is not documented in the sources I read.

## 3. Kāmaʻo / large Kauaʻi thrush (Myadestes myadestinus)

### Takeaway
RECORDED: Robert Shallenberger, July 1975, Alakaʻi, at dusk. It was once the most common bird on Kauaʻi and sang before dawn and after dusk. Model it on the puaiohi and ʻōmaʻo.

### Cited Findings
- ML6027: Robert Shallenberger, 2 Jul 1975, 7:15 PM, headwaters of Halehaha Stream, Alakaʻi. ML6029: same recordist, 3 Jul 1975, 7:15 PM. — [ML6027](https://macaulaylibrary.org/asset/6027), [ML6029](https://macaulaylibrary.org/asset/6029) (search-snippet metadata)
- Song: "a complex melody composed of flute-like notes, liquid warbles, buzzy trills, and gurgling whistles". Call: "a raspy 'braak,' with an alternate high pitched note similar to a police whistle". In the late 1800s it was "the most common bird on Kauaʻi, occurring throughout all areas of the island". It used the understory of densely vegetated gulches. Last documented 1989 in the Alakaʻi. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C4%81ma%CA%BBo)
- USFWS: song given "just before dawn and after dusk". — [GovInfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-10-17/html/2023-22377.htm)
- **Perkins 1903, on all Hawaiian thrushes (Phaeornis = Myadestes), DESCRIBED.** All quotes: [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/agk2408.0001.004.umich.edu), pp. 375–378.
  - The song "surpasses in beauty that of all other native birds", and is "less melodious, far more jerky and less sustained" than the European song thrush.
  - "The wild outburst of song, which the Amaui pours forth on the wing as it descends from the top of a lofty tree to lower cover."
  - Counter-singing: "individuals sing in turn… the one beginning its song immediately after the other finishes", including multi-bird "concerts".
  - Birds keep favourite song posts and "sing at intervals during the whole day".
  - Alarm note "quah", which spreads from bird to bird. It also has "a very distinct simple whistle for a call note".
  - "The Amaui is of all birds the first to make itself heard in the morning… in full song while the stars were still shining."
  - Kauaʻi status: "generally distributed and common".
  - The name "Amaui" means "Maui's bird".
- Perkins on the small Kauaʻi thrush (puaiohi, P. palmeri, the living model): its song "is a simple trill with much resemblance to that of the Akialoa nukupuu", sometimes delivered in flight; alarm "a squeak". — [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/agk2408.0001.004.umich.edu)

### Inferences
- The kāmaʻo should open the dawn chorus, before first light, and close it at dusk. Songs are bursts with jerky phrasing, flight-song descents and counter-singing. Include "quah" alarms that propagate across the soundscape.

### Gaps
- No kHz data retrieved. Measuring ML6027/6029 in the same way as ML6050 requires the user's go-ahead to download.
- Puaiohi/ʻōmaʻo frequency data not retrieved.

## 4. ʻŌʻū (Psittirostra psittacea)

### Takeaway
RECORDED (Kauaʻi 1975). Canary-like whistled song opening with three or four penetrating whistles, and a long "enquiring" call. Formerly on all main islands. Last confirmed on Kauaʻi in 1989. Its extinction status is contested.

### Cited Findings
- HPR credits Steven R. Pantle, Macaulay ML 5897. Last confirmed Kauaʻi sighting 1989. "A handful of credible sightings on Hawaiʻi Island over the last few years"; searches use automated recorders and eDNA. — [HPR Manu Minute: the long-lost ʻōʻū](https://www.hawaiipublicradio.org/podcast/manu-minute/2023-10-11/manu-minute-the-long-lost-ou)
- Shallenberger recordings, 3 Jul 1975, Alakaʻi (ML6030/6032 per search results; unconfirmed). — [ML6030](https://macaulaylibrary.org/asset/6030), [ML6032](https://macaulaylibrary.org/asset/6032)
- Song "an ascending or descending whistle that may break into a sweet and distinct canary-like song". Diet centred on ʻieʻie fruit (Freycinetia). Breeding probably March–May. Wikipedia says IUCN declared it extinct in 2024. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%CA%BB%C5%8C%CA%BB%C5%AB). The IUCN 2024 claim is unverified here, and HPR (2023) implies it is still searched for.
- **Perkins 1903 (DESCRIBED).** All quotes: [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/agk2408.0001.004.umich.edu), pp. 394, 430–433.
  - "A rather sweet song, which at times reminds one somewhat of a canary… Three or four very distinct and penetrating whistled notes form the beginning of its full song."
  - "Sings very freely in the early morning hours, especially just before the nesting season", from "the loftiest dead branch".
  - Sometimes sings in flight, and small companies "sing in concert".
  - Call: "a rather long note, so whistled as generally to appear of an enquiring nature. In foggy weather especially, the birds are continually uttering this call."
  - The ʻōʻū and palila are the best Drepanid songsters.
  - Range: "all the forest-clad islands. Extinct or nearly so on Oahu".

### Gaps
- Whether IUCN has uplisted it to Extinct is unverified.
- The Wikipedia/Boynton 1987 attribution for the ʻōʻū appears conflated with the ʻōʻō.

## 5. Kauaʻi ʻakialoa (Akialoa stejnegeri) and Kauaʻi nukupuʻu (Hemignathus hanapepe)

### Takeaway
Both are DESCRIBED only; no recordings are known. Perkins describes short, vigorous trills and a loud "kee-wit" contact call for both genera, plus audible bark-tapping in the nukupuʻu group. Model on the living ʻakiapōlāʻau and Kauaʻi ʻamakihi.

### Cited Findings
- **ʻAkialoa (Hemignathus).** "The song of Hemignathus is a short, vigorous trill, recalling that of both Heterorhynchus [nukupuʻu] and Chlorodrepanis [ʻamakihi], but distinct from either. It has a louder and deeper call or alarm note." Birds sang more "when feeding on the nectar of flowers" (lobelias, which Hawaiians call haha and ʻōhāwai). Kauaʻi status: "at various elevations and in many localities, not at all rare". Formerly called ʻiʻiwi on Kauaʻi. — [Perkins 1903](https://archive.org/details/agk2408.0001.004.umich.edu)
- **Nukupuʻu (Heterorhynchus).**
  - "The song of the three species is very similar and is a short, loud trill… fairly lavish of this song".
  - "The call note is a loud 'kee-wit,' very clear and distinct, when the birds are in solitary pairs, and by it the sexes keep in touch when feeding in different trees". This "degenerates at times into a loud monosyllabic squeak" when alarmed.
  - Tapping: "gives several blows to the trunk, the sound of which can be heard at a considerable distance" (the Hawaiʻi bird is loudest).
  - Kauaʻi: "local and not common on the high plateau". Maui: "north-west slopes of Haleakala at 4000–4500 ft. Not rare."
  - Source: [Perkins 1903](https://archive.org/details/agk2408.0001.004.umich.edu)
- Pseudonestor (the living Maui parrotbill) has a song "identical" to Heterorhynchus. — [Perkins 1903](https://archive.org/details/agk2408.0001.004.umich.edu)

### Inferences
- Maui ʻalauahio, ʻakiapōlāʻau and kiwikiu recordings are defensible acoustic proxies for the nukupuʻu trill and the "kee-wit" call.

## 6. Other Hawaiian species: primary sound descriptions from Perkins 1903 (DESCRIBED)

### Takeaway
Perkins's Fauna Hawaiiensis (1903) contains explicit, synthesis-ready sound descriptions for most species that later went extinct. Several are cues for behaviour, weather and time of day, not just timbre. All quotes: [archive.org, Perkins 1903](https://archive.org/details/agk2408.0001.004.umich.edu).

### Cited Findings
- **Hawaiʻi ʻōʻō (Moho nobilis) and Bishop's ʻōʻō (M. bishopi).**
  - "The cry of the Oo is unlike that of any other native bird… usually dissyllabic, as represented by the bird's name."
  - Bishop's ʻōʻō at its fullest: "five syllables, 'owow, owów, -ow'", given as two continuous pairs with the stress on the second syllable. There is a slight pause between the pairs and a longer one before the last note, "which is extremely loud and might at times be almost termed a shriek."
  - Audible range: the Hawaiʻi bird at about half a mile; bishopi "at least 3000 feet".
  - Timing: "In fine weather they are mostly silent during the hotter hours, but in wet or foggy weather are often vociferous throughout the day." Cries are given in flight between flowering ʻōhiʻa, and are "incessant" when birds gather.
  - Song: a quiet song "extremely rarely heard", only from undergrowth.
  - In 1892 on a Hawaiʻi lava flow, "as many as a dozen… in a single tree" with ʻiʻiwi and ʻapapane. A few years later, "hardly a single 'Oo'" (after cattle arrived).
  - Wikipedia's "took-took" for Bishop's ʻōʻō conflicts with Perkins's "owow" (the snippet also attached "took-took" to the Kauaʻi bird). Prefer Perkins. — [Wikipedia: Bishop's ʻōʻō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop%27s_%CA%BB%C5%8D%CA%BB%C5%8D)
- **Kioea (Chaetoptila angustipluma).** Observed by Pickering and Peale (US Exploring Expedition) on the Hawaiʻi high plateau, at ʻōhiʻa flowers, with "a loud 'chuck' as its call". Already "very probably extinct" by 1903.
- **Mamo (Drepanis pacifica) and black mamo / ʻōʻō nuku ʻumu (D. funerea).**
  - Mamo call: "a single, rather long and plaintive note, as imitated by natives… regularly used to attract it to the hunter's snare". This is IMITATION evidence from kia manu practice.
  - Black mamo "sometimes… utter a loud cry of extraordinary clearness, repeated at short intervals".
  - Status: Molokaʻi, "higher forest and not common". Up to 12 mamo were taken in a day in Hilo around 1880.
- **Kia manu (bird-catching).** Natives "can imitate their call notes in the most perfect manner". The mamo "was first attracted by an imitation of its call note". Decoy birds were kept caged.
- **Koa-finches (Rhodacanthis).**
  - Song "entirely different from that of any other native bird. It consists of four, five, or even six whistled notes, of which the latter ones are much prolonged". It varies between individuals and is "very easily imitated". Birds answer especially "in misty weather".
  - A frightened female gives "a deep single note… so as to have a ventriloquial effect".
  - Elsewhere Perkins says the whistles "might appear to be rather the utterance of a man than of a bird".
  - Range: Kona, 4000–5000 ft, koa woods.
- **Kona grosbeak (Chloridops kona).**
  - Feeds "mostly in silence". Cracking naio seeds "a sound is produced, which is audible at some distance, and as it is incessant when the bird is feeding, by far the most easy way to get sight of this, is to listen attentively for the sound."
  - Only heard giving a squeaking cry in courtship chases.
  - Range about 4 sq miles in Kona, about 4000 ft. This is a non-vocal "trace" sound.
- **Kākāwahie and Oreomyza (creepers).**
  - Named "from the characteristic cry… the reiterated 'chip,' with which they resent the presence of an intruder". Kākāwahie is listed among names "derived from the nature of the sounds uttered".
  - Only the Maui and Lānaʻi species were heard to sing: a short trill during a spiral song flight.
  - Kākāwahie was "common both on leeward and windward Molokai".
- **ʻĀkepa (Loxops).**
  - Full song "a short trill, nearly or quite identical" across species. The female sings "more musically, but still more softly… practically continuous song lasting for some minutes."
  - Call "a simple 'keewit'". Oʻahu ʻākepa: "now very rare… I recently heard a pair calling to each other".
- **ʻAmaui / Oʻahu thrush and a probable Maui thrush.** A native said the Amaui "was abundant in the Iao valley" about 30 years before and "particularly mentioned and described the song".
- **Hawaiian rail / moho (Porzana/Zapornia sandwichensis).** Molokaʻi natives "still remembered and could imitate its cry". This is an IMITATION-tier trace for a bird with no description.
- **Onomatopoeic names.** Perkins lists ʻalalā, ʻelepaio, ʻōʻō and kioea as onomatopoeic, and ʻapapane, ʻakikeke and kākāwahie as named from the sounds they make.

### Inferences
- The ʻōʻō's weather gating (loud in fog and rain, silent in hot sun) and dawn/dusk choruses (Kauaʻi) can drive the artwork's timing directly.
- The Kona grosbeak can be rendered almost entirely as seed-cracking percussion.

### Gaps
- Kia manu, feather cloaks, ʻōlelo noʻeau and cultural protocol were assigned to a parallel researcher and are not yet integrated.
- Wilson & Evans, Rothschild (Laysan rail) and Munro are not covered.

## 7. Pacific headliners: sihek and Christmas Island pipistrelle

### Takeaway
The sihek is extinct in the wild on Guam (1986), but a population now lives on Palmyra Atoll (released September 2024, first wild eggs in about 40 years in 2025). The Christmas Island pipistrelle's last echolocation call was recorded on 26 Aug 2009 and is held by the NFSA.

### Cited Findings
- **Sihek.** Extinct in the wild in 1986 because of the brown tree snake. Nine birds (4 females, 5 males) were released at Palmyra Atoll, about 1,000 miles south of Honolulu, in September 2024. Pairs set up territories and three of four pairs laid eggs. — [AZA](https://www.aza.org/connect-stories/stories/bird-species-lost-from-the-wild-for-almost-40-years-lays-first-eggs-on-new-island-home); [USFWS](https://www.fws.gov/story/2024-09/guam-sihek-released-wild); [ZSL](https://zsl.org/news-and-events/news/extinct-wild-sihek-released-wild-first-time-40-years). The release count conflicts: one snippet says "six… on September 23, 2024", another says nine.
- **Christmas Island pipistrelle.** Last acoustic signal recorded on the evening of 26 Aug 2009. Dr Rupert Baker (Healesville Sanctuary) recorded it through a handheld detector onto his mobile phone during an attempt to capture a breeding pair. The recording is in the NFSA's Sounds of Australia. — [NFSA](https://www.nfsa.gov.au/collection/item/last-call-christmas-island-pipistrelle); [Wilderness Society](https://wilderness.org.au/news-events/last-call-of-the-wild); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christmas_Island_pipistrelle)

### Gaps
- Pipistrelle call frequencies (kHz), sihek and koko call acoustics, and all other Micronesian, Bonin, Ryukyu, Lord Howe, Norfolk, Polynesian and Melanesian species are pending from parallel researchers.

## 8. Oʻahu, Molokaʻi, Lānaʻi, Maui, Hawaiʻi Island and Laysan: last records, voices, cultural context

### Takeaway
None of the species below is confirmed RECORDED, apart from the poʻouli (one Macaulay recording reported, catalogue number unverified) and the living ʻalalā. There are also unconfirmed 1994–95 audio recordings of the Maui ʻākepa. Everything else is DESCRIBED, IMITATION or INFERRED.

The strongest acoustic clues are:
- The mamo's "long plaintive note", which kia manu imitated as a lure.
- The black mamo's loud, clear repeated cry.
- The koa-finches' human-like whistles.
- The kākāwahie's wood-chopping "chip".
- The Kona grosbeak's seed-cracking.
- The Laysan rail's "marbles on a glass roof" evening chorus.
- Dickey's 1923 note on the last Laysan honeycreepers singing.

### Cited Findings
- **2023 rule last-record years for Maui and Molokaʻi species:**
  - Maui ʻākepa: last confirmed 1988.
  - Maui nukupuʻu: "last confirmed … 1981". This conflicts with Wikipedia, which reports a male around 1994 and late-1990s reports.
  - Kākāwahie: 1963.
  - Rare-bird searches used "audio playback of species vocalizations".
  - The olomaʻo and ʻōʻū were NOT delisted.
  - Sources: [GovInfo 88 FR 71644](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-10-17/html/2023-22377.htm); [Wikipedia: Maui nukupuʻu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maui_nukupu%CA%BBu)
- **Oʻahu ʻōʻō (Moho apicalis).**
  - Range: Koʻolau wet forest. Subfossils at Barbers Point.
  - Bloxam in 1825: "very scarce … I did not see even one."
  - Last record: three birds collected by Deppe in Nuʻuanu Valley, 11–15 Jan 1837 (approx. 21.35N, 157.82W).
  - Feathers went into capes and were paid as tribute.
  - No voice description found; INFERRED from other ʻōʻō.
  - Source: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/O%CA%BBahu_%CA%BB%C5%8D%CA%BB%C5%8D)
- **Bishop's ʻōʻō.**
  - Discovered by Palmer in 1892 on Molokaʻi. Subfossils at Olinda, Maui (~4,500 ft).
  - Last confirmed 1904 (Munro); Munro searched without success in 1915.
  - A 1981 claim by Stephen Sabo on Maui is unconfirmed.
  - Source: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop%27s_%CA%BB%C5%8D%CA%BB%C5%8D). Perkins's five-syllable "owow" description is in section 6.
- **Hawaiʻi ʻōʻō.**
  - Last record 1934, slopes of Mauna Loa.
  - Calls "harsh and disyllabic". Its wings made "a buzzing sound while flapping quickly", a non-vocal layer.
  - Puʻu ʻŌʻō means "Hill of the ʻōʻō bird".
  - Yellow feathers went into ʻahu ʻula and kāhili.
  - Source: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawai%CA%BBi_%CA%BB%C5%8D%CA%BB%C5%8D)
- **Kioea.**
  - Hawaiʻi Island only; four specimens. Last record 1859.
  - Its feathers were "not used in Hawaiian featherwork, nor is it mentioned in any chants or legends".
  - Voice: only Perkins's "loud 'chuck'" (see section 6).
  - Source: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kioea)
- **ʻĀmaui (Myadestes woahensis).**
  - Oʻahu; one specimen from 1825. Probably extinct by the 1850s.
  - Song "reported to be similar to the Molokai olomaʻo".
  - Source: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%CA%BB%C4%80maui)
- **Olomaʻo (Myadestes lanaiensis).**
  - Maui, Lānaʻi and Molokaʻi; "common to abundant" in the late 1800s.
  - Last records: Lānaʻi 1933 (other sources say 1931). Molokaʻi 1980, Kamakou Preserve (approx. 21.11N, 156.90W).
  - Status: IUCN CR (possibly extinct); still federally listed.
  - Voice: "a complex melody of flute-like notes, liquid warbles, and gurgling whistles", "a catlike rasp", and high whistles.
  - Source: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olomao)
  - Henshaw 1902 on the living ʻōmaʻo: song "jerky and consists of widely-spaced syllables, but is pleasing and, at times, sweet". — [Henshaw 1902, archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/cu31924000063275/cu31924000063275_djvu.txt)
- **Lānaʻi hookbill.**
  - One specimen, collected by Munro on 22 Feb 1913 in Kaiholena Valley (approx. 20.83N, 156.92W). Munro also saw it on 16 Mar 1916 and 12 Aug 1918.
  - Habitat: dry forest at 2,000–2,600 ft.
  - Voice: "an inconspicuous chirp".
  - Source: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C4%81na%CA%BBi_hookbill)
- **ʻUla-ʻai-hāwane.**
  - Name: "red [bird] that eats hāwane" (loulu palm fruit).
  - Range: Kohala, Hilo and Kona.
  - Last confirmed 1892, Kohala (approx. 20.08N, 155.72W); unconfirmed 1937.
  - No voice description found; INFERRED.
  - Source: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%CA%BBUla-%CA%BBai-h%C4%81wane)
- **Hawaiʻi mamo.**
  - Last specimen taken by Palmer in 1892. Last sighting July 1898 near Kaumana, reported by Henshaw (approx. 19.68N, 155.15W).
  - Call: "a single rather long and plaintive note", used as a lure.
  - Source: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawai%CA%BBi_mamo)
- **Black mamo / hoa / ʻōʻō nuku ʻumu.**
  - Molokaʻi. Discovered by Perkins in 1893; last specimen taken by Bryan in 1907.
  - Voice: "nasal whistles that sounded like a flute along with a long held out trill".
  - Source: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_mamo)
- **Kona grosbeak.**
  - Naio forest on ʻaʻā lava at 1,400–1,500 m; the whole range was about 10 km². Last collected 1894.
  - Wilson: "the incessant cracking of the fruits … can be heard for a considerable distance".
  - Source: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kona_grosbeak)
- **Koa-finches.**
  - Greater koa-finch last recorded 1896; lesser 1891.
  - Sources: [Greater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_koa_finch); [Lesser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lesser_koa_finch). Perkins's whistle descriptions are in section 6.
- **ʻAkialoa (Hawaiʻi Island).**
  - Henshaw: "The akialoa has a characteristic call, but I have never heard it sing." — [Henshaw 1902](https://archive.org/stream/cu31924000063275/cu31924000063275_djvu.txt)
  - The Wikipedia extinction date of "1940" is doubtful. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawai%CA%BBi_%CA%BBakialoa)
- **Oʻahu nukupuʻu.** About nine specimens taken in 1837 (Deppe, Townsend), a few more through 1841. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/O%CA%BBahu_nukupu%CA%BBu)
- **Maui ʻākepa.**
  - Last confirmed 1988, Hanawī (approx. 20.80N, 156.12W).
  - "Unconfirmed audio recordings in 1994 (Hanawī NAR) and 1995 (Kīpahulu)". This is a possible disputed RECORDED tier.
  - Voice: "a slight quivering whistle ending with a long trill".
  - Source: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maui_%CA%BB%C4%81kepa)
- **Oʻahu ʻākepa.** Gone around 1900. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/O%CA%BB%C4%81kepa)
- **Kākāwahie.**
  - Last record 1963, ʻŌhiʻalele Plateau, Kamakou (approx. 21.13N, 156.93W).
  - Wilson heard "a chip like someone was cutting wood in the distance". The name means "wood-chopping", so it is IMITATION as well as DESCRIBED.
  - Its red feathers were used in capes and lei.
  - Source: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kakawahie)
- **Greater ʻamakihi.**
  - Near Hilo, 1890–93; lost after 1901 cane clearing (approx. 19.72N, 155.12W).
  - Source: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_%CA%BBamakihi)
  - Henshaw on the Hawaiʻi ʻamakihi as a model: call "a low sweet 'tweet'", song "a low, simple but … rather sweet trill". — [Henshaw 1902](https://archive.org/stream/cu31924000063275/cu31924000063275_djvu.txt)
- **Poʻouli.**
  - Discovered 1973 on NE Haleakalā at about 1,980 m.
  - The last bird was captured on 9 Sep 2004 and died on 26 Nov 2004.
  - IUCN Extinct 2019.
  - Source: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Po%CA%BBouli)
  - Only documented vocalization: a sharp, harsh "chik", similar to the Maui creeper's. It "seldom calls or sings". — [USGS publication record](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/poouli-melamprosops-phaeosoma) (search snippet)
  - One Macaulay recording is reported online. — [Earbirding](https://earbirding.com/blog/?p=316)
- **Laysan rail.**
  - About 2,000 birds around 1900; two left on Laysan by 1923.
  - Last record June 1944, Eastern Island, Midway (approx. 28.21N, 177.33W), after rats arrived in WWII.
  - Voice: soft warbling chirps, and "rattling, scolding calls not unlike a mechanical alarm clock".
  - Evening chorus: "[a] handful or two of marbles being thrown on a glass roof and then descending in a succession of bounds". Wikipedia credits Frohawk 1892; the attribution is unverified.
  - No film or recording found.
  - Source: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laysan_rail)
- **Laysan millerbird.**
  - Abundant in the 1890s; last records in the late 1910s.
  - Living model: the Nihoa millerbird. 24 birds were moved to Laysan in Sep 2011.
  - Sources: [Laysan millerbird](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laysan_millerbird); [Nihoa millerbird](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nihoa_millerbird)
- **Laysan honeycreeper.**
  - Palmer in 1890 called it "the rarest of the island's birds". About 300 birds in 1911.
  - On 18 Apr 1923, Donald Dickey (Tanager Expedition) filmed a singing male. The film was presumably silent. A gale with sandstorms began on 23 Apr and the last birds died.
  - Dickey: "charming song is out of proportion to their size".
  - Source: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laysan_honeycreeper)
  - Palmer, via Rothschild: a bird held in the hand "began to sing … I answered it with a whistle, which it returned and continued to do so for some minutes". — [Rothschild, Avifauna of Laysan](https://archive.org/details/avifaunaLaysannTextRoth)
- **Hawaiian rail (moho).**
  - Name read as "bird that crows in the grass".
  - Range: Puna around present-day Mountain View (approx. 19.55N, 155.11W).
  - Last reliable record 1884; questionable 1893.
  - Hunting was reserved to aliʻi by kapu.
  - Source: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaiian_rail). Perkins's note that Molokaʻi natives could still imitate its cry is in section 6.
- **ʻAlalā (extinct in the wild 2002).**
  - At least 24 call types, plus "a complex, burbling song".
  - Regarded as an ʻaumakua by some families.
  - Five birds were released on Maui on 4 Dec 2024.
  - Source: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%CA%BBAlal%C4%81)
- **ʻAkikiki.**
  - Pakele, the last known wild ʻakikiki, was last seen on 29 Aug 2025. KFBRP posted a tribute on 27 Aug 2026. Four of her offspring are in conservation breeding.
  - No formal "extinct in the wild" declaration was found.
  - Sources: [KFBRP](https://kauaiforestbirds.org/honoring-pakele-the-last-known-wild-%ca%bbakikiki/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%CA%BBAkikiki)
- **ʻAkekeʻe.** Fewer than 638 birds in 2021, declining about 21% a year. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%CA%BBAkeke%CA%BBe)
- **Cultural: featherwork.**
  - ʻAhu ʻula used red feathers from ʻiʻiwi and ʻapapane, yellow from ʻōʻō and mamo, black from ʻōʻō, and occasionally green from ʻōʻū.
  - Feathers were tied onto olonā netting.
  - Kamehameha I placed a kapu requiring that black-and-yellow birds be released after plucking.
  - Source: [Wikipedia: ʻAhu ʻula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%CA%BBAhu_%CA%BBula)
  - The figure of "450,000 feathers from 80,000 birds" for Kamehameha's cloak is a popular estimate, not verified with Bishop Museum. — same page
  - Henshaw, quoting Townsend 1835: catchers held out a flower so a nectar-feeding bird put its bill in and could be seized. — [Henshaw 1902](https://archive.org/stream/cu31924000063275/cu31924000063275_djvu.txt)

### Inferences
- Many "voices" survive only as Hawaiian imitations: bird catchers' lure calls, names such as kākāwahie and moho, and the Molokaʻi elders who could still imitate the rail's cry. This IMITATION tier is culturally owned knowledge. Rendering it calls for consultation with Kanaka Maoli practitioners, especially for birds regarded as ʻaumakua (ʻalalā, and others by family).
- Suggested living acoustic models:

| Lost species | Living model |
|---|---|
| Mamo, black mamo | ʻiʻiwi |
| Laysan honeycreeper | ʻapapane |
| Olomaʻo, ʻāmaui, kāmaʻo | ʻōmaʻo, puaiohi |
| Maui and Oʻahu ʻākepa | Hawaiʻi ʻākepa |
| Nukupuʻu | ʻakiapōlāʻau, kiwikiu |
| Kākāwahie | Maui ʻalauahio |
| Koa-finches | Laysan finch |
| Laysan millerbird | Nihoa millerbird |

### Gaps
- No catalogue numbers were verified for the poʻouli, the Maui ʻākepa 1994–95 audio, or the olomaʻo.
- No frequency figures for any model.
- ʻŌlelo noʻeau (Pukui 1983) entries and oli/mele that imitate bird calls were not researched.
- Munro 1944, Banko's history and Richardson 1949 were not read.
- Unresolved date conflicts: Maui nukupuʻu (1981 vs. 1990s), olomaʻo on Lānaʻi (1931 vs. 1933), Hawaiʻi ʻakialoa ("1940").

## 9. Micronesia, Bonin/Ogasawara and the Ryukyus

### Takeaway
Only the sihek and the koʻkoʻ are RECORDED, and both mostly from captive or reintroduced birds. The only voice descriptions are:
- Sihek: 3–5 harsh, loud notes followed by softer ones, with a dawn call regular enough to tell time by.
- Koʻkoʻ: "kip" notes and a "kee-yu" whistle in group choruses triggered by disturbance.
- Pohnpei starling: "see-ay".
- Bonin grosbeak (Kittlitz 1828): a soft, pure, high note.

Everything else is INFERRED. No frequency figures were found for any bird. The Mariana swiftlet's echolocation (3–10 kHz click pairs) is the one quantitative sound.

### Cited Findings
- **Sihek (Todiramphus cinnamominus).**
  - Range: all Guam habitats "except pure savannah and wetlands".
  - Numbers: about 3,000 in the early 1980s, about 30 by 1986.
  - Breeding: mainly December–July, in tree cavities 3–8 m up.
  - Voice: "a loud, raspy, distinct call that generally consists of three to five harsh, loud notes, followed by several similar but much softer notes". The first call comes "at about the same time every day, usually at dawn".
  - Source: [Smithsonian National Zoo](https://nationalzoo.si.edu/animals/guam-kingfisher); [Tracy Aviary](https://tracyaviary.org/liberty-park/birds-nature/guam-kingfisher)
  - Last wild year: 1986 per [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guam_kingfisher); 1988 per the [National Aviary](https://www.aviary.org/conservation/projects/ssp/guam-kingfishers/).
  - Palmyra: 9 birds on Cooper Island (Sept 2024), first eggs April 2025 (infertile), all 9 alive in Feb 2026, second cohort planned for late 2026. — [National Aviary](https://www.aviary.org/conservation/projects/ssp/guam-kingfishers/); [Colossal](https://colossalfoundation.org/?p=899)
  - Hatching of chicks on Palmyra is unverified.
  - The "kshh-kshh" wording and formal pair duets were not found in any source.
- **Koʻkoʻ / Guam rail (Hypotaenidia owstoni).**
  - Numbers: 60,000–80,000 in the late 1960s; 21 wild birds captured in 1985.
  - Breeding: ground nests year-round, peak July–November.
  - Reintroduced to Rota and Cocos Island. Downlisted from EW to CR in 2019.
  - Voice: short "kip" notes and breeding-season screeches.
  - Sources: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guam_rail); [Smithsonian](https://nationalzoo.si.edu/animals/guam-rail-koko); [Guampedia](https://www.guampedia.com/guam-rail/)
  - Cincinnati Zoo (search snippet): "a piercing kee-yu whistle or a series of short kip whistles … typically given by two or more birds in response to loud noise".
  - A Guam NWR recording is mentioned by Guampedia; no catalogue number.
- **Chuguangguang / Guam flycatcher (Myiagra freycineti).**
  - Common in the early 1970s. Last recorded 1983–84 around Santa Rosa (approx. 13.53N, 144.91E).
  - No voice description found; INFERRED from Myiagra oceanica.
  - Source: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guam_flycatcher)
- **Nosaʼ / bridled white-eye.**
  - Guam form gone in 1983. The living model is Z. c. saypani of Saipan, Tinian and Aguiguan (620,000–940,000 birds).
  - Source: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridled_white-eye). The "chipping calls" line is in the USFWS rule (section 1).
- **Fanihi / little Mariana fruit bat (Pteropus tokudae).**
  - Wingspan about 70 cm.
  - Last records: a roosting female escaped capture at Tarague cliff in March 1967; a female with young was shot on 5 Jun 1968 (approx. 13.60N, 144.89E).
  - No voice data.
  - Source: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guam_flying_fox)
  - The Mariana fruit bat is a CHamoru "culinary delicacy". — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariana_fruit_bat)
- **Åga / Mariana crow.**
  - Extirpated from Guam by 2011; fewer than 200 on Rota.
  - Voice: "a high-pitched series of one to three caw or hi sounds", with nasal and guttural calls.
  - Source: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariana_crow)
- **Nightingale reed warbler (Guam).** Last seen 1969; nested year-round in wetlands. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nightingale_reed_warbler)
- **Other Guam losses.**
  - Guam rufous fantail: last 1984.
  - Micronesian myzomela: gone from Guam in 1986. On Kosrae its song is "a continuous stream of trilled whistles and phrases, lasting for about 25 minutes".
  - Sources: [Marianas list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_threatened,_endangered_and_extinct_species_in_the_Mariana_Islands); [Micronesian myzomela](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micronesian_myzomela)
- **Mariana mallard (Anas oustaleti).**
  - Range: Guam (Talofofo), Tinian and Saipan.
  - Breeding: January–July.
  - Last records: Guam 1967, Tinian 1974, Saipan 1979. Delisted in 2004.
  - INFERRED from mallard and Pacific black duck.
  - Source: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariana_mallard)
- **Kosrae crake.**
  - Kosraean name "the one who lands in the taro plot".
  - Two specimens taken by Kittlitz in December 1827 in coastal swamp and taro marsh. Absent in 1880 and 1931.
  - Source: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kosrae_crake)
- **Kosrae starling.** Montane forest; 5 Kittlitz specimens from 1827–28. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kosrae_starling)
- **Pohnpei starling.**
  - Montane forest above 425 m. Seen by Marshall in 1956; a female shot in 1994 was recovered in 1995.
  - Voice: "a bell-like shrill 'see-ay'", which matches its name "sie".
  - Source: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pohnpei_starling)
- **Bonin grosbeak.**
  - Chichijima; collected 1827–28, gone by about 1830.
  - Kittlitz: "a soft, pure and high note, sometimes short, sometimes drawn out; sometimes given singly, sometimes in a short series".
  - Source: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonin_grosbeak)
- **Bonin thrush.** Kittlitz in 1828 found it "common enough around the landing site", ground-dwelling. Gone by the 1830s. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonin_thrush)
- **Bonin wood pigeon.** Last specimen taken on Nakodojima, 15 Sep 1889. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonin_wood_pigeon)
- **Bonin night heron.** Last taken 1889. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonin_nankeen_night_heron)
- **Ryukyu kingfisher.**
  - One specimen, dated 1887 and labelled Miyako. It may be a mislabelled Guam bird.
  - Sources: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ryukyu_kingfisher); [ja.wikipedia](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ミヤコショウビン)
- **Ryukyu wood pigeon.**
  - Last Okinawa record 1904. Last bird: a female on Minami-Daitō in 1936.
  - Sources: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ryukyu_wood_pigeon); [ja.wikipedia](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/リュウキュウカラスバト)
- **Daitō subspecies.**
  - Daitō varied tit: last 1938. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daito_varied_tit)
  - Daitō wren: one specimen from 1938; its validity is doubtful. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daito_wren)
- **Okinawa flying fox.** Now Data Deficient; the specimens' provenance is doubtful. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Okinawa_flying_fox)
- **Mariana swiftlet.**
  - Status: Vulnerable/Endangered; about 900 on Guam in 2006.
  - Aerodramus echolocation: "two broad band pulses (3–10 kHz) separated by a slight pause (1–3 milliseconds)".

### Inferences
- The sihek's reliable dawn call makes a strong time-of-day anchor for Guam in the artwork. So does the koʻkoʻ's group "kip" chorus, which is triggered by disturbance.

### Gaps
- Jenkins 1983 (Ornithological Monographs 31), Baker 1951 and Kittlitz 1858 were inaccessible. These are the key sources for Guam and Bonin voices.
- No Macaulay catalogue numbers were confirmed for any species.
- No Hz values for any bird.
- Unresolved conflicts: sihek last wild year (1986 vs. 1988), Guam flycatcher (1983 vs. 1984), IUCN extinction year for P. tokudae (2004 vs. 2020).
- Not covered: Palau, Yap and Chuuk species; the Daitō bush warbler; the Borodino thrush.
