# Extinct and "not heard since" species of Aotearoa New Zealand and Australia: what is known of their sounds

Scope: NZ (incl. Chatham Is., subantarctic islands) and Australia (mainland, Tasmania, Kangaroo Is., King Is., Torres Strait; Christmas Island added because the brief lists the Christmas Island rat). Species lost since about 1500, with pre-1500 NZ taxa (moa, Haast's eagle, owlet-nightjar) marked as borderline.

Provenance tiers used throughout:
- **RECORDED**: an actual recording of the animal exists.
- **IMITATION**: a recorded human mimicry exists.
- **DESCRIBED**: written descriptions by observers only.
- **INFERRED**: nothing survives. Only anatomy or living relatives are available as a model.

Research method note: the WebSearch budget for the session ran out part-way through. The late Australian-mammal items therefore rest on Wikipedia pages fetched directly, not on primary literature. Several primary sites returned HTTP 403 and could not be read: Te Ara, NZ Geographic, PNAS (Woinarski et al. 2015), Royal Society (Crates et al. 2021) and allaboutbirds. Coordinates in the Inferences sections are my own approximate gazetteer estimates from place names, not sourced values.

## 1. Which species survive as real sound, and which only as words? (provenance overview)

### Takeaway
For the huia, no recording exists. What survives is one human imitation, Hēnare Hāmana's whistled male and female calls from 1949. Among truly lost species in this region, only these have genuine recordings of the animal itself: the two gastric-brooding frogs (Keith McDonald recordings, via FrogID) and the Christmas Island pipistrelle (ultrasonic echolocation, 26 Aug 2009, NFSA). There are also "not heard since" cases: unconfirmed mystacinid-like bat echolocation (1999) and a possible South Island kōkako call (2021). Everything else is DESCRIBED (19th-century prose) or INFERRED (anatomy and relatives). The thylacine was filmed, but no sound of it was recorded.

### Cited Findings
- Huia: "No original recordings exist; only human imitations survive." — [NZ Birds Online: Huia](https://www.nzbirdsonline.org.nz/species/huia)
- Huia imitation by Hēnare Hāmana, 1949, Wellington, 3 min 50 s, introduced by R.A.L. Batley. Ngā Taonga ref. 26325 (RNZ Collection). — [Ngā Taonga catalogue 26325](https://ngataonga.org.nz/search-use-collection/search/26325)
- Thylacine: "Few thylacine vocalisations were reported and unfortunately none recorded." The 1933 David Fleay film at Beaumaris Zoo is silent. — [Search summary incl. NFSA](https://www.nfsa.gov.au/latest/tasmanian-tiger-last-known-footage-thylacine); [Wikipedia: Thylacine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thylacine)
- The NFSA describes the 1935 film *Tasmania the Wonderland* as a professionally produced sound film that includes the last captive thylacine. Wikipedia says no sound recording of the animal exists. So the film's soundtrack is narration or music, not thylacine voice. Treat any "thylacine sound" claim as unverified. — [NFSA: last known footage](https://www.nfsa.gov.au/latest/tasmanian-tiger-last-known-footage-thylacine); [Wikipedia: Thylacine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thylacine)
- Southern gastric-brooding frog (*Rheobatrachus silus*): "Call Recording: Available. Recorded by Keith McDonald." — [FrogID: R. silus](https://www.frogid.net.au/frogs/rheobatrachus-silus/)
- Northern gastric-brooding frog (*R. vitellinus*): call recording available, recorded by Keith McDonald. — [FrogID: R. vitellinus](https://www.frogid.net.au/frogs/rheobatrachus-vitellinus/)
- Christmas Island pipistrelle: the last acoustic signal was recorded 26 Aug 2009 with a detector. It was later added to the NFSA "Sounds of Australia" collection as "Last call of the Christmas Island Pipistrelle". — [NFSA collection item](https://www.nfsa.gov.au/collection/item/last-call-christmas-island-pipistrelle); [Wilderness Society 2018](https://wilderness.org.au/news-events/last-call-of-the-wild)
- Greater short-tailed bat: a 1999 expedition recorded "unusual 'mystacinid-like' echolocation calls" on Putauhina Island. No bat was captured. — [Wikipedia: NZ greater short-tailed bat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_greater_short-tailed_bat)
- South Island kōkako: a possible call ("a distinct couple of soft long notes") was recorded on the Heaphy Track on 29 Dec 2021 and followed by a sighting. It has not been accepted as confirmed. — [Wikipedia: South Island kōkako](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Island_k%C5%8Dkako)
- Haast's eagle: NZ Birds Online hosts a "Digital reconstruction (with weather effects)" and a "Simulated call". Both are reconstructions, not recordings. — [NZ Birds Online: Haast's eagle](https://www.nzbirdsonline.org.nz/species/haasts-eagle)

### Inferences
- A usable provenance ladder for the artwork:
  - **RECORDED**: *R. silus*, *R. vitellinus*, Christmas Island pipistrelle.
  - **RECORDED-unconfirmed**: *Mystacina robusta* (1999), South Island kōkako (2021).
  - **IMITATION**: huia (Hāmana 1949).
  - **DESCRIBED**: laughing owl, piopio, bush wren, NZ quail, Chatham bellbird, Chatham fernbird, NZ little bittern, merganser, Chatham rail, thylacine, lesser bilby, Maclear's rat, sharp-snouted day frog, southern day frog.
  - **INFERRED**: moa, Haast's eagle, owlet-nightjar, Hodgens' waterhen, Lyall's wren, KI and King Island emus, paradise parrot (no voice description found), desert rat-kangaroo, toolache, crescent nailtail, pig-footed bandicoots, broad-faced potoroo, Nullarbor dwarf bettong, Bramble Cay melomys, bulldog rat, other rodents.
- "Returned" and "fading" contrast cases: night parrot (thought lost, now recorded) and regent honeyeater (song itself disappearing).

### Gaps
- I could not confirm whether the gastric-brooding frog recordings are publicly playable on FrogID, or held only in the Australian Museum or McDonald archive.
- I could not confirm the recording format and technical details of the pipistrelle file (NFSA page returned 403).

## 2. Huia (Heteralocha acutirostris): the imitation, the duet, the tapu

### Takeaway
The huia was a North Island wattlebird with sex-differentiated, flute-like calls audible to about 400 m. Pairs duetted in alternation. Its name comes from its whistle "uia, uia, uia". The only sonic trace is Hāmana's 1949 whistled imitation:
- male: a descending cadence
- female: a three-note rise and fall

Both come from a Māori hunter's lure-call tradition. The bird and its feathers are tapu. This is the most culturally sensitive sound in the catalogue.

### Cited Findings
- **Names:** Scientific *Heteralocha acutirostris* (Gould, 1837). The Māori name derives from the call, "a smooth, unslurred whistle rendered as *uia, uia, uia* or *where are you?*" — [NZ Birds Online](https://www.nzbirdsonline.org.nz/species/huia)
- **Range and abundance:**
  - Pre-human bones show it was common throughout the North Island and absent from the South Island.
  - After Europeans arrived it survived in the Ruahine, Tararua and Rimutaka ranges, northern Wairarapa and the Wellington hills.
  - Genetic estimate: 34,000–89,000 birds historically. — [NZ Birds Online](https://www.nzbirdsonline.org.nz/species/huia)
  - Evans also places the calls in the Urewera. — [Evans, "Echoes of the past"](https://www.thomvandooren.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/Evans-Echoes-of-the-Past.pdf)
- **Last record:** last accepted sighting 1907. "Several birds probably survived into the 1920s." — [NZ Birds Online](https://www.nzbirdsonline.org.nz/species/huia). Ngā Taonga places the last authenticated sighting in the Tararua Ranges. — [Ngā Taonga blog](https://www.ngataonga.org.nz/blog/nz-history/the-call-of-the-huia/)
- **Status:** Extinct in the NZ Threat Classification (Robertson et al. 2021, "No change"). — [DOC NZTCS 36 (2021)](https://www.doc.govt.nz/globalassets/documents/science-and-technical/nztcs36entire.pdf)
- **Voice (DESCRIBED):**
  - "various melodious and flute-like calls, audible up to 400 m away"
  - "the calls of males and females differed"
  - duetting pairs "repeatedly called alternately, answering one another", with bills angled 30–45° from vertical. — [NZ Birds Online](https://www.nzbirdsonline.org.nz/species/huia)
- **Breeding:**
  - Season September–February, egg-laying peak September–October.
  - Monogamous pairs. Males fed females during courtship and incubation, as in kōkako and saddleback. — [NZ Birds Online](https://www.nzbirdsonline.org.nz/species/huia)
- **Movement sound cue:** huia moved by hopping and bounding along branches (reportedly "twenty feet" at a time) and seldom flew above tree height. — [NZ Birds Online](https://www.nzbirdsonline.org.nz/species/huia)
- **IMITATION, the Hāmana recording:**
  - Robert A.L. Batley of Moawhango took Hēnare Hāmana, a guide on the unsuccessful 1909 government expedition into the Ruahine Ranges, to record in Wellington in 1949.
  - When hunting huia, "Māori imitated the bird's call to lure it to their snares—and Hamana was an expert."
  - Batley's narration: "Let us imagine two birds are feeding on a rotten tree … After a while, the female climbs to the top of the tree and glides into the distance."
  - "Hāmana whistles: the male's call, a descending cadence; the female's answer, a three-note rise and fall. He repeats the calls." — [Evans (NZ Geographic), PDF](https://www.thomvandooren.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/Evans-Echoes-of-the-Past.pdf)
- **Recording details: sources conflict.**
  - Evans / NZ Geographic: 2YA studios, 10-inch acetate disc, 1949. — [Evans PDF](https://www.thomvandooren.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/Evans-Echoes-of-the-Past.pdf)
  - Ngā Taonga's own blog: Columbus Recording Studios, Wakefield Street, Wellington, 1948 "(or 1949)", 78 rpm disc. — [Ngā Taonga blog](https://www.ngataonga.org.nz/blog/nz-history/the-call-of-the-huia/)
  - Ngā Taonga catalogue: 1949, Wellington, 3:50, RNZ Collection. — [Ngā Taonga 26325](https://ngataonga.org.nz/search-use-collection/search/26325)
- **Where the copies are:**
  - The original disc is at Whanganui Regional Museum.
  - Ngā Taonga holds a 1960s reel-to-reel copy.
  - Hāmana was also known as Harry Salmon. — [Evans PDF](https://www.thomvandooren.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/Evans-Echoes-of-the-Past.pdf); [Ngā Taonga 26325](https://ngataonga.org.nz/search-use-collection/search/26325)
- **Related archive items:**
  - A 1967 documentary, "The Huia", opens with a poroporoaki by Rev. Kingi Ihaka (Te Aupōuri). — [Ngā Taonga blog](https://www.ngataonga.org.nz/blog/nz-history/the-call-of-the-huia/)
  - Matthew Lark's radio series "Animals on the Brink, Part 1: The Huia". — [Ngā Taonga A271254](https://ngataonga.org.nz/search-use-collection/search/A271254/)
- **Artistic reuse:** Ngā Taonga "frequently receives requests from overseas artists to use the recording". Uses include David Hindley's 1992 score and Julianne Warren's works, one shown at the Deutsches Museum. — [Evans PDF](https://www.thomvandooren.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/Evans-Echoes-of-the-Past.pdf)
- **Cultural significance:**
  - "Considered tapu by Māori". — [Evans PDF](https://www.thomvandooren.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/Evans-Echoes-of-the-Past.pdf)
  - Tail feathers were status markers. After the Duke of York wore one during his 1901 visit, international demand spiked.
  - Harvest scale: 646 skins taken by 11 hunters in one month in 1863. Reischek collected 212 pairs between 1877 and 1889. — [NZ Birds Online](https://www.nzbirdsonline.org.nz/species/huia)
  - Wearing huia feathers "was reserved for leaders and people of great mana" (aggregated search result; secondary). — [Science Learning Hub](https://www.sciencelearn.org.nz/resources/3279-huia-the-bird-of-the-century)
- **Closest living models:**
  - North Island kōkako: rich, sonorous, organ-like duets, "the longest known duetting of any songbird", sung at dawn from treetops, with a "took" contact call. — [NZ Birds Online: NI kōkako](https://www.nzbirdsonline.org.nz/species/north-island-kokako)
  - Tīeke (saddleback): chattering "cheet te-te-te-te"; male rhythmic songs with more than 200 song types; soft flute-like calls that differ by sex. — [NZ Birds Online: NI saddleback](https://www.nzbirdsonline.org.nz/species/north-island-saddleback)

### Inferences
- **Synthesis model:** an antiphonal pair.
  - Male: pure-tone, lightly vibrato-free whistle with a descending contour.
  - Female: a three-note up-down figure, answering after a short gap. Place her at a slightly different position or height, per Batley's "climbs to the top of the tree".
  - Timbre: kōkako and tīeke flute-like tones, not a human-lip whistle character.
  - Absolute pitch and duration can only be measured from the Hāmana recording. Treating it as a *reference for contour and rhythm* (not sampling) fits the artwork's "never sample" rule, but see the cultural note below.
- **Season and time:** dawn-weighted calling, breeding September–February. Density: the 34k–89k birds are spread over North Island forest, so modest density per km² in the southern and eastern ranges.
- **Last-known-place "trace":** Tararua Ranges (~-40.9, 175.4, approximate). A second trace could sit at Moawhango (~-39.58, 175.87) and Wellington (~-41.29, 174.78) for the human-imitation lineage.
- **Cultural protocol:** a synthesized huia call derived from Hāmana's lure call should be treated as taonga. Recommended before release:
  - Contact Ngā Taonga about rights (the catalogue notes that online content "may not be available for supply").
  - Seek guidance from Mōkai Pātea / Moawhango-area iwi and hapū associated with Hāmana.
  - Consider whether a publicly triggered "rare trace" is appropriate at all.

### Gaps
- **"Manakau 1954" recording:** I found no source for the brief's mention of a 1954 recording at Manakau. All sources found say 1948/1949, Wellington studio. This may be a conflation; verify with Ngā Taonga.
- **Hāmana's iwi and hapū affiliation:** not stated in the sources I could access.
- **Buller's primary text:** I could not read Buller's own descriptions (1873/1888) on huia voice beyond NZ Birds Online's paraphrase. Te Ara was blocked (403).
- **Other pūoro or waiata imitations:** no specific source found.
- **Measured frequencies:** none published that I found.

## 3. Laughing owl / whēkau (Ninox albifacies, formerly Sceloglaux)

### Takeaway
A large nocturnal owl, roughly twice the size of the ruru (morepork). It is richly DESCRIBED as sounding like:
- "dismal shrieks"
- "cack-cack-cack" on rainy nights
- a barking "just like the barking of a young dog"
- men "cooeying"
- a "melancholy hooting"

Males were harsher and louder than females, and calls varied with season. Last specimen: Bluecliffs Station, South Canterbury, 5 July 1914.

### Cited Findings
- **Names:** whēkau (also hakoke). *Ninox albifacies* (G.R. Gray, 1844). Size 40–45 cm, about 600 g, roughly twice a morepork. — [NZ Birds Online: Laughing owl](https://www.nzbirdsonline.org.nz/species/laughing-owl)
- **Subspecies:** South Island *albifacies*, North Island *rufifacies* (smaller). — [Wikipedia: Laughing owl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laughing_owl). NZTCS 2021 lists both subspecies as Extinct (as *Sceloglaux albifacies*). — [DOC NZTCS 36](https://www.doc.govt.nz/globalassets/documents/science-and-technical/nztcs36entire.pdf)
- **Range:**
  - Formerly throughout NZ. By European arrival, restricted to south of a Taranaki–East Cape line in the North Island, plus Nelson, Marlborough, the eastern South Island and Stewart Island.
  - Open country and forest. Roosted in rock caves, fissures and ledges.
  - Common in the South Island in the mid-1800s. — [NZ Birds Online](https://www.nzbirdsonline.org.nz/species/laughing-owl)
- **Last record:** specimen found dead at Bluecliffs Station, Canterbury, 5 July 1914. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laughing_owl); "collected in 1914 from South Canterbury"; extinct by about 1940. — [NZ Birds Online](https://www.nzbirdsonline.org.nz/species/laughing-owl)
- **Unconfirmed later reports:**
  - Waikaremoana, 1925–26
  - Ōpōtiki area, 1940s
  - Saddle Hill, Fiordland, Feb 1956: "a most unusual weird cry which might almost be described as maniacal"
  - Canterbury egg fragments, 1960. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laughing_owl)
- **Voice (DESCRIBED):**
  - "loud cry made up of a series of dismal shrieks frequently repeated"
  - "prolonged cack-cack-cack", reportedly repeated incessantly on rainy nights
  - barking like "the yelping of a young dog". — [NZ Birds Online](https://www.nzbirdsonline.org.nz/species/laughing-owl)
  - "Precisely the same as two men 'cooeying' to each other from a distance"
  - "A melancholy hooting note"
  - high-pitched chattering when airborne, typically on dark or drizzly nights or before rain
  - whistling, chuckling and mewing notes from captive birds
  - one correspondent claimed the owls were attracted by accordion playing. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laughing_owl)
- **Potts (1870)** described "doleful shrieks" similar to, but louder and more strident than, a call of Cook's petrel. He had "never been able to trace the slightest approach to mirthful sound". **Smith (in Buller 1883):** calls varied seasonally, and the male voice was harsher and louder than the female's. — [NZ Geographic "Laughter in the night" via search summary](https://www.nzgeo.com/stories/laughter-in-the-night/); [Notornis 19(1)](https://www.birdsnz.org.nz/wp-content/uploads/2011/03/Notornis_19_1_4-19.pdf)
- **Breeding:** 2 white eggs laid September–October; chicks fledged October–November. Nests in ground cavities in caves and rock shelters. — [NZ Birds Online](https://www.nzbirdsonline.org.nz/species/laughing-owl)
- **Diet:** birds, frogs, bats, lizards, juvenile tuatara, insects, and later kiore (Pacific rat). — [NZ Birds Online](https://www.nzbirdsonline.org.nz/species/laughing-owl)

### Inferences
- **Synthesis model:**
  - Nocturnal, near-cliff or limestone sources.
  - Repeated harsh shriek series: louder and more strident than a Cook's petrel call, so a broadband, noisy, higher-pitched "scream" element.
  - A rapid "cack-cack" chatter, gated to rain and drizzle conditions (a nice weather coupling for the artwork).
  - Occasional dog-like double yelps.
  - Rare antiphonal "cooee"-like exchanges between two distant birds.
  - Male rougher and louder than female.
  - Peak season late winter and spring (egg-laying September–October).
- **Models:** ruru/morepork for owl timbre (a relative in *Ninox*). Cook's petrel for shriek quality (per Potts).
- **Last-known trace:** Bluecliffs, South Canterbury (~-44.4, 171.0, approximate). Fiordland Saddle Hill 1956 could be a fainter "unconfirmed" trace.

### Gaps
- No frequencies or durations are published that I found.
- NZ Geographic and Te Ara were blocked. The Potts and Smith quotes come via search summaries, not the primary texts.
- No Māori-authored source on whēkau lore was found.

## 4. South Island kōkako (Callaeas cinerea): extinct, then "not heard since"

### Takeaway
It was declared extinct in 2008 and reclassified Data Deficient in 2013 after an accepted 2007 Reefton sighting. It remains Data Deficient in NZTCS 2021; the panel considers it "at least functionally extinct". IUCN lists it as Critically Endangered (Possibly Extinct). Its voice was "organ- and flute-like" duets, like its northern relative. A 2021 Heaphy Track audio candidate exists.

### Cited Findings
- **Status history:**
  - Extinct (2008), then Data Deficient (2013) after the 2007 sighting near Reefton. — [NZ Birds Online: SI kōkako](https://www.nzbirdsonline.org.nz/species/south-island-kokako)
  - NZTCS 2021, Data Deficient: "very high likelihood that [it is] at least functionally extinct, but it is difficult to declare them extinct due to the remote habitats"; last record accepted by the BNZ/OSNZ Records Appraisal Committee was 2007. — [DOC NZTCS 36 (Robertson et al. 2021)](https://www.doc.govt.nz/globalassets/documents/science-and-technical/nztcs36entire.pdf)
  - IUCN: Critically Endangered (Possibly Extinct), 2016. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Island_k%C5%8Dkako)
- **Range:**
  - West Coast from NW Nelson to Fiordland, Stewart Island, Banks Peninsula, the Catlins.
  - Formerly throughout the South Island before forest burning. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Island_k%C5%8Dkako)
  - Beech and mixed podocarp forests. "Very rare by the late 1800s". Last confirmed 20th-century sighting: Mt Aspiring NP, 1967. — [NZ Birds Online](https://www.nzbirdsonline.org.nz/species/south-island-kokako)
- **Voice (DESCRIBED):**
  - "slow, organ-like notes sung…usually as a duet"; kōkako song is among the slowest of songbirds. — [NZ Birds Online](https://www.nzbirdsonline.org.nz/species/south-island-kokako)
  - Explorer Charlie Douglas: "very few, but the sweetest and most mellow toned". — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Island_k%C5%8Dkako)
- **2021 candidate:** "distinct couple of soft long notes", Heaphy Track, 29 Dec 2021. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Island_k%C5%8Dkako)
- **Search:**
  - The South Island Kōkako Charitable Trust (founded 2010) runs a public search launched in January 2017, with more than 430 reports by February 2024.
  - It offers a $10,000 reward for verified photo or video evidence. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Island_k%C5%8Dkako); [Rewild](https://www.rewild.org/blog/wanted-bird-with-an-orange-wattle-extraordinary-call-and-aura-of-mystery)
- **Living model, North Island kōkako (RECORDED widely):**
  - Both sexes sing "rich, sonorous, sustained, organ-like notes", about a 30-second sequence, often in duet.
  - Females sing less in duets and at higher pitch than males.
  - Strong regional dialects. — [NZ Birds Online: NI kōkako](https://www.nzbirdsonline.org.nz/species/north-island-kokako); [search summary of Valderrama/Waikato research](https://researchcommons.waikato.ac.nz/bitstreams/c31ae2c9-12b8-4385-919f-342a70ca5627/download)

### Inferences
- **Synthesis model:** slow sustained pure tones with organ-pipe timbre, long inter-note gaps, male and female in antiphony (female higher). Dawn-weighted. Low density across West Coast and Fiordland beech and podocarp forest.
- **Traces:**
  - Mt Aspiring 1967 (~-44.4, 168.7, approximate)
  - Reefton 2007 (~-42.1, 171.9, approximate)
  - Heaphy Track 2021 (~-41.0, 172.3, approximate)
- Because the species may persist, "secret traces" should use coarse locations, as with the night parrot.

### Gaps
- No verified South Island-specific acoustic measurements were found.
- No accessible information on whether South Island song differed from northern dialects.

## 5. Piopio: North Island (Turnagra tanagra) and South Island (T. capensis)

### Takeaway
New Zealand's "sweetest" songbirds, now extinct. The song had five distinct bars, each repeated 6–7 times, with mimicry (yellowhead, Australian magpie), a sharp repeated whistle ("piopio"), and a purring "chur-r-r" threat call. Singing peaked in the early morning and evening.

### Cited Findings
- **North Island piopio:**
  - Names: piopio-kata, tiotio-kata, korohea.
  - Range: Far North to Gisborne and Wairarapa; strongholds in today's Whanganui NP and Hauhungaroa.
  - Rarest endemic by 1888. Last confirmed: shot at Ohura, February 1902.
  - Call: "short, sharp, whistling cry, quickly repeated". The song comprised five distinct bars, each repeated 6–7 times. — [NZ Birds Online: NI piopio](https://www.nzbirdsonline.org.nz/species/north-island-piopio)
- **South Island piopio:**
  - Throughout the South Island, Fiordland islands and Stephens Island (as a separate subspecies, *T. c. minor*). Abundant on the West Coast through the 1880s, declining after 1870.
  - Last: the page cites "the final confirmed sighting in west Otago in 1963", with unconfirmed reports into the 1930s. The sequence is odd as extracted; verify.
  - Voice:
    - "The sweetest of New Zealand songbirds, comprising five distinct bars, repeated six or seven times"
    - "Powerful, melodic song with tuneful phrasing and some mimicry", including yellowhead and Australian magpie
    - "Long purring *chur-r-r*" nest-threat call
    - song mainly early morning and early evening
    - birds spread their tails and lowered their wings while singing
  - Breeding: laying seen in late December. — [NZ Birds Online: SI piopio](https://www.nzbirdsonline.org.nz/species/south-island-piopio)
- **Status:** NZTCS 2021 lists *T. tanagra*, *T. c. capensis* and *T. c. minor* as Extinct. — [DOC NZTCS 36](https://www.doc.govt.nz/globalassets/documents/science-and-technical/nztcs36entire.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Synthesis model:** a structured song of 5 motifs × 6–7 repeats. This is a strong, rhythmically legible pattern generator (like a thrush). Add mimic phrases borrowed from local living species, plus sharp, repeated "pio" whistles.
- **Timing:** crepuscular (morning and evening). The magpie mimicry is itself a colonial-era marker, since magpies were introduced; a poignant detail.
- **Traces:** Ohura 1902 (~-38.85, 174.98, approximate); west Otago (coarse).

### Gaps
- There is an internal inconsistency in the extracted South Island last-record text (1930s vs 1963). Verify on NZ Birds Online or in Miskelly's papers.
- No frequencies found.

## 6. Smaller NZ, Chatham and subantarctic losses

### Takeaway
These are mostly thin DESCRIBED voices. They are good candidates for quiet, sparse "texture" layers rather than signature calls.

### Cited Findings
- **Bush wren / mātuhituhi (*Xenicus longipes*: *stokesii* NI, *longipes* SI, *variabilis* Stead's):**
  - Voice: "a subdued trill, faint rasp or loud 'seep', sometimes rapidly repeated", "a loud cheep when alarmed", and pairs in contact through "continuous soft calls".
  - Last records: North Island 1955 (Lake Waikaremoana); South Island 1968 (Moss Pass); Stewart Island 1951; final accepted sighting on Kaimohu Island in 1972, after the 1964 rescue translocation when ship rats reached Big South Cape. — [NZ Birds Online: Bush wren](https://www.nzbirdsonline.org.nz/species/bush-wren)
  - All three subspecies are Extinct in NZTCS 2021. — [DOC NZTCS 36](https://www.doc.govt.nz/globalassets/documents/science-and-technical/nztcs36entire.pdf)
- **Lyall's / Stephens Island wren (*Traversia lyalli*):**
  - A flightless passerine. Once in both main islands (subfossil); in historic times only on Stephens Island (Takapourewa).
  - "Almost nocturnal, running around the rocks like a mouse."
  - Roughly 16–18 specimens (1894–1905). Extinct by c. 1895–97.
  - Galbreath & Brown (2004) show a feral-cat population, not the single cat "Tibbles", caused it.
  - **No voice recorded or described.** — [NZ Birds Online](https://www.nzbirdsonline.org.nz/species/lyalls-wren); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyall%27s_wren)
- **NZ quail / koreke (*Coturnix novaezelandiae*):**
  - North and South Islands, most abundant east of the Southern Alps. Lived in coveys; daily bags of 40+ were reported.
  - Presumed extinct by 1875. Call per early hunters: "*twit, twit, twit, twee-twit*". — [NZ Birds Online: NZ quail](https://www.nzbirdsonline.org.nz/species/new-zealand-quail)
- **Chatham Island bellbird (*Anthornis melanocephala*):**
  - Chatham, Pitt, Mangere and Little Mangere islands. Last record: Little Mangere, 1906.
  - "ringing notes without grunts or wheezes", comparable to mainland bellbirds but louder. Observers disagreed on whether it was less musical or richer and fuller.
  - Breeding August–October. — [NZ Birds Online: Chatham Island bellbird](https://www.nzbirdsonline.org.nz/species/chatham-island-bellbird)
- **Chatham Island fernbird (*Poodytes / Bowdleria rufescens*):**
  - Alive only on Mangere Island; last seen January 1895.
  - Had the "typical" fernbird call plus "a peculiar whistle, very like that which a man would use" to attract attention at a distance. — [NZ Birds Online](https://www.nzbirdsonline.org.nz/species/chatham-island-fernbird)
- **Chatham Island rail / mātirakahu (*Cabalus modestus*):**
  - Mangere Island; last specimens 1893, extinct by 1894 after cats arrived.
  - Only known voice: a captive young bird's "plaintive cry" that attracted its mother. — [NZ Birds Online](https://www.nzbirdsonline.org.nz/species/chatham-island-rail)
- **Auckland Island merganser / miuweka (*Mergus australis*):**
  - Auckland Islands; last specimen January 1902. Pairs were probably territorial year-round in inlets and streams.
  - Voice: only "quacking like a duck". A separate Chatham species, *M. milleneri*, is known from bones. — [NZ Birds Online](https://www.nzbirdsonline.org.nz/species/auckland-island-merganser)
- **NZ little bittern / kaoriki (*Ixobrychus novaezelandiae*):**
  - West Coast (also Tauranga, Lake Wakatipu). Last definite records mid-1890s.
  - Wild birds not documented calling. A captive bird in distress made "a peculiar snapping cry" and "a cry not unlike that of a kingfisher, but not so loud". — [NZ Birds Online](https://www.nzbirdsonline.org.nz/species/new-zealand-little-bittern)

### Inferences
- **Bush wren:** high, thin "seep" and trill sounds near the ground, very sparse. Rifleman (tītitipounamu, a living acanthisittid) is the natural timbre model.
- **Chatham bellbird:** synthesize from the korimako (NZ bellbird) model, louder and without the grunt/wheeze elements.
- **Quail:** a "twit-twit-twit-twee-twit" rhythm from grassland coveys, at high historical density east of the Alps (40+ bagged per day implies abundance).
- **Chatham fernbird:** a human-like attention whistle, good for an eerie rare trace on Mangere (~-44.27, -176.3, approximate).
- **Lyall's wren:** silence, or the faintest rustle on rock. The absence is the honest representation.
- **Approximate traces:**
  - Kaimohu / Big South Cape (~-47.2, 167.4)
  - Little Mangere (~-44.28, -176.3)
  - Carnley Harbour, Auckland Islands (~-50.8, 166.0)
  - Stephens Island (~-40.67, 174.0)

### Gaps
- No Moriori names or Chatham Islands oral sources were located.
- No sources on the Chatham Island duck or other Chatham extinctions' voices.

## 7. Borderline pre-1500 NZ: moa, Haast's eagle, owlet-nightjar, Hodgens' waterhen

### Takeaway
All of these are INFERRED. The strongest anatomical clue is the looped trachea of up to 1 m in *Euryapteryx* and *Emeus* moa, which implies deep, resonant, far-carrying calls. Haast's eagle survives as a Māori name (hōkioi) tied to a cry "Hōkioi! Hōkioi! Hūūū". That cry may in fact describe the aerial display sound of the snipe (hakawai).

### Cited Findings
- **Moa:**
  - Nine species in six genera. "Euryapteryx and Emeus … trachea up to 1 metre long formed a large loop within the body cavity", associated with "deep resonant vocalisations that can travel long distances". These are the only ratites known with this feature.
  - All moa extinct by about 1445 CE, within about 100 years of human settlement (Perry et al. 2014).
  - Population at settlement: estimates of 58,000 to 2.5 million.
  - Whakataukī: "kua ngaro i te ngaro o te moa" ("lost as the moa is lost"). — [Wikipedia: Moa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moa)
  - Moa species (e.g. *Dinornis robustus*, *Emeus crassus*, *Euryapteryx curtus*) are listed Extinct in NZTCS 2021. — [DOC NZTCS 36](https://www.doc.govt.nz/globalassets/documents/science-and-technical/nztcs36entire.pdf)
- **Haast's eagle / pouakai, hōkioi (*Aquila moorei*, often *Hieraaetus moorei*):**
  - South Island, mostly east of the Southern Alps. Females up to 17.8 kg, wingspan up to 3 m.
  - Extinct about 500–600 years ago with the moa. Preyed on moa.
  - NZ Birds Online offers a *simulated* call. — [NZ Birds Online](https://www.nzbirdsonline.org.nz/species/haasts-eagle)
  - A Māori story, "Kahū and Hōkioi", gives the cry "hokioi–hokioi–hu–u". — [TKI Junior Journal "Kahū and Hōkioi" (Ministry of Education)](https://instructionalseries.tki.org.nz/content/download/36273/408436/file/Kahū%20and%20Hōkioi-JJ51%20L2%202015.pdf)
  - The attribution of the nocturnal "hakawai" sound to the snipe's display flight ("queeyoo queeyoo…" followed by a "roar" of air through feathers) comes from a secondary search summary; weaker sourcing. — [kiwifoto: NZ snipe](https://www.kiwifoto.com/galleries/birds/new_zealand_snipe)
- **NZ owlet-nightjar (*Aegotheles novaezealandiae*):**
  - North and South Islands; 150–200 g, the largest aegothelid; nearly flightless ground forager; nested in caves.
  - Extinct around the late 13th–14th centuries, attributed to kiore (Pacific rat). No voice data. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_owlet-nightjar)
  - Listed Extinct in NZTCS 2021. — [DOC NZTCS 36](https://www.doc.govt.nz/globalassets/documents/science-and-technical/nztcs36entire.pdf)
- **Hodgens' waterhen (*Gallinula hodgenorum*):** listed Extinct in NZTCS 2021. — [DOC NZTCS 36](https://www.doc.govt.nz/globalassets/documents/science-and-technical/nztcs36entire.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Moa (*Emeus*, *Euryapteryx*):**
  - A tube of about 1 m suggests very low formant or resonance energy, roughly tens of Hz to about 100 Hz if modelled as a simple quarter-wave resonator (≈ c/4L ≈ 86 Hz for L = 1 m). This is my back-of-envelope estimate, not a published figure.
  - The emu is the closest *living* big-ratite sound model. Emu calls are reportedly all below 300 Hz, with female booming at 60–80 Hz audible to about 2 km. The source is low quality ([search summary](https://www.anthropology-news.org/articles/close-encounters-of-the-bird-kind/)); verify against primary emu bioacoustics.
  - Kiwi, tinamou and cassowary could be references for other moa genera (*Dinornis*, *Anomalopteryx*) that lacked the loop.
- **Moa for the artwork:** sub-audible booms and rumbles, sparse, across the lowland shrubland and forest mosaic, at very high historical density if the upper estimate is used. The "~1400–1445" horizon should be flagged as pre-contact memory.
- **Haast's eagle:** use the whakapapa of the name (hōkioi) with care, or render as silence plus wingbeat. The only "voice" is legend and a modern simulation.
- **Owlet-nightjar:** if sounded at all, model on Australian owlet-nightjar churring as INFERRED only (no source gathered).

### Gaps
- Worthy & Holdaway primary texts on moa trachea were not accessed; search budget exhausted.
- I found no published acoustic modelling of moa calls.
- Hodgens' waterhen dates and range were not retrieved (Wikipedia page 404).
- No Māori-authored scholarly source on pouakai or hōkioi was accessed (Te Ara blocked).

## 8. Greater short-tailed bat (Mystacina robusta): the ultrasonic "not heard since"

### Takeaway
Last confirmed in 1965 (a 1967 sighting is also reported) at Big South Cape (Taukihepa) after the 1963–64 ship-rat invasion. It remains Data Deficient because of unconfirmed "mystacinid-like" echolocation calls recorded in 1999. It is a strong candidate for an ultrasonic "secret trace".

### Cited Findings
- Once widespread in NZ until kiore arrived. In the 20th century, known only from Taukihepa/Big South Cape and Rerewhakaupoko/Solomon islands, including "several hundred" in a cave at Puwai. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_greater_short-tailed_bat); [search summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_greater_short-tailed_bat)
- Last confirmed sighting: 1965 (Don Merton photo, Puwai Bay). Some sources give 1967. Rats invaded in 1963. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_greater_short-tailed_bat)
- 1999: "unusual 'mystacinid-like' echolocation calls" on Putauhina Island. Status: IUCN Critically Endangered (possibly extinct); NZTCS Data Deficient. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_greater_short-tailed_bat)
- Edgar Stead observed them flying about 10 ft above the ground only after dusk, and crawling on cave floors. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_greater_short-tailed_bat)

### Inferences
- **Model:** lesser short-tailed bat (*M. tuberculata*) echolocation, pitch-shifted down for the larger body. Render as heterodyned or time-expanded clicks, since the real sound is ultrasonic.
- **Trace:** Big South Cape (~-47.23, 167.4, approximate), after dusk.

### Gaps
- Echolocation frequencies of *M. robusta* or *M. tuberculata* were not retrieved (search budget exhausted).

## 9. Thylacine / kaparunina (Thylacinus cynocephalus)

### Takeaway
There is no sound recording. Described vocalisations:
- a "terrier-like double yap, repeated every few seconds" when hunting
- husky, coughing barks when anxious
- growl and hiss with a threat-yawn
- a long whining cry for contact at a distance
- low snuffling between family members

It was nocturnal and crepuscular.
- Last wild kill: Mawbanna, 1930.
- Last captive: Beaumaris/Hobart Zoo, 7 Sept 1936.
- Declared extinct: IUCN 1982, Tasmania 1986.

### Cited Findings
- **Vocalisations:**
  - "When anxious or excited made a series of husky, coughing barks. When hunting, they gave a distinctive terrier-like double yap, repeated every few seconds." — [Tasmania Parks & Wildlife](https://parks.tas.gov.au/discovery-and-learning/wildlife/tasmanian-tiger)
  - "growl and hiss when agitated, often accompanied by a threat-yawn"
  - "series of rapidly repeated guttural cough-like barks (described as 'yip-yap', 'cay-yip' or 'hop-hop-hop'), probably for communication between the family pack members"
  - "long whining cry, probably for identification at distance"
  - "low snuffling noise used for communication between family members". — [Wikipedia: Thylacine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thylacine)
- **Activity:** "nocturnal and crepuscular hunter"; spent days in caves or hollow logs. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thylacine)
- **Range and abundance:** pre-European Tasmanian estimates of about 2,000–4,000 (or up to 5,000). Preferred drier open forest and woodland in the Midlands, East Coast, Central Highlands and North West. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thylacine)
- **Last records:**
  - Last known wild kill by Wilf Batty, Mawbanna (NW Tasmania), 1930.
  - Last captive died the night of 7 Sept 1936, Hobart (Beaumaris) Zoo.
  - Protection was enacted 10 July 1936. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thylacine)
  - Tasmania Parks also lists "1933: last wild individual captured". — [Tas Parks](https://parks.tas.gov.au/discovery-and-learning/wildlife/tasmanian-tiger)
- **Status:** IUCN extinct 1982; Tasmania 1986. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thylacine)
- **Contested persistence:** Brook et al. (2023) modelled possible persistence into the late 1990s–2000s. This is contested. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thylacine)
- **Aboriginal names:** coorinna, kanunnah, cab-berr-one-nen-er, loarinna, laoonana, can-nen-ner, lagunta. *kaparunina* in palawa kani. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thylacine)
- **Film:** Fleay's December 1933 Beaumaris footage is silent. Sidney Cook's 1935 *Tasmania the Wonderland* includes the last captive animal. — [NFSA](https://www.nfsa.gov.au/latest/tasmanian-tiger-last-known-footage-thylacine); [NFSA colourised footage](https://www.nfsa.gov.au/latest/colourised-footage-last-tasmanian-tiger)
- **Palawa archaeology resources:** [Recently Extinct Species: Thylacines in Palawa archaeology](https://recentlyextinctspecies.com/thylacine-archive/thylacines-palawa-tasmanian-aboriginal-archaeology)

### Inferences
- **Synthesis model:**
  - Hunting pairs or family groups trade a nasal double yap ("cay-yip") every few seconds while moving at night.
  - Rare long whines at distance.
  - Close-range snuffles.
  - Hiss and growl only in the rare "encounter" trace.
- **Timbre models:** Tasmanian devil or quolls (fellow dasyuromorphs) for throat texture, and a small terrier or fox for the yap. These are INFERRED choices; no source compares them acoustically.
- **Density:** about 2,000–5,000 animals across Tasmania means very sparse. A given listener point should hear one only rarely.
- **Traces:** Mawbanna (~-40.95, 145.35, approximate) and Beaumaris Zoo, Hobart (~-42.87, 147.33, approximate), with the zoo trace as a captive, not wild, sound (pacing and hiss).
- **Cultural note:** palawa kani is a revived language managed by the Tasmanian Aboriginal community. I believe the Tasmanian Aboriginal Centre asserts control over its use, but I have not verified this. Seek permission before displaying "kaparunina".

### Gaps
- No acoustic measurements exist.
- I could not verify whether the 1935 film's soundtrack contains any ambient sound from the enclosure.
- No primary palawa source on thylacine sound or lore was found.

## 10. Australian mammals: the silent majority (Woinarski et al. 2015 context)

### Takeaway
Almost all lost Australian mammals are INFERRED. The only DESCRIBED sounds found:
- lesser bilby: "harsh hissing" when handled
- Maclear's rat (Christmas Island): "querulous squeaks" by the thousand at night
- thylacine (above)

For the artwork, most of these species are better rendered as movement sounds (hopping cadence, digging, rustling) at historically high densities than as voices.

### Cited Findings
- **Lesser bilby / yallara (*Macrotis leucura*):**
  - NT names: *Urpila* (lesser) vs *Urgata* (greater).
  - Gibson and Great Sandy deserts, NE South Australia, SE Northern Territory; spinifex sandplains and dunes.
  - Finlayson collected 12 live animals near Cooncherie Station, 1931. Last evidence: a skull below a wedge-tailed eagle nest at Steele Gap, Simpson Desert, 1967. Oral tradition suggests survival into the 1960s.
  - Voice: "harsh hissing sounds" when handled; "repeated savage snapping bites". — [Wikipedia: Lesser bilby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lesser_bilby)
- **Pig-footed bandicoots (*Chaeropus ecaudatus*, *C. yirratji*):**
  - Names: landwang, tubaija; Noongar boda, woda, boodal; *yirratji* is itself a local Aboriginal name.
  - Last specimen 1901. Aboriginal reports indicate *C. yirratji* survived to the 1950s in the Gibson and Great Sandy deserts.
  - Described as "capable of running with considerable speed by breaking into a smooth, galloping sprint". — [Wikipedia: Chaeropus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaeropus); [Australian Museum blog](https://australian.museum/learn/news/blog/a-new-type-of-pig-footed-bandicoot/)
  - Burbidge et al. (2008) treat 1920s sightings as unconfirmed; Pintupi accounts give c. 1958. — [search summary / recentlyextinctspecies](https://recentlyextinctspecies.com/peramelemorphia/chaeropus-yirratji)
- **Desert rat-kangaroo / oolacunta (*Caloprymnus campestris*):**
  - Clay pans, gibber and sand ridges east of Lake Eyre. "Never abundant".
  - Rediscovered by Finlayson in 1931. Last confirmed 1935 near Ooroowilanie, east of Lake Eyre. Declared extinct 1994.
  - Hopping with the right foot landing in front of the left.
  - Grass-lined ground nest covered with twigs; solitary; partly nocturnal. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desert_rat-kangaroo)
- **Toolache wallaby (*Notamacropus greyi*):**
  - Name possibly from Ngarrindjeri *rtulatji*.
  - SE South Australia and W Victoria grasslands and swamps. Social, grazing in groups. Crepuscular and nocturnal. Extremely fast.
  - Last wild 1924; last captive died 1939. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toolache_wallaby)
- **Crescent nailtail wallaby (*Onychogalea lunata*):**
  - Names: waurong/worong (Nyungar), tjawalpa, warlpartu, yiwutta (Arrernte), unkalda, towala (Anangu).
  - Last report by Finlayson in 1956 between the Jervois and Tarlton Ranges, NT, though reliably unrecorded since the 1940s. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crescent_nail-tail_wallaby)
- **Broad-faced potoroo / moda (*Potorous platyops*):** SW WA (subfossils to the Nullarbor and Kangaroo Island). Last live capture 1874 (William Webb). No sound data. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broad-faced_potoroo)
- **Nullarbor dwarf bettong (*Bettongia pusilla*):** known only from cave bones (Koonalda, Weekes caves). Assumed to have gone extinct around colonisation. No dates or sound. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nullarbor_dwarf_bettong)
- **Christmas Island rats:**
  - Maclear's rat (*Rattus macleari*) was abundant and fearless. At night it "emerged and foraged in all directions … making querulous squeaks", running over sleeping expedition members. Last recorded 1903; a trypanosome from black rats is suspected.
  - Bulldog rat (*R. nativitatis*): last recorded 1897–98. — [Wikipedia: Maclear's rat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maclear%27s_rat); [Wikipedia list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_extinct_animals_of_Australia)
- **Bramble Cay melomys / Maizab Kaur Mukeis (*Melomys rubicola*):**
  - Bramble Cay (9.14°S, 143.87°E), 340 × 150 m, about 2 ha habitable.
  - Numbers: 1845 so plentiful that crew shot them with bows and arrows; "hundreds" in 1978; about 90 in 1998; 10–12 captured in 2002–04.
  - Last seen 2009 by fishermen; none in 2011 or 2014 surveys.
  - Declared extinct: IUCN 2015, Queensland 2016, Australian Government February 2019.
  - The Meriam name *Maizab Kaur Mukeis* comes from Erub. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bramble_Cay_melomys); [National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/first-mammal-extinct-climate-change-bramble-cay-melomys)
  - Cause: repeated ocean inundation, the "first recorded mammalian extinction due to anthropogenic climate change". — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bramble_Cay_melomys)
- **Other last-record dates (Wikipedia list):**
  - eastern hare-wallaby: 1889
  - desert bandicoot: 1943
  - lesser stick-nest rat: 1933
  - white-footed rabbit-rat: 1860–62
  - short-tailed hopping mouse: 1896 (Charlotte Waters)
  - big-eared hopping mouse: 1843
  - Darling Downs hopping mouse: 1846
  - Percy Island flying fox: 1874
  - — [Wikipedia: List of extinct animals of Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_extinct_animals_of_Australia)
- Woinarski et al. 2015, PNAS, "Ongoing unraveling of a continental fauna" (URL, not accessible in this session). — [PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1417301112)

### Inferences
- **Sound design by proxy:**
  - Bilby: sniffing, digging, hiss.
  - Pig-footed bandicoot: light galloping patter.
  - Desert rat-kangaroo: fast, irregular hop cadence on gibber, with a distinctive asymmetric footfall.
  - Toolache: fast group thumping over grassland at dusk.
  - Maclear's rat: dense nocturnal squeak chorus across Christmas Island rainforest. One of the few "abundance" soundscapes with a written anchor.
  - Bramble Cay melomys: tiny rustles among the grass of a cay, drowned by surf. An apt climate-loss image.
- **Approximate traces:**
  - Ooroowilanie (~-28.2, 138.6)
  - Steele Gap, Simpson Desert (coarse)
  - Jervois Range (~-22.6, 136.1)
  - Bramble Cay (-9.14, 143.87, sourced)
  - Christmas Island (~-10.49, 105.62)

### Gaps
- No verified EPBC listing status for each mammal was retrieved (FrogID confirms EPBC "Extinct" for the frogs only).
- No Woinarski table accessed.
- No voice descriptions found for toolache, crescent nailtail, pig-footed bandicoot, desert rat-kangaroo, potoroos or hopping mice.
- Aboriginal accounts of sounds (e.g. in Burbidge et al. 1988 ethnographic surveys) were not accessed.

## 11. Australian island emus, paradise parrot, Roper River scrub-robin

### Takeaway
None have voice descriptions in the sources found. Model the emus on the living emu (drumming and booming, low frequency). Model the paradise parrot on its living *Psephotellus* relatives. The Roper River scrub-robin may never have existed as a distinct taxon.

### Cited Findings
- **King Island emu (*Dromaius novaehollandiae minor*):**
  - About 87 cm tall, 20–23 kg.
  - Péron's sealer interview: solitary, but in flocks of 10–20 at breeding time; foraged mainly morning and evening; liked lagoon shade and shorelines.
  - Péron in 1802 said the island "swarmed" with emus. Wild population gone by 1805; two captives in Paris (Jardin des Plantes) died in 1822.
  - No voice recorded. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Island_emu)
- **Kangaroo Island emu (*D. n. baudinianus*):** "quite common around Nepean Bay" (Flinders 1802). Extinct "by about 1827" (Wikipedia) versus last recorded 1819 (Wikipedia list), a conflict. Hunting and burning are blamed. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kangaroo_Island_emu); [List](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_extinct_animals_of_Australia)
- **Living emu model:** vocalisations reportedly all below 300 Hz, with female booming at 60–80 Hz audible to about 2 km, produced via a tracheal slit and gular sac. The sources in the search were low quality. — [Anthropology News (via search)](https://www.anthropology-news.org/articles/close-encounters-of-the-bird-kind/)
- **Paradise parrot (*Psephotellus pulcherrimus*):**
  - Grassy woodlands of the Qld–NSW border region, Condamine to Mitchell rivers. Nested in termite mounds. Ate grass seed on the ground.
  - A 1902 drought and changed burning drove decline.
  - Last confirmed sighting: 14 Sept 1927, Burnett River (Wikipedia); 13 Nov 1927 by Cyril Jerrard (search summary); or November 1928 (Olsen 2007). Sources conflict.
  - No voice description was found. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradise_parrot); [Recently Extinct Species](https://recentlyextinctspecies.com/psittaciformes-cockatoos-and-parrots/psephotellus-pulcherrimus); [Chisholm & the Paradise Parrot, Historical Records of Australian Science](https://publish.csiro.au/hr/Fulltext/HR20019)
- **Roper River scrub-robin (*Drymodes superciliaris colcloughi*):** two skins from the 1910 H.L. White expedition (Roper area, 15 July–22 Nov 1910). Provenance is suspected to be Cape York. Taxon validity is disputed (Bennett 1983 for, Schodde & Mason 1999 against). — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roper_River_scrub_robin)

### Inferences
- **Emus:** island dwarfs; synthesize a smaller-bodied emu drum (slightly higher resonance than mainland), peaking at dawn and dusk (morning and evening foraging). Breeding-flock choruses at King Island.
- **Paradise parrot:** use golden-shouldered and hooded parrot calls as INFERRED timbre models. Place the trace at the Burnett River, Qld (~-25.6, 151.6, approximate).
- **Roper River scrub-robin:** treat as a "ghost taxon". If used, model on the living northern scrub-robin and flag it as uncertain.

### Gaps
- Chisholm's and Jerrard's descriptions of the paradise parrot voice were not accessed; the CSIRO article is likely to contain them.
- No primary emu bioacoustics paper was verified.

## 12. Frogs: the recorded dead (gastric-brooders) and the chytrid silence

### Takeaway
Both gastric-brooding frogs were *recorded* by Keith McDonald and called in October–November from rock crevices by rainforest streams. Both disappeared by the mid-1980s (chytrid). The day frogs are DESCRIBED only. The sharp-snouted day frog made "metallic tinkling" and chirps. The southern day frog had no vocal sac and a quiet call. The "Mount Glorious torrent frog" and the "southern day frog" appear to be the same species, *Taudactylus diurnus*.

### Cited Findings
- **Southern gastric-brooding frog (*Rheobatrachus silus*):**
  - Conondale and Blackall ranges, SE Qld. Rocky rainforest and wet sclerophyll streams.
  - Recorded by Keith McDonald; calling October–November.
  - Last wild sighting 1981; last captive died 1983.
  - Status: Extinct under EPBC and IUCN. — [FrogID](https://www.frogid.net.au/frogs/rheobatrachus-silus/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rheobatrachus_silus)
  - Call parameters (as summarised from AmphibiaWeb in search; wording garbled, verify): an upward-inflected call of about 0.5 s, repeated every 6–7 s; pulsed (30–34 pulses, about 260–290 ms); dominant frequency about 1000 Hz with energy also at 500, 700, 1200 and 1400 Hz. Males called from rock crevices above pools. — [AmphibiaWeb R. silus](https://amphibiaweb.org/species/3543)
- **Northern gastric-brooding frog (*R. vitellinus*):**
  - Clarke Range, Eungella NP and Pelion SF near Mackay; 400–1000 m altitude; under 500 km².
  - Recorded by Keith McDonald; calling October–November.
  - Discovered 1984; gone by March 1985. FrogID says "not seen since 1986"; other sources say 1985 or 1987 (conflict).
  - Status: Extinct (EPBC, IUCN; declared 2015). — [FrogID](https://www.frogid.net.au/frogs/rheobatrachus-vitellinus/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rheobatrachus_vitellinus)
- **Sharp-snouted day frog (*Taudactylus acutirostris*):**
  - Wet Tropics from Innisfail to Big Tableland near Cooktown. Calling recorded in November, December and January.
  - Last seen 1997 (FrogID) or November 1996 (other sources).
  - First species in which *Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis* was identified. Status: Extinct (EPBC, IUCN). — [FrogID](https://frogid.net.au/frogs/taudactylus-acutirostris)
  - Two call types: "metallic tinkling, repeated a number of times in quick succession" and a "chirp". — [EDGE of Existence](https://edgeofexistence.org/species/sharp-snouted-day-frog)
- **Southern day frog (*Taudactylus diurnus*):**
  - Blackall, Conondale and D'Aguilar ranges; common in the early 1970s; last recorded 1979.
  - The male "lacked a vocal sac, which made the call quiet".
  - Status: Extinct (EPBC, IUCN). — [FrogID](https://www.frogid.net.au/frogs/taudactylus-diurnus/)

### Inferences
- **Mount Glorious:** it lies in the D'Aguilar Range, and "Mount Glorious day/torrent frog" is a known common name for *T. diurnus*. Treat these as one species. This is my identification, not stated on FrogID.
- ***R. silus* synthesis:** a pulsed, rising "croak-chirp" around 1 kHz, every 6–7 s, from stream-side rocks at night in spring. Place the chorus density along Conondale and Blackall streams (~-26.7, 152.7, approximate).
- ***R. vitellinus*:** similar, at Eungella (~-21.1, 148.6, approximate). Keep the density high, since it was abundant until 1984.
- ***T. acutirostris*:** fast metallic tinkles by torrents, day-active (day frog). ***T. diurnus*:** a very soft version.
- **Chytrid-era decline:** a timeline of *falling* chorus density from the mid-1970s through 1990s could itself be an artwork layer.

### Gaps
- McDonald recording locations and dates, and whether they are playable, were not verified.
- The AmphibiaWeb parameters come via search summary only.
- No call data were found for other extinct Australian frogs (e.g. *Taudactylus* relatives).

## 13. "Thought lost, returned": night parrot (Pezoporus occidentalis)

### Takeaway
It was rediscovered in 2013 in western Queensland. Recordings were kept secret until February 2017, and more populations have since been found in WA. It is now acoustically well documented (Leseberg et al. 2019). Its calls are:
- ~2 kHz whistles (0.1–0.6 s)
- bell-like "dink-dink" trills like a ringing telephone
- frog-like croaks

Calling is concentrated in the first hour after sunset and the 2 hours before sunrise, year-round.

### Cited Findings
- John Young photographed the bird in 2013 after hearing candidate calls from 2008. The site became the secret Pullen Pullen reserve. His recordings were withheld until February 2017 to protect the birds. Controversy later surrounded his other claims, not the 2013 rediscovery. — [Audubon](https://audubon.org/news/john-young-rediscovered-australian-night-parrot-did-he-lie-about-his-later); [search summary](https://www.allaboutbirds.org/news/a-new-dawn-for-the-night-parrot/)
- **Historical descriptions:**
  - "a plaintive whistling note" (Mallee Bird 1908)
  - "sweet low two-note whistle" (McGilp 1931)
  - "long drawn-out mournful whistle" (Wilson 1937)
  - when alarmed, a "very peculiar croaking note" like the "loud croak of a frog"
  - — [Leseberg et al. 2019, Australian Field Ornithology 36:79–88](https://conservationpartners.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/Descriptions-of-known-vocalisations-of-the-Night-Parrot-Leserberg-et-al-2019.pdf)
- **Measured calls (Table 1, Leseberg et al. 2019):**

  | Call | Description | Duration | Peak frequency |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Hollow whistle | Loud; carries several hundred metres; EM calls have a downward hook at the start, CC calls an upward hook at the end; very similar to Pallid Cuckoo | 0.5–0.6 s | ~2.0 kHz |
  | One- and two-note trills | "sounds like a ringing telephone" | ~0.3 s per note | ~2.2 kHz |
  | Two-, three- and four-note mellow whistles | "dee-de-dee-de" | ~0.4 s (two-note) | 2.1–3.0 kHz |
  | Toot | | 0.1 s | ~1.8 kHz |
  | Short whistle | | 0.2 s | ~2.1 kHz |
  | Peel | Breeding-associated | 0.2 s | ~2.4 kHz |
  | Lapwing-like grating call | Breeding | 0.3 s | ~3.6 kHz |
  | Pratincole-like call | | 0.2 s | ~2.2 kHz |
  | Nestling rasp | Given when the nest is approached | ~3 s, repeated | |

  Plus croak ("gruk").
  - Sites: Channel Country Qld, East Murchison and Great Sandy Desert WA. — [Leseberg et al. 2019](https://conservationpartners.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/Descriptions-of-known-vocalisations-of-the-Night-Parrot-Leserberg-et-al-2019.pdf)
- **Timing:** "calling periods occur in the first hour after sunset and in the 2 hours before sunrise", year-round and brief (sometimes only minutes). Calling is more frequent and all-night at breeding sites. — [Leseberg et al. 2019](https://conservationpartners.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/Descriptions-of-known-vocalisations-of-the-Night-Parrot-Leserberg-et-al-2019.pdf)

### Inferences
- For "returned" species, the artwork can use real parameters rather than inference.
- Locations must stay coarse: the recovery program deliberately withheld recordings and sites. Any "last/secret place" should be blurred to the bioregion scale (e.g. "Channel Country" or "East Murchison"), and no coordinates should be published.

### Gaps
- Aboriginal names were not retrieved. Indigenous ranger programmes (e.g. Ngururrpa and Martu) have been involved in WA detections, per the Smithsonian summary, but this was not verified in detail.

## 14. Regent honeyeater (Anthochaera phrygia): song itself disappearing

### Takeaway
Crates et al. (2021, Proc. R. Soc. B, doi:10.1098/rspb.2021.0225) showed that where birds are rare, young males sing simplified or "totally incorrect" songs. 18 males (about 12% of the population) sang only other species' songs, and captive-bred birds sang different songs from wild birds.

### Cited Findings
- Rich, complex songs where birds remain relatively numerous; simplified or incorrect songs where rare. 18 males (about 12% of the population) only copied other species. Captive-bred birds' songs differed from wild birds'. Recordings are being used to tutor captive birds before release. — [ANU news](https://www.anu.edu.au/news/all-news/lost-song-spells-trouble-for-endangered-songbird)
- Quote from Ross Crates: "If endangered birds are unable to learn how to sing correctly, it seriously impacts their ability to communicate … Females will avoid males that sing unusual songs." — [ANU / search summary](https://www.anu.edu.au/news/all-news/lost-song-spells-trouble-for-endangered-songbird)

### Inferences
- An artwork layer where a species' song *degrades* over time: sparse birds singing borrowed phrases from other honeyeaters and friarbirds, contrasted with a historic dense chorus across the box-ironbark woodlands.

### Gaps
- The paper itself was blocked (403). No spectral parameters were retrieved, and I could not identify which species were mimicked.

## 15. Status authorities, cultural sensitivities, and how to handle "secret traces"

### Takeaway
- **Status authorities:** NZ status comes from the DOC NZ Threat Classification System (Robertson et al. 2021). Australian status comes from EPBC and IUCN.
- **Cultural weight:** strongest for the huia (tapu, chiefly feathers, a lure-call tradition carried by a named Māori elder), the moa (whakataukī of loss) and the thylacine (Palawa names and palawa kani).
- **Location sensitivity:** species that may persist or are recovering (SI kōkako, *Mystacina robusta*, night parrot) need deliberately blurred locations.

### Cited Findings
- NZTCS 2021 lists 62 extinct bird taxa, including the huia, laughing owls (both subspecies), all three piopio taxa, three bush wren subspecies, Stephens Island wren, NZ quail, Chatham Island bellbird, fernbird and rail, NZ and Chatham mergansers, NZ little bittern, Hodgens' waterhen, owlet-nightjar, Haast's eagle and moa species. Only two taxa are Data Deficient: SI kōkako and SI brown teal. — [DOC NZTCS 36 (Robertson et al. 2021)](https://www.doc.govt.nz/globalassets/documents/science-and-technical/nztcs36entire.pdf)
- Huia: "Considered tapu by Māori". — [Evans PDF](https://www.thomvandooren.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/Evans-Echoes-of-the-Past.pdf)
- Moa whakataukī: "kua ngaro i te ngaro o te moa". — [Wikipedia: Moa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moa)
- Thylacine Aboriginal names and the palawa kani name *kaparunina*. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thylacine)
- Bramble Cay melomys: Meriam name *Maizab Kaur Mukeis* (Erub). — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bramble_Cay_melomys)
- Night parrot recordings were withheld for four years to protect the birds. — [Audubon](https://audubon.org/news/john-young-rediscovered-australian-night-parrot-did-he-lie-about-his-later)

### Inferences
- **Huia:** a "rare secret trace" at Tararua could be the most powerful moment in the piece, but it is also the one most needing Māori consultation. Consider:
  - opening with silence or a karakia-respectful framing rather than playback
  - crediting Hēnare Hāmana and Robert Batley by name
  - not deriving pitch data from the Ngā Taonga copy without permission
- **Location blurring:** use bioregion-scale placement for any species with a live search or recovery programme.
- **Abundance scaling:**
  - Huia: 34k–89k
  - Moa: 58k–2.5M
  - Thylacine: 2k–5k
  - Bramble Cay melomys: hundreds on 2 ha
  - Maclear's rat: "abundant"
  - NZ quail: coveys, 40+ per day bags
  - These give defensible relative densities.

### Gaps
- EPBC status for the individual Australian mammals was not verified.
- No iwi, Palawa or Aboriginal community sources were directly consulted; the Te Ara Māori-perspective pages were blocked.
- I could not confirm the Tasmanian Aboriginal Centre's usage policy for palawa kani.
