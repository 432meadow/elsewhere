# Lost non-bird sound sources since ~1500: insects, marine soundscapes, bats, abundance soundscapes

Scope note: these notes feed "elsewhere", mode [ what can no longer be heard ]. Provenance tiers used throughout:
- **RECORDED**: an actual recording of the lost population or species exists (who/when/archive given where found).
- **DESCRIBED**: only written eyewitness description exists (author, year).
- **INFERRED**: nothing heard or written; the sound must be modelled from living kin or anatomy.

Coordinates are approximate and added by the note-taker for placement; they are not from the cited sources.

Research limits: the session's web-search budget ran out partway through. Several items the brief asked for (passenger pigeon, Eskimo curlew, Carolina parakeet, Puerto Rican flower bat, Palau dusky flying fox, Christmas Island pipistrelle call frequency, historic Columbia salmon numbers) could not be sourced, and are listed under Gaps rather than guessed.

## (a) Insects and swarms: which lost insect sounds can be reconstructed, at what scale, and from what evidence?

### Takeaway
The best-documented lost insect sound is the **Rocky Mountain locust swarm** (DESCRIBED, not recorded). Witnesses heard it as a roaring storm of wings, a crackling "like fire" of jaws, and a hail-like drumming as the insects landed, at a scale of up to ~12.5 trillion insects over ~198,000 sq mi. The **lost periodical cicada broods** (Brood XI in New England, last seen 1954; Brood XXI in Florida, last seen 1870) can be rebuilt with high fidelity, because their species' songs are still sung by living broods: *M. septendecim* is a ~1.3 kHz pure-tone "Pharaoh", *M. cassini* is broadband around 6 kHz with synchronized ticks and a buzz. Most extinct orthopterans (for example *Neduba extincta* and *Leptogryllus deceptor*) are known from single specimens, with **song unknown** (INFERRED only).

### Cited Findings

**Rocky Mountain locust (*Melanoplus spretus*): Albert's swarm and the 1873–77 outbreak**
- Range was the western half of the US and parts of western Canada. Permanent breeding grounds were probably "somewhere between 3 and 3,000 square miles of sandy soils near streams and rivers in the Rockies", which overlapped settler farmland. — [Wikipedia: Rocky Mountain locust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocky_Mountain_locust)
- The last major outbreak ran 1873–1877 across Colorado, Kansas, Minnesota, Missouri, Nebraska and other states, with about $200 million in crop damage. — [Wikipedia: Rocky Mountain locust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocky_Mountain_locust)
- Albert's swarm (1875) is estimated at 198,000 sq mi (510,000 km², larger than California), weighing 27.5 million tons, with about 12.5 trillion insects. Guinness cites it as the greatest concentration of animals ever recorded. — [Wikipedia: Rocky Mountain locust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocky_Mountain_locust); [Wikipedia: Albert's swarm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert%27s_swarm)
- **How it was measured:** Albert Child in Plattsmouth, Nebraska (~41.0°N, 95.9°W) watched the swarm pass overhead in **June 1875**. He timed its speed, telegraphed surrounding towns, and watched it stream by for **five days**, giving an estimate of ~1,800 miles long × 110 miles wide. — [HistoryNet: 1874 The Year of the Locust](https://historynet.com/1874-the-year-of-the-locust/). **Conflict:** Wikipedia calls Child "a physician interested in meteorology"; HistoryNet calls him "a county judge and sometime meteorologist". — [Wikipedia: Albert's swarm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert%27s_swarm)
- **Last specimens:** Fletcher (1901) and Criddle (1902) collected specimens in Manitoba. Morgan Hebard collected the final known specimens in **August 1904 in Colorado**. — [Wikipedia: Rocky Mountain locust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocky_Mountain_locust). **Correction to the brief:** 1902 in Canada is the last *Canadian* record, not the last specimen overall.
- Causes of extinction: plowing and irrigation of the Rocky Mountain breeding sites, conversion of prairie to farmland, and winter wheat that matured before the locusts migrated. Frozen remains survive in Grasshopper Glacier (Montana) and Knife Point Glacier (Wyoming). — [Wikipedia: Rocky Mountain locust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocky_Mountain_locust)
- **Sound, DESCRIBED (primary eyewitness):** Addison E. Sheldon, Nebraska historian, who saw the 1874 invasion as a boy, described a clear, hot July day with a haze over the sun that turned into a gray cloud. The wing vibration "filled the ear with a roaring sound like a rushing storm", and where the locusts landed they covered the ground "like a heavy crawling carpet". — quoted in [Wagner 2008, "Grasshoppered", *Nebraska History* 89:154–167](https://history.nebraska.gov/wp-content/uploads/2017/11/doc_publications_NH2008Grasshoppered.pdf)
- **Sound, DESCRIBED (feeding):** newspapers, letters and diaries reported that "their jaws crackled like a deadly fire as they ate fields bare". — [Wagner 2008](https://history.nebraska.gov/wp-content/uploads/2017/11/doc_publications_NH2008Grasshoppered.pdf)
- **Sound, DESCRIBED (approach):** in Swain Finch's 1876 account (Custer County, Nebraska, collected by Solomon Butcher), the swarm first looked like prairie-fire smoke in the northwest and darkened the air "like an eclipse". At about 100 yards the family "heard a continuous cracking and snapping sound, which increased to a perfect roar", and within seconds "every green thing in sight" was covered. — [Wagner 2008](https://history.nebraska.gov/wp-content/uploads/2017/11/doc_publications_NH2008Grasshoppered.pdf)
- **Sound, DESCRIBED (hail):** one prairie family's recollection says "the rasping whirring of their wings filled the whole air and they hit the ground and the house with the noise of a hailstorm". — via search summary of [Outdoor Nebraska: Clouds of Grasshoppers in 1874](https://magazine.outdoornebraska.gov/stories/history-and-culture/clouds-of-grasshoppers-in-1874/) (not verified against full text)
- Visual comparisons: "like a great, white glistening cloud", and like "a big snowstorm, where the air was filled with enormous-size flakes". — [Wikipedia: Rocky Mountain locust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocky_Mountain_locust)
- Timing: invasions in 1874–76 arrived in summer (Sheldon's "July day"; the Finch corn was at the roasting-ear stage). Swarms arrived as a daytime cloud. — [Wagner 2008](https://history.nebraska.gov/wp-content/uploads/2017/11/doc_publications_NH2008Grasshoppered.pdf)
- 1874 crop damage: about $50 million conservatively, around 74% of the total value of US farm products (as stated in the article). — [Wagner 2008](https://history.nebraska.gov/wp-content/uploads/2017/11/doc_publications_NH2008Grasshoppered.pdf)

**Lost periodical cicada broods (*Magicicada*)**
- **Brood XI** (17-year): made up only of *M. septendecim*, in CT, MA and RI. Recorded emergences were 1835 (Tolland Co.), 1869 (Suffield CT), 1903 (RI) and 1937 (rediscovered near Willington CT). The last was **1954**, when Jerauld Manter (UConn) saw cicadas from **10 June**, but "at no time did their numbers approach those of 1937". He found no egg-laying or twig damage. The brood is now regarded as extinct. — [Wikipedia: Brood XI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brood_XI). Last site: the Ashford/Willington town line, eastern CT (~41.87°N, 72.26°W). David Marshall suggests small patches may have been missed. — [Cicada Mania: extinct](https://cicadamania.com/cicadas/category/extinct)
- **Brood XXI** (13-year): a small brood, last recorded **1870** in the **Apalachicola River valley, Florida** (~30.4°N, 84.9°W). Extinct. — [Cicada Mania: extinct](https://cicadamania.com/cicadas/category/extinct); [Wikipedia: Brood XI (lists XXI)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brood_XI)
- **Brood VII** (Onondaga, NY) has contracted and now survives mainly on Onondaga Nation land (Cooley, Marshall & Simon 2004). It is a near-lost chorus. — [Cicada Mania: extinct](https://cicadamania.com/cicadas/category/extinct)
- **Bermuda cicada *Tibicen bermudiana*** went extinct in the 1950s after the cedar blight. — [Cicada Mania: extinct](https://cicadamania.com/cicadas/category/extinct)
- ***M. septendecim* song:** the calling song is a modulated pure tone, not pulsed, sharply tuned at a peak of **1.3 kHz**. It is heard as "Pharaoh" / "wee-oh", and a group sounds like "a UFO". — [Wikipedia: Decim periodical cicadas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decim_periodical_cicadas). Another source gives the drone range as 1.2–1.5 kHz. — (search summary; [UConn cicadas](https://cicadas.uconn.edu/m_septendecim/) hosts recordings and sonograms of calling song, Courtship I–III and chorus)
- ***M. cassini* song:** complex pulses, each divided into about 9 sub-pulses, broadly tuned near **6 kHz**. The call is "a series of ticks followed by a buzz" ("someone trying to get a lawnmower started"). Thousands of males sing in unison, then fly together, so the chorus alternates between bursts and silence. Cassini choruses peak **mid- to late afternoon**, later than decim or decula. — [Wikipedia: Cassini periodical cicadas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassini_periodical_cicadas); [Wikipedia: Decim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decim_periodical_cicadas)
- ***M. septendecula/tredecula* (decula):** a "hesitating, pulsating song". — [Wikipedia: Decim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decim_periodical_cicadas)
- Loudness: periodical cicada choruses are often 80–85 dB, and *M. cassini* chorus centres peak at 85 dB. Single insects on a microphone reach 109–111.4 dB. — [Cicada Mania: can periodical cicada song damage hearing](https://www.cicadamania.com/cicadas/can-periodical-cicada-song-damage-hearing/)

**Extinct orthopterans with no known song**
- **Antioch Dunes shieldback katydid (*Neduba extincta* Rentz 1977)**: known from one male collected **1 July 1937** at the Antioch Dunes, Contra Costa Co., CA (~38.02°N, 121.79°W), on sandy banks of the San Joaquin River. It was recognized only after extinction, from a specimen in a drawer. Sand mining and development destroyed its habitat. **"The song of this species is unknown."** — [Wikipedia: Neduba extincta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neduba_extincta); [Plazi treatment](https://treatment.plazi.org/GgServer/html/039C87AE7D48FFF8FF4702FE753EFCBD)
- **Oʻahu deceptor bush cricket (*Leptogryllus deceptor* Perkins 1910)**: last record 1910 or earlier. It may be known from a single specimen (Zimmerman 1948). The IUCN "Extinct in the Wild" listing appears to be an error, since no captive stock is known. No song data. — [Wikipedia: Leptogryllus deceptor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leptogryllus_deceptor)

**Insect decline as a density note**
- Hallmann et al. 2017 (*PLOS ONE*, 18 Oct 2017) used Malaise traps across 63 German protected areas over 27 years (1989–2016). Flying-insect biomass fell **76% across the season and 82% in midsummer**. Weather, land use and habitat change did not explain it. — [PubMed 29045418](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29045418/); [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2017-10-percent-decrease-total-insect-biomass.html)

### Inferences
- **Locust synthesis (INFERRED from DESCRIBED):** the eyewitness accounts give three layers:
  1. A diffuse wing roar ("rushing storm"). This could be modelled as dense, band-limited noise from trillions of low wing-beat sources, with the wing-beat fundamental well below 100 Hz, smeared into broadband noise by incoherence.
  2. A near-field "cracking and snapping" that builds to a roar as the front approaches. This could be modelled as crepitation clicks, with rate and density rising as distance falls.
  3. A hail layer of sparse-to-dense impulsive impacts on roofs and ground, plus a ground-level feeding "crackle like fire" (mandible clicks) after landing.
  A plausible diel envelope is a daytime arrival with a multi-day passage (Child's five days).
  Model from living *Melanoplus* (for example *M. sanguinipes*, its closest relative, per Lockwood) or desert locust swarms. **No wing-beat or crepitation frequency for *M. spretus* was found (see Gaps).**
- **Brood XI (1954 or earlier) in New England, June:** a pure *septendecim* chorus, so a single ~1.3 kHz "Pharaoh" drone with no cassini ticks or buzz. That makes it acoustically simpler and more tonal than mixed Midwestern broods. The 1954 emergence was sparse, so a historically faithful last emergence would be a thin chorus, not a wall.
- **Brood XXI (Florida, 1870 and before):** species composition is not documented in the sources found. The nearest living 13-year southern broods (for example Brood XIX) contain *M. tredecim/neotredecim*, *tredecassini* and *tredecula*. Treat this as INFERRED.
- The Hallmann figure could scale the overall insect bed of any European summer soundscape: roughly 0.24× the 1989 density, or 0.18× in midsummer. Biomass is not the same as acoustic output, so this is a density proxy only.

### Gaps
- No measured wing-beat frequency, crepitation spectrum or recording exists for *M. spretus*. I could not verify Lockwood's *Locust* (2004) text, Riley's Entomological Commission reports (1877–80) or Swinton's descriptions (search budget ran out).
- I did not verify the exact wording and attribution of the "noise of a hailstorm" quote (search summary only).
- I did not find the per-acre densities of periodical cicadas (the often-cited ~1.5 million per acre) or song durations in seconds for *septendecim* phrases.
- No song information exists for any extinct katydid, cricket or island orthopteran found. Lord Howe stick insect (*Dryococelus australis*, rediscovered on Ball's Pyramid) is not a sound source. Not researched further.
- No Indigenous-relationship sources for *M. spretus* were found. Onondaga Nation lands are the refuge of Brood VII; consultation-sensitive.

## (b) Marine: lost marine mammals, and what the pre-whaling and pre-collapse ocean sounded like

### Takeaway
Only the **baiji** has true recordings of a now-extinct marine mammal (whistles around 6 kHz; clicks lower than other dolphins). The **vaquita** is not extinct yet (~6–8 in 2024) and is well recorded (NBHF clicks at 128–139 kHz). Steller's sea cow and the Caribbean and Japanese pinnipeds are DESCRIBED or INFERRED only. At the soundscape scale, strong numbers support louder lost choruses:
- North Atlantic pre-whaling estimates of ~240,000 humpback and ~360,000 fin whales, 6–20× today.
- Blue whale song is now **31% lower** in pitch than in 1963, with one hypothesis tying pitch to population density.
- Northern cod spawning biomass fell to ~1% of peak by 1992, silencing a ~50 Hz grunt chorus.
- 85% of oyster reefs have been lost globally.
- Bleached Great Barrier Reef sites became **~15 dB quieter**.

### Cited Findings

**Steller's sea cow (*Hydrodamalis gigas*)**: extinct 1768, DESCRIBED
- By 1741 it survived only around the **Commander Islands** (~55°N, 166°E). There are Pleistocene records from California to Japan, and western Aleutian populations lasted until about 1,000 years ago. At discovery perhaps ≤2,000 remained (Stejneger 1887: <1,500). Hunting (Krassilnikov 1754, Korovin 1762) wiped it out by 1768, and Bragin found none in 1772. — [Wikipedia: Steller's sea cow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steller%27s_sea_cow)
- **Sound (Steller, *De bestiis marinis*, 1751):** "nearly mute", making only heavy breathing, raspy snorting "similar to a horse", and sighs. It fed most of the day, lifting its head **every 4–5 minutes** to breathe, and slept on its back after feeding. It lived in small family groups. — [Wikipedia: Steller's sea cow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steller%27s_sea_cow); [National Geographic](https://nationalgeographic.com/history/article/stellers-sea-cow-30-foot-hunted-extinction)
- Closest living relative: dugong. Aleut and Siberian Yupik peoples probably hunted it. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steller%27s_sea_cow)

**Caribbean monk seal (*Neomonachus tropicalis*)**: extinct, last seen 1952, DESCRIBED/INFERRED
- Range: Caribbean, Gulf of Mexico and western Atlantic, hauling out on isolated atolls and sandy beaches. Pre-exploitation population perhaps **233,000–338,000**. Haul-out groups were typically 20–40, sometimes up to 100. First European record: **1494**, when Columbus's crew killed eight on Alta Velo. **Last confirmed: 1952, Serranilla Bank** (~15.8°N, 79.8°W). Declared extinct by the US (NOAA) in 2008 after a five-year search. — [Wikipedia: Caribbean monk seal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caribbean_monk_seal); [ScienceDaily 2008](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2008/06/080608074828.htm)
- No vocal description was found.
- **Analogue: Hawaiian monk seal (*N. schauinslandi*).** Sills et al. 2021 found six regular underwater call types, all with energy **below 1 kHz** (growls, rumbles, whoops), calling tied to a ~6-month reproductive season, and best hearing 0.2–33 kHz. — [Sills et al. 2021, ESR 44:61–78](https://int-res.com/abstracts/esr/v44/p61-78)

**Mediterranean monk seal (*Monachus monachus*)**: extirpated from most of its range
- Historical range ran from the Black Sea and all Mediterranean islands to Gambia, the Canaries, Madeira and the Azores. Depletion began in Roman times, followed by medieval Iberian exploitation in the Atlantic. Today about 600–700 remain, in three populations: Cabo Blanco/Western Sahara, Madeira, and Greece–Turkey. — [Frontiers in Marine Science 2023 (search summary)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2023.1034124/pdf); [Wikipedia: Monachus](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monachus)

**Japanese sea lion (*Zalophus japonicus*)**: extinct 1970s, INFERRED
- Harvests were ~3,200 around 1900, fell to ~300 by 1915, and to a few dozen by the 1930s. The last colony was 50–60 animals at **Takeshima/Dokdo (Liancourt Rocks, ~37.24°N, 131.87°E) in 1951** (IUCN). The last confirmed record is a juvenile caught off **Rebun Island, Hokkaido (~45.4°N, 141.0°E) in 1974**. — [Wikipedia: Japanese sea lion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_sea_lion)
- Geopolitically sensitive: the Takeshima/Dokdo name is disputed between Japan and Korea.
- Analogue: California sea lion (*Z. californianus*) barks. No acoustic parameters were retrieved.

**Baiji (*Lipotes vexillifer*)**: functionally extinct 2006, RECORDED
- Range: middle–lower Yangtze, from Yichang to Shanghai, plus the Poyang and Dongting lakes. It had gone from the Qiantang River since the 1950s. Population was ~400 in 1979–81, at least 13 in 1997–99, with the last confirmed records in 2001–2002 (a stranded female, a photograph). The survey of **6 Nov–13 Dec 2006** covered 1,669 km twice, visually and acoustically, and found **zero sightings or acoustic detections**. The main cause was bycatch: rolling hooks, gillnets, fyke nets and electrofishing. — [Turvey et al. 2007, Biol. Lett.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2391192/)
- The last known individual, **Qiqi**, was rescued in 1980 and died in captivity in **2002** (Wuhan). — [China Daily 2007](https://covid-19.chinadaily.com.cn/china/2007-08/10/content_6020016.htm)
- **Acoustics:** dominant whistle frequency ~**5.8 kHz**, lasting ~**1 s**. Echolocation clicks ~**60–110 kHz**, lower than bottlenose (~110–130 kHz) and well below the sympatric Yangtze finless porpoise (peak 87–145 kHz, mean ~125 kHz), which suggests acoustic niche partitioning. — [DOSITS: Baiji audio gallery](https://dosits.org/galleries/audio-gallery/marine-mammals/toothed-whales/baiji-yangtze-river-dolphin) (page returned 403; figures via search summary); [Li et al. 2015, PLoS ONE, finless porpoise clicks](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4459872/)

**Vaquita (*Phocoena sinus*)**: NOT extinct, critically endangered, RECORDED
- Clicks are narrow-band high-frequency (NBHF) with peaks at **128–139 kHz** and **11–28 kHz bandwidth**, given in regular click trains. It makes no whistles. — [IUCN-CSG Vaquita 2024 Appendices](https://iucn-csg.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/Appendices-Vaquita-2024-final.pdf)
- The **May 2024** survey in the Upper Gulf of California (~31.0°N, 114.6°W) used 34 acoustic loggers and logged 70 acoustic detections (61 in 2023). The sighting-based estimate was **6–8 individuals**, down from 8–13 in 2023. All animals seen, including a yearling, looked healthy. — [AWI Quarterly Fall 2024](https://awionline.org/awi-quarterly/fall-2024/latest-vaquita-survey-paints-grim-picture)
- Flag it in the work as "nearly", not lost.

**Atlantic gray whale (*Eschrichtius robustus*, North Atlantic population)**: extirpated by the 1700s, INFERRED
- Once present on the North American east coast, in the Baltic and North Seas, around Iceland and in the English Channel. There are accounts of live whales from **Iceland in the early 1600s** and possibly **New England in the early 1700s**. The youngest dated subfossil is ~**1675**. Extirpated before the end of the 1700s. — [COSEWIC Grey Whale addendum 2009](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/grey-whale-addendum.html); [whatismissing.org](https://whatismissing.org/content/atlantic-grey-whale)
- **Model on Pacific gray whales:** calls span ~40 Hz–4 kHz. The commonest lagoon call, "S1" (knocks), is pulses of 100–1600 Hz in sets of 3–18. Migrating whales mainly give "S3" low FM moans. Other calls include rumbles, growls, croaks, grunts and bubble blasts. — [DOSITS: Gray whale](https://dosits.org/galleries/audio-gallery/marine-mammals/baleen-whales/gray-whale) (via search summary); [Frontiers Mar. Sci. 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fmars.2018.00329/pdf)

**North Atlantic right whale (*Eubalaena glacialis*)**: critically endangered, RECORDED
- The upcall is a ~1–2 s upsweep, given as ~100→400 Hz in one source and ~50–200 Hz in another. It is the commonest call for all ages and sexes, a contact call. — [phys.org 2015](https://phys.org/news/2015-05-analysis-archived-north-atlantic-whales.html); [NOAA repository](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/56136) (via search summary)
- Population: the retrieved source gives ~450, a 2015-era figure and outdated. The current NOAA estimate was not retrieved.

**Pre-whaling ocean chorus**
- Roman & Palumbi 2003 (*Science*, 25 July 2003) used mtDNA coalescent estimates for the pre-whaling North Atlantic: **~240,000 humpback, ~360,000 fin, ~265,000 minke**. The fin and humpback figures are **6–20× present-day** populations. — [Roman & Palumbi 2003, Science](https://fdn.montana.edu/kalinowski/teaching/documents-and-images/14%20CONGEN%20-%20READING%20-%20Whales%20before%20whaling%20-%20Science.pdf); [Stanford news](https://news.stanford.edu/pr/03/whales86.html)
- McDonald, Hildebrand & Mesnick 2009 (*ESR* 9:13–21) found at least 10 blue whale song types worldwide, each stable in units and phrasing over decades. In **7 of 7** types with multi-year data, the tonal frequency is falling. — [McDonald et al. 2009 PDF](https://www.cetus.ucsd.edu/docs/publications/McDonaldESR2009.pdf)
  - **Eastern North Pacific:** the third harmonic fell from 65.7 Hz in 1963 to 45.5 Hz in 2008 (**0.4 Hz/yr**, r = −0.99). The fundamental went from **21.9 Hz to 15.2 Hz**, 31% lower.
  - Song structure: unit A is pulsed, ~20 s, fundamental ~16 Hz with 5 overtones. Unit B is nearly tonal, ~20 s, with harmonics at integer multiples.
  - Individuals within a year match frequency to within 3%.
  - Blue whales were hunted commercially until 1972.
  - Hypotheses tested were sexual selection, ocean noise, body size after whaling, warming, and **population recovery**. The authors' favoured, testable idea: at the low densities after whaling, males had to sing **higher and louder** to be heard far away. As density recovers, they can shift lower. — [McDonald et al. 2009](https://www.cetus.ucsd.edu/docs/publications/McDonaldESR2009.pdf)
- Ocean noise: shipping and seismic surveys dominate 10–500 Hz. The shipping contribution has risen by up to **12 dB** over recent decades. Before the mid-19th century, low-frequency ocean sound came from waves, spray and bubbles, marine life and seismic sources. — [Hildebrand 2009, MEPS 395:5–20](https://int-res.com/abstracts/meps/v395/p5-20). A comparison of pre-industrial and modern levels puts degradation at 50 Hz at **>15 dB**. — search summary of [Frontiers Mar. Sci. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fmars.2021.689860/pdf) (not verified in full text)

**Lost fish choruses**
- **Atlantic cod (*Gadus morhua*):** males give low-frequency grunts during courtship and spawning. Grunts last 57–360 ms, with a fundamental of **49.7 ± 5.6 Hz** and a second harmonic of **102.9 ± 10.9 Hz**. The broader band is ~50–500 Hz ("grunts or bops"). A "hum" is reported during the ventral mount just before spawning. Male drumming muscles grow before spawning, consistent with sexual selection. — [MDPI JMSE 13:378 (2025)](https://mdpi-res.com/d_attachment/jmse/jmse-13-00378/article_deploy/jmse-13-00378.pdf); [Rowe & Hutchings, drumming muscle](https://latam-cl.demo.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/the-function-of-sound-production-by-atlantic-cod-as-inferred-from); [Sound Production by Atlantic Cod during Spawning (TAFS)](https://openpolar.no/Record/crwiley:10.1577%2Ft04-061.1)
- **Northern cod collapse:** by 1992 spawning biomass was about **1% of its estimated peak**. John Crosbie declared a moratorium on **2 July 1992**, and about 40,000 workers lost jobs. — [Canadian Encyclopedia](https://thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/cod-moratorium-of-1992); [Wikipedia: Collapse of the Atlantic northwest cod fishery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collapse_of_the_Atlantic_northwest_cod_fishery)
- **Gulf corvina (*Cynoscion othonopterus*):** a living analogue for a mass croaker chorus. Up to **1.5 million fish** aggregate over up to **27 km** of the Colorado River Delta each **February–June**. The chorus reaches **202 dB**, with individual calls at 177 dB and energy below ~2 kHz. It is "the loudest fish ever documented" (Rowell & Erisman 2017, *Biol. Lett.*). — [phys.org 2017](https://phys.org/news/2017-12-fish-sex-loud-deafen-dolphins.html); [Rowell et al. (VLIZ pdf)](https://www.vliz.be/imisdocs/publications/ocrd/309702.pdf)

**Oyster reefs**
- About **85% of oyster reefs have been lost globally**, and in many bays more than 99% are gone and "functionally extinct". The study covered 144 bays and 44 ecoregions. — [Beck et al. 2011, BioScience 61(2)](https://bioone.org/journals/BioScience/volume-61/issue-2/bio.2011.61.2.5/Oyster-Reefs-at-Risk-and-Recommendations-for-Conservation-Restoration-and/10.1525/bio.2011.61.2.5.full)
- North Carolina oyster reefs are consistently louder than nearby soft bottom in the **1.5–20 kHz** band, driven by snapping shrimp and oyster toadfish. Reef sound playback increased oyster larval settlement in the lab and in the field. — [Lillis, Eggleston & Bohnenstiehl 2013, PLoS ONE](https://cmast.ncsu.edu/cmast-docs/eggleston/pubs/soundscape-ecology/Lillis-etal-2013-PlosOne.pdf); [NCSU news](https://news.ncsu.edu/2013/10/oyster-sound)

**Coral reefs**
- Gordon et al. 2018 (*PNAS*) recorded Lizard Island reefs, northern GBR (~14.67°S, 145.45°E), in **November 2012 and November 2016**. In between came Cyclone Ita (Apr 2014), Cyclone Nathan (Mar 2015) and the 2016 bleaching, when more than 60% of coral bleached. After degradation the reefs were on average **15 dB quieter**, with lower acoustic complexity and richness. They were 8% less attractive to larvae, drew **40% less settlement**, and were "no more attractive than open-ocean sound". — [Exeter news 2018](https://news-archive.exeter.ac.uk/2018/april/title_655930_en.html); [Gordon et al. 2018 (PMC5960293)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5960293)
- Gordon et al. 2019 (*Nature Communications*) played healthy-reef recordings on 33 dead-coral patches for 40 nights (Oct–Dec 2017). This **doubled fish abundance** and raised species richness by 50%. — [Exeter news 2019](https://news-archive.exeter.ac.uk/2019/november/title_768084_en.html); [JCU repository](https://researchonline.jcu.edu.au/61219)
- **Snapping shrimp** (the base "crackle" of reefs and oyster beds): snaps are made by collapsing cavitation bubbles. Peak frequencies are 2–5 kHz, the band runs from 2 kHz to over 200 kHz (typically peaking 2–20 kHz), and source levels reach **up to ~190 dB re 1 µPa @ 1 m**. Snap rates **peak at dawn and dusk** and are higher by day than by night. — [Frontiers Mar. Sci. 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fmars.2023.1029003/full); [Lillis & Mooney 2018](https://www2.whoi.edu/site/amooney/wp-content/uploads/sites/31/2018/06/Lillis_Mooney_2018_CR.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Steller's sea cow synthesis:** mostly silence at the surface. Model a horse-like rasping exhalation and inhalation every ~4–5 min per animal, staggered across a small family group, over kelp-bed surf. There is no underwater vocal evidence. Dugong chirps and whistles are the only kin model, and adding them would be a speculative INFERRED layer; the "nearly mute" description argues for restraint.
- **Caribbean monk seal:** at 233k–338k animals in haul-outs of 20–100, colonies would have produced an in-air layer (INFERRED from other monk seals, parameters not retrieved) plus sub-1 kHz underwater growls and whoops on the Hawaiian model, seasonally concentrated.
- **Pre-whaling ocean (INFERRED):** density scaling from Roman & Palumbi means 6–20× more humpback and fin sources in the North Atlantic. Combined with ≥12–15 dB less ship noise at low frequency, whale calls would have carried much farther. McDonald's hypothesis implies the few post-whaling singers of the 1960s sang *higher* than today. Whether pre-whaling songs were lower still is unknown. One option is a low-density/high-pitch vs high-density/low-pitch parameter, clearly flagged as hypothesis.
- **Cod chorus:** a Grand Banks or Norwegian spawning ground at pre-1960s density would be a dense bed of ~50 Hz fundamental grunts with ~100 Hz harmonics, 57–360 ms each, plus occasional hums, concentrated in the spawning season. Gulf corvina (202 dB in aggregate) shows how loud a spawning aggregation can get, but cod are much quieter per fish (source levels not retrieved).
- **Reef and oyster restoration:** a "lost reef" can be heard as roughly +15 dB of broadband crackle (2–20 kHz) with more biophony and dawn/dusk peaks, against today's degraded state. Oyster-bay layers at 1.5–20 kHz could return wherever historical reefs stood (for example Chesapeake), about 85% of global reef area.

### Gaps
- No recording or description of the vocalizations of the Caribbean monk seal or the Japanese sea lion was found. The only Steller's sea cow evidence is Steller's text (the Latin original was not consulted directly).
- No Atlantic gray whale acoustics exist; the model is Pacific only.
- The current NARW estimate (post-2020) was not retrieved. Baiji source papers (for example Wang Ding / Xiao & Jing whistle studies) were not verified directly; the DOSITS page was blocked (403).
- Historical cod stock size in individuals, cod source levels, and Atlantic sturgeon or black drum chorus parameters were not researched (search budget exhausted).
- Pre-whaling blue whale abundance and whether pre-whaling blue whale songs were lower are unknown.

## (c) Bats and ultrasonic voices: lost bats, and whether any were recorded

### Takeaway
The **Christmas Island pipistrelle** is the emblematic case. Its extinction was literally *heard*: ultrasonic detectors tracked the population's collapse (a 99.4% fall in detections 2006–09), and the **last echolocation call was recorded on 26 August 2009**. The recording is held by Australia's National Film and Sound Archive. **Its call frequency was not found in any source I could verify.** The other lost bats (Lord Howe long-eared bat, greater short-tailed bat, Guam flying fox, small Mauritian flying fox, large Palau flying fox) are known only from specimens or sightings, so their sounds are INFERRED from living congeners.

### Cited Findings

**Christmas Island pipistrelle (*Pipistrellus murrayi*, now *Alionoctula murrayi*)**: extinct 2009, RECORDED
- Endemic to Christmas Island (135 km², Indian Ocean; ~10.49°S, 105.62°E). It weighed 3–4.5 g, the smallest described bat in Australia. It roosted in mature primary forest and foraged in primary and secondary forest. It was "common" in 1985 and declined from about 1990. Acoustic detections fell **99.4% between Jan 2006 and Jan 2009**, leaving ~20 individuals in Jan 2009. The last communal roost held 4 bats, against 54 three years earlier. — [Wikipedia: Christmas Island pipistrelle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christmas_Island_pipistrelle)
- "The last echolocation call of this bat was recorded on **26 August 2009**, when it went silent." A capture-for-breeding program began in July 2009 but caught nothing (one bat detected in four weeks). It was the first Australian mammal extinction in about 50 years. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christmas_Island_pipistrelle)
- Suspected causes: yellow crazy ants, the wolf snake, and possibly fipronil (2001–04), disease or cascade effects. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christmas_Island_pipistrelle)
- It was the island's only echolocating bat, which made acoustic monitoring reliable. During the August 2009 rescue, "night after night, the devices recorded only a single individual." Lindy Lumsden: "the species became extinct on 26 August 2009". Sources: Lumsden 2009, *Australasian Bat Society Newsletter* 33:21–25; Woinarski 2018, *A Bat's End* (CSIRO). — [artensterben.de](https://www.artensterben.de/en/christmas-island-pipistrelle/)
- **Archive:** NFSA holds "Last call of the Christmas Island Pipistrelle". Per the search snippet it was captured by "Dr Baker" on a handheld echolocation detector. — [NFSA collection item](https://www.nfsa.gov.au/collection/item/last-call-christmas-island-pipistrelle) (page returned 403; not verified). **Conflict or ambiguity:** artensterben.de credits the final survey effort to Lindy Lumsden. Both may be true (a team effort).
- A 2018 Wilderness Society piece is also titled "Last call of the wild". — [wilderness.org.au](https://wilderness.org.au/news-events/last-call-of-the-wild)

**Lord Howe long-eared bat (*Nyctophilus howensis*)**: extinct, INFERRED
- Known only from one skull found in **1972** in Gooseberry Cave, Lord Howe Island (~31.55°S, 159.08°E). It was described by McKean in 1975, and the skull was later dated to the early 20th century. Bats may have been seen casually in the 20th century. The likely cause was rats from shipwrecks and the owls introduced to control them. — [Wikipedia: Lord Howe long-eared bat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Howe_long-eared_bat); [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/australian-endangered-species-lord-howe-long-eared-bat-19977)

**New Zealand greater short-tailed bat (*Mystacina robusta*)**: probably extinct, INFERRED
- By European arrival it was restricted to islets off Stewart Island/Rakiura: Taukihepa/Big South Cape (~47.23°S, 167.40°E) and Solomon Island. Rats invaded in 1963–64, and there has been **no confirmed sighting since 1965**. The only photo was taken by Don Merton in 1965 at Puwai Bay. Its extinction was among the first attributed definitively to rats. — [Wikipedia: NZ greater short-tailed bat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_greater_short-tailed_bat)
- Living analogue: the lesser short-tailed bat (*M. tuberculata*). Acoustic parameters were not retrieved.

**Flying foxes: audible, with camps full of squabbling**
- **Small Mauritian flying fox / "rougette" (*Pteropus subniger*)**: Mauritius and Réunion. Last recorded Mauritius **1859** and Réunion **1862**. It roosted in old trees and caves and was hunted, and its habitat was cleared. — [Wikipedia: Small Mauritian flying fox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_Mauritian_flying_fox)
- **Guam flying fox (*Pteropus tokudae*)**: the last known individual was killed in **1968**. There was a sighting at Tarague cliff in March 1967, and unconfirmed reports into the late 1970s. Hunting was probably the main cause, possibly with brown tree snakes. — [Wikipedia: Guam flying fox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guam_flying_fox)
- **Large Palau flying fox (*Pteropus pilosus*)**: known from two specimens collected before **1874**, probably extinct around then. — [Wikipedia: Large Palau flying fox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large_Palau_flying_fox)
- Mauritian flying fox (*P. niger*) still lives on Mauritius and is a direct living analogue for *P. subniger*. — [Wikipedia: Mauritian flying fox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mauritian_flying_fox)

### Inferences
- **Christmas Island pipistrelle synthesis:** pipistrelles typically use FM-to-quasi-constant-frequency search calls in the high tens of kHz. The artensterben source says only "high frequency range". Treat any kHz value as INFERRED from congeners until the Lumsden or NFSA data are checked. Within the work's existing bat-detector framing (the moth variation), this is ideal material: a **heterodyne/time-expanded "last call"**, with density scaled from "common" in 1985, down 99.4% by 2009, to a single bat.
- **Flying fox camps (Mauritius, Réunion, Guam, Palau):** audible squabbling and chatter at dawn and dusk returns and by day in the camp. Model on living *P. niger* or other *Pteropus*. No parameters were retrieved.

### Gaps
- **Christmas Island pipistrelle call frequency and shape:** the search summary claim of "30–50 kHz" is **unverified** (no primary source) and should not be used without checking Lumsden et al. 2007/2009 or the NFSA recording metadata.
- Puerto Rican flower bat (*Phyllonycteris major*) and the Palau dusky flying fox were not researched (search budget exhausted). Puerto Rican flower bat is, as far as I know, known from fossil or subfossil material, so it may fall outside "since 1500" (unverified).
- No acoustic data exist for any *Pteropus* in these notes.

## (d) Lost abundance soundscapes: what did mass phenomena sound like, and at what scale?

### Takeaway
Bison give the richest DESCRIBED abundance soundscape. Hornaday (1889) describes the August–September "running season", when herds of many thousands massed and the bulls' roaring merged into "a great volume of sound like distant thunder", audible **1–3 miles** away, reportedly up to 5. Herds on the move rumbled at night, and the great southern herd was ~4 million. By 1889 Hornaday counted only 1,091 bison. Other abundance soundscapes with usable parameters are prairie-chicken and heath-hen booming grounds (~270–325 Hz, 1.9 s booms; last heath hen March 1932), wolf chorus howls (F0 ~150 Hz to over 1 kHz, 0.5–15 s, audible ~10 km), red knot flocks at Delaware Bay (down ~75% since the 1980s), and autumn salmon runs (detectable by hydrophone). Passenger pigeon and other bird flocks are covered in a separate file.

### Cited Findings

**American bison (*Bison bison*)**: extirpated from most of its range, DESCRIBED
- **Rut ("running season"), August–September:** the herd "came together in a dense and confused mass of many thousand individuals, so closely congregated as to actually blacken the face of the landscape". A bull "roars like a fog-horn until the earth seems to fairly tremble with the vibration". "The mingled roaring—it can not truthfully be described as lowing or bellowing—of a number of huge bulls unite and form a great volume of sound like distant thunder, which has often been heard at a distance of from 1 to 3 miles," and "under favorable atmospheric conditions such sounds have been heard five miles." — [Hornaday 1889, *The Extermination of the American Bison*, Project Gutenberg](https://gutenberg.org/cache/epub/17748/pg17748-images.html)
- **Catlin, on the Missouri near the mouth of White River (Dakota; ~43.7°N, 99.4°W):** in the running season "we had heard the 'roaring' (as it is called) of the herd when we were several miles from them". — quoted in [Hornaday 1889](https://gutenberg.org/cache/epub/17748/pg17748-images.html)
- **Stampede or night herd:** at about 10 pm "a low rumble was heard, which gradually increased to 'a thundering noise'", and by morning an estimated 10,000 were in sight. A buffalo surround brought "roaring of the bulls, the bellowing of the cows, and the piteous moaning of the calves". — [Hornaday 1889](https://gutenberg.org/cache/epub/17748/pg17748-images.html)
- **Eastern range:** at the site of Nashville in 1770, "Their bellowings sounded from the hills and forest" (Ramsey, quoted). — [Hornaday 1889](https://gutenberg.org/cache/epub/17748/pg17748-images.html)
- **Scale:** the great herd on the Arkansas (Col. R. I. Dodge) was at least 25 miles wide and about 50 miles deep, taking five days to pass, at 15–20 per acre at rest. Hornaday settles on **4,000,000** for the great southern herd. **1,091** bison remained at the time of his 1889 census. — [Hornaday 1889](https://gutenberg.org/cache/epub/17748/pg17748-images.html)
- Modern rut behaviour: a defending bull's bellow "closely resembl[es] the roar of a lion", a signature midsummer sound in Yellowstone. — [NPS Bison Bellows](https://www.nps.gov/articles/bison-bellows-6-23-16.htm) (via search summary)

**Heath hen and prairie-chicken booming grounds (*Tympanuchus cupido cupido* / *T. c. pinnatus*)**: DESCRIBED/RECORDED (analogue)
- "Booming Ben", the last heath hen, was last seen **11 March 1932** on Martha's Vineyard (~41.40°N, 70.62°W). The nickname came from the booming display. — [Wikipedia: Booming Ben](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Booming_Ben); [JSTOR Daily](https://daily.jstor.org/last-heath-hen)
- Alfred O. Gross made early films of heath hens. — [Bowdoin news 2018](https://www.bowdoin.edu/news/2018/10/the-heath-hen-and-other-early-ornithological-films-of-alfred-otto-gross.html)
- **Greater prairie-chicken boom:** chorus average peak **297 Hz**, carried in a narrow band of ~**270–325 Hz**, each boom ~**1.9 s**. It travels far across grassland as an advertisement. — [Tethys/PNNL: wind-turbine noise and prairie-chicken](https://tethys.pnnl.gov/publications/effects-wind-turbine-noise-surrounding-soundscape-context-greater-prairie-chicken); [Acoustics.org 169th meeting](https://acoustics.org/category/meetings/169th-meeting/page/2)

**Grey wolf (*Canis lupus*)**: extirpated across much of Europe and the US
- Mean fundamental of howls is 274–908 Hz, and adult F0 ranges from ~150 Hz to over 1,000 Hz. Iberian wolves average 270–720 Hz, with howls of 1.1–12.8 s. Overall howl durations run 0.52–15.5 s. Howls can be audible over up to 130 km² and at least ~10 km in open terrain. — [Wikipedia: Howling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howling); [Palacios et al. 2007, J. Mammal. (Iberian wolf howls)](https://bioone.org/journals/Journal-of-Mammalogy/volume-88/issue-3/06-MAMM-A-151R1.1/Iberian-Wolf-Howls--Acoustic-Structure-Individual-Variation-and-a/10.1644/06-MAMM-A-151R1.1.full)
- The last wild wolf in Ireland was reportedly killed in **1786**, about 300 years after wolves were thought gone from England and about 100 years after Scotland. — [Wikipedia: Wolves in Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolves_in_Ireland)

**Red knot (*Calidris canutus rufa*) at Delaware Bay**: DESCRIBED/RECORDED
- Counts in Tierra del Fuego and Delaware Bay fell by about 75% from the 1980s to the 2000s. Delaware Bay supports 50–80% of migrating rufa each spring, timed to horseshoe crab spawning. The subspecies was listed as Threatened under the US ESA in 2014. Flock call is a low monosyllabic *knutt*; the migration call is a disyllabic *knuup-knuup*. — [Wikipedia: Red knot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_knot)

**Salmon runs**
- Most salmon runs are in autumn (September–November). — [Wikipedia: Salmon run](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salmon_run)
- Hydrophones can detect female fall Chinook moving gravel while digging redds (Columbia River studies). — [OSTI 962011](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/962011); [USGS: DIDSON imaging of Chinook redds](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70026384)

### Inferences
- **Bison synthesis:** the parameters are an August–September season, a massed herd, and low roars "like a fog-horn" that merge into distant thunder audible 1–3 (up to 5) miles away. The rest of the year the herd was dispersed and mostly quiet: grunting, hoof noise, and stampede rumble building from a "low rumble" to thunder. Model the timbre on living rutting bulls (Yellowstone, American Prairie) and scale the source count to Hornaday's "many thousand" in a single rut aggregation. The Arkansas herd (at least 25 × 50 miles, 15–20 per acre) is a spatial-density parameter.
- **Booming ground:** 270–325 Hz narrow-band 1.9 s booms at dawn in spring, with overlapping males on a lek. The heath hen can be modelled from greater prairie-chicken recordings (INFERRED; subspecies-level differences are unknown).
- **Wolf chorus:** use the Iberian parameters (270–720 Hz, 1–13 s) for western Europe and the British Isles before extirpation. Placement dates: England around the 1480s–1500s, Scotland around the 1680s, Ireland 1786 (derived from the Wikipedia "300/100 years earlier" statement, so approximate).
- **Salmon:** an autumn, riverine, largely non-vocal layer of splashing, leaping (up to ~3.65 m per Wikipedia) and gravel-rattling redd digging. Scale it to historical run sizes once those are sourced.

### Gaps
- **Passenger pigeon, Eskimo curlew, American golden plover, Carolina parakeet** abundance soundscapes were not researched here. They are deferred to the bird file, and the search budget was exhausted.
- **Historic Columbia River salmon numbers** (often cited as 10–16 million) and sources on Indigenous salmon relationships (First Salmon ceremonies of Columbia River tribes, for example CRITFC) were not retrieved.
- **Indigenous bison relationships:** the Buffalo Treaty page (Wikipedia) returned 404, so no citation. The report writer should treat bison, salmon and wolves as culturally sensitive and consult Indigenous-led sources, for example the 2014 Buffalo Treaty and InterTribal Buffalo Council (not verified here).
- Delaware Bay 1980s red knot peak counts (the figure of ~95,000 is often quoted) were not verified.
- No bison vocal frequency measurements (Hz) were retrieved; only historical descriptions.
