## 1. Contested and "not heard since" status, rediscoveries ("Lazarus" species), and a labelling scheme

### Takeaway
"Extinct" is often an administrative verdict reached years after the last good evidence. The ivory-billed woodpecker is still federally Endangered, and the final USFWS decision was scheduled for Sept 2025 and is still not published as far as I could confirm. A claim that the proposal was withdrawn in July 2024 appears only in low-quality sources and is contradicted by the federal Unified Agenda. The artwork needs a status label that is separate from the provenance tag and records the date of the last good evidence, the kind of evidence it was, and any formal verdict with its date.

### Cited Findings

**Ivory-billed woodpecker (*Campephilus principalis*)**
- USFWS proposed delisting 23 species as extinct in Sept 2021, and the ivory-bill was one of them. — [USFWS press release 2021](https://www.fws.gov/press-release/2021-09/us-fish-and-wildlife-service-proposes-delisting-23-species-endangered-species)
- The final rule on 17 Oct 2023 (effective 16 Nov 2023) delisted 21 species: 1 mammal, 10 birds, 2 fishes and 8 mussels. The plant proposal was withdrawn. For the ivory-bill the agenda says "FWS will make a final determination on our proposal", with final action projected for "09/00/2025" in both the Fall 2024 and Spring 2025 Unified Agendas. — [reginfo.gov RIN 1018-BC98, Fall 2024](https://www.reginfo.gov/public/do/eAgendaViewRule?pubId=202410&RIN=1018-BC98); [Spring 2025](https://www.reginfo.gov/public/do/eAgendaViewRule?pubId=202504&RIN=1018-BC98)
- Oct 2023 reporting: the ivory-bill was left out of the 21 and USFWS said it would "continue to analyze and review". — [CNN 16 Oct 2023](https://www.cnn.com/2023/10/16/us/ivory-billed-woodpecker-not-extinct-yet-climate); [KUAF Nov 2023](https://www.kuaf.com/show/ozarks-at-large/2023-11-09/ivory-billed-woodpeckers-federal-extinction-declaration-deferred)
- Wikipedia, as fetched Sept 2026: USFWS status still Endangered and unchanged, IUCN Critically Endangered (Possibly Extinct), NatureServe "Presumed Extinct". — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivory-billed_woodpecker)
- **Conflicting claim:** a 2026 aggregator says USFWS "formally withdrew the extinction proposal" in July 2024. — [allaboutwildlife.com](https://www.allaboutwildlife.com/ivory-billed-woodpecker-2026/). The official Unified Agenda still lists the proposal as pending, and Wikipedia also says pending. **Treat the withdrawal claim as unreliable.**
- The last universally accepted sighting was April 1944 in the Singer Tract, Louisiana (Don Eckelberry). The 1935 Arthur Allen expedition made "the only universally accepted audio and motion picture recordings". — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivory-billed_woodpecker). The Macaulay Library calls the 1935 Allen recording one of its "crown jewels". — [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/digital-sound-archives-can-bring-extinct-birds-briefly-back-to-life-176115)
- 2004–05 Arkansas (Cache River NWR): the Cornell team led by Fitzpatrick had sightings, possible audio, and the 4-second Luneau video. Sibley et al. argued the video was consistent with a pileated woodpecker. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivory-billed_woodpecker)
- Project Principalis (National Aviary) submitted trail-camera, audio and drone evidence from Louisiana during the comment period and published it without claiming it was definitive. A 2024 analysis by Michalak found inconsistencies and judged the audio "more similar to great horned owl vocalizations". — [National Aviary](https://www.aviary.org/news/project-principalis-submits-to-u-s-fish-and-wildlife-service-new-evidence-of-ivory-billed-woodpeckers-survival-during-extended-comment-period/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivory-billed_woodpecker)

**Bachman's warbler (*Vermivora bachmanii*)**
- Delisted as extinct in the 17 Oct 2023 final rule, as one of the 21 species. First listed in 1967. Reports put the last sightings in the 1980s. — [Fox8/Gray News 17 Oct 2023](https://www.fox8live.com/2023/10/17/songbird-once-found-southeast-us-among-21-species-delisted-endangered-species-act/); [ABC7](https://abc7.com/post/21-species-declared-extinct-delisted-from-endangered-species-act/13933315/)
- A 1954 recording by Arthur A. Allen and Peter P. Kellogg is in the Macaulay Library. — [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/digital-sound-archives-can-bring-extinct-birds-briefly-back-to-life-176115)

**Slender-billed curlew (*Numenius tenuirostris*)**
- Declared extinct in Nov 2024 in a paper in *Ibis* by RSPB, BirdLife, NHM London and Naturalis. The last irrefutable record was Morocco, Feb 1995, and the probability of extinction was put at 96%. It is the first known global bird extinction from mainland Europe, North Africa and West Asia. — [BirdLife 18 Nov 2024](https://www.birdlife.org/news/2024/11/18/new-publication-indicates-devastating-extinction-of-the-slender-billed-curlew/); [BirdGuides](https://www.birdguides.com/news/slender-billed-curlew-officially-declared-extinct/)
- Causes given: hunting on migration, and loss of wetland and steppe to drainage and agriculture. — [ABC Birds](https://abcbirds.org/news/slender-billed-curlew-extinct/)

**Eskimo curlew (*Numenius borealis*)**
- The last confirmed records were Galveston, Texas in 1962 (photographed) and Barbados in 1963 (specimen collected). No reliable sighting since 1987. IUCN lists it as Critically Endangered (Possibly Extinct). — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eskimo_curlew); [Audubon](https://www.audubon.org/news/the-eskimo-curlew-hasnt-been-seen-55-years-it-time-declare-it-extinct)

**South Island kōkako (*Callaeas cinereus*)**
- Declared extinct in 2007. DOC reclassified it as "Data Deficient" in 2013 after a reported sighting. There is no definitive evidence since 1967, or since 1937 by some accounts. Unconfirmed reports continued in 2024 (Southland) and later (Nelson). — [NZ Birds Online](https://www.nzbirdsonline.org.nz/species/south-island-kokako); [RNZ](https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/national/508073/excitement-over-possible-sighting-of-elusive-south-island-kokako-in-southland); [Stuff](https://www.stuff.co.nz/nz-news/360759478/could-be-return-south-island-kokako-nelson-sighting-sparks-fresh-hope)
- Dec 2025: Te Papa DNA-tested a putative kōkako feather collected in 1986 and found it matched a blackbird. — [Te Papa blog 5 Dec 2025](https://blog.tepapa.govt.nz/2025/12/05/dna-identification-of-a-putative-south-island-kokako-feather/)

**Thylacine**
- Brook et al. 2023 (*Science of the Total Environment*) analysed 1,237 records from 1910 onward. The most likely extinction window is the 1940s–1970s, possibly as late as the late 1980s to early 2000s, with "a very small chance" it persists in the southwest. — [Inspiring Tasmania](https://inspiringtas.org.au/2023/04/tasmanian-tiger-may-have-survived-into-the-2000s-new-analysis-suggests/)
- **No sound recording exists.** It was described as mostly silent, with a quick "yip-yip" bark when hunting. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thylacine). Its voice can only be tagged DESCRIBED or INFERRED.

**Other species not heard since**
- Himalayan quail: the last record is 1876 near Nainital. A 2010 study estimates extinction in the late 1890s. — [artensterben.de](https://www.artensterben.de/en/himalayan-quail/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Himalayan_quail); [bioRxiv 2020 search attempt](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2020.07.14.201137.full.pdf)
- Pink-headed duck: the last specimen is often given as 1935, with unconfirmed reports until 1949. — [species-extinction.com](https://www.species-extinction.com/pink-headed-duck/); [eBird](https://ebird.org/species/pihduc1)
- Imperial woodpecker: the only film is an 85-second 16 mm clip shot by William L. Rhein in Durango in 1956. Cornell analysed it in 2011 (*The Auk*). — [Cornell Chronicle 2011](https://news.cornell.edu/stories/2011/10/study-analyzes-film-imperial-woodpecker); [The Auk paper](https://academic.oup.com/auk/article/128/4/671/5148599). The film is silent, so the voice must come from historical descriptions or be inferred from *Campephilus* relatives.

**"Possibly extinct" frameworks**
- Butchart, Stattersfield & Collar 2006 (*Oryx*) found that 16 bird species would probably have gone extinct in 1994–2004 without conservation. Their mean minimum population rose from 34 to 147. — [Cambridge PDF](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/6011F6BE0D5BF3A7A91DDF3F7DB2A8DA/S0030605306000950a.pdf/how-many-bird-extinctions-have-we-prevented.pdf); [BirdLife case study](https://datazone.birdlife.org/sowb/casestudy/over-ten-years-action-by-birdlife-partners-and-others-prevented-the-extinction-of-16-bird-species)
- Butchart et al. 2018 (*Biological Conservation*), "Which bird species have gone extinct? A novel quantitative classification approach", made these recommendations:
  - Reclassify as Extinct: cryptic treehunter (last seen 2007), Alagoas foliage-gleaner (2011) and poʻouli (2004).
  - Reclassify Spix's macaw as Extinct in the Wild.
  - Treat as Critically Endangered (Possibly Extinct): New Caledonian lorikeet, Javan lapwing, Pernambuco pygmy-owl and glaucous macaw.
  - Source: [Mongabay Sept 2018](https://news.mongabay.com/2018/09/8-species-of-birds-have-possibly-gone-extinct-over-past-few-decades/); [Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/study-says-last-decade-4-birds-went-extinct-and-4-more-are-likely-gone-180970226/)
- Search for Lost Birds (American Bird Conservancy, Re:wild, BirdLife) defines a species as "lost" when it has no documented photo, video or audio for 10 or more years and is not assessed as EX or EW. The first analysis found 144 species (1.2% of all birds). A later count is 126. The data came from Macaulay Library, iNaturalist and xeno-canto, about 42 million media records, and was published in *Frontiers in Ecology and the Environment*. — [Global Wildlife/Re:wild](https://assets.globalwildlife.org/web/ffda9899912fc985/new-analysis-reveals-126-birds-are-lost-to-science-and-haven-t-had-a-confirmed-sighting-in-at-least-a-decade); [ABC 2023](https://abcbirds.org/news/abc-results-2023-preventing-extinctions)

**Rediscoveries ("Lazarus" species)**
- Takahē: rediscovered 20 Nov 1948 by Geoffrey Orbell in the Murchison Mountains, Fiordland. — [DOC](https://doc.govt.nz/our-work/takahe-recovery-programme/the-takahe-story); [Te Ara](https://teara.govt.nz/en/photograph/20091/takahe-rediscovered)
- Cahow (Bermuda petrel): rediscovered Jan 1951, including by 15-year-old David Wingate, after about 330 years without a record. — [Birds Outside My Window](https://www.birdsoutsidemywindow.org/2010/09/07/rare-bird-rediscovering-the-cahow/)
- Jerdon's courser: rediscovered 1986 in Cuddapah (Kadapa), Andhra Pradesh, after more than 80 years. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerdon%27s_courser); [Search for Lost Birds](https://searchforlostbirds.org/birds/1625)
- Night parrot: John Young's 2013 Queensland evidence included the first known recordings of its call. They were withheld to protect the birds and released in Feb 2017. Young's later claims (a 2018 WA recording) were discredited, but the 2013 rediscovery is accepted. Leseberg et al. 2019 published the known vocalisations. — [Audubon](https://audubon.org/news/john-young-rediscovered-australian-night-parrot-did-he-lie-about-his-later); [Leseberg et al. 2019 PDF](https://conservationpartners.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/Descriptions-of-known-vocalisations-of-the-Night-Parrot-Leserberg-et-al-2019.pdf); [All About Birds](https://www.allaboutbirds.org/news/a-new-dawn-for-the-night-parrot/)
- Black-browed babbler: rediscovered 2020 in South Kalimantan (Borneo). Before that it was known from one specimen collected in the 1840s. — [Search for Lost Birds](https://searchforlostbirds.org/birds/1688)
- Dusky tetraka: rediscovered Dec 2022 (Masoala) and Jan 2023 (near Andapa), Madagascar, after 24 years. — [BirdLife Mar 2023](https://www.birdlife.org/news/2023/03/01/found-mysterious-songbird-rediscovered-in-madagascar-after-eluding-scientists-for-24-years/)
- Santa Marta sabrewing: lost for 64 years, photographed in 2010, lost again, and rediscovered July 2022 by Yurgen Vega. — [ABC Birds](https://abcbirds.org/?p=34392)

### Inferences
- **Proposed labelling scheme.** It has two axes plus a date, so it does not overload the RECORDED / DESCRIBED / IMITATION / INFERRED provenance tag.
  - **Axis A, provenance** (already planned): how the voice is known.
  - **Axis B, status verdict**, one of:
    - `EXTINCT (declared YYYY, by body)`, e.g. slender-billed curlew: IUCN/BirdLife 2024. Bachman's warbler: USFWS 2023.
    - `POSSIBLY EXTINCT`: the IUCN CR(PE) tag, e.g. Eskimo curlew, New Caledonian lorikeet, Pernambuco pygmy-owl.
    - `CONTESTED`: an active formal dispute, e.g. the ivory-billed woodpecker (USFWS decision pending since 2021).
    - `DATA DEFICIENT / UNCONFIRMED REPORTS`: e.g. South Island kōkako.
    - `LOST`: the Search for Lost Birds criterion of no media for 10+ years, with no extinction verdict.
    - `EXTINCT IN THE WILD`, and `EXTIRPATED HERE` for topic 2.
    - `REFOUND (year)`: Lazarus species.
  - **Date field:** "last certain voice" (year and place of the last accepted recording or record), kept separate from the verdict year.
  - Draw the status from IUCN/BirdLife first, with the national verdict (USFWS, DOC) second, and show both when they disagree. The ivory-bill needs exactly this: IUCN CR(PE), USFWS Endangered and pending, NatureServe Presumed Extinct.
- **Rendering "lost then found":** a refound species could be heard in the lost world only for its gap years. The takahē would be faint from about 1898 to 1948, then graduate to the living world. It could also carry a distinct timbre, such as a "returned" shimmer. Rediscoveries often produced the *first* recordings (night parrot 2013), so the provenance tag can change over time from DESCRIBED to RECORDED. Showing that transition is itself a statement.
- For CONTESTED species, the "secret traces" at last-known places (Singer Tract 1935–44, Cache River 2004–05, Louisiana 2019–23) should use only the accepted 1935 recording material and label later claims as claims. Rendering the Project Principalis audio as ivory-bill would take a side in a live dispute.
- A soft rule: render CONTESTED and POSSIBLY EXTINCT species a little *louder* than declared-extinct ones at their last places. This honours the possibility that they are still there, without asserting it.

### Gaps
- The USFWS final ivory-bill decision was scheduled for Sept 2025. I found no Federal Register final action or withdrawal notice. My web search budget was exhausted before checking the Fall 2025 or 2026 agendas. **Check [reginfo.gov RIN 1018-BC98](https://www.reginfo.gov/public/do/eAgendaViewRule?pubId=202504&RIN=1018-BC98) and the Federal Register before stating the current status.**
- Not verified this session: Kauaʻi ʻakialoa (last record commonly given as 1969), Jamaican petrel (last specimen 1879, possibly surviving), Negros fruit dove (known from one 1953 specimen), New Caledonian owlet-nightjar (specimens 1880 and 1915, reported 1998), Short-tailed albatross (declared extinct about 1949, refound 1950–51 on Torishima). These dates are from memory only. Confirm on the BirdLife datazone or IUCN Red List pages.
- I did not confirm whether Butchart et al. 2018 recommendations such as the poʻouli have since been adopted by IUCN. The poʻouli and cryptic treehunter were reportedly reclassified EX in 2019–2021, but this is unverified here.
- The details of the Project Principalis 2022/2023 paper (Latta et al., *Ecology and Evolution*) were not fetched.

## 2. Extirpations: regional losses that matter for a place-based soundscape

### Takeaway
Many "restorable" voices belong to species that still live elsewhere. That makes them the best-documented class for the artwork: they can be RECORDED from living conspecifics, placed with a known loss date, and given a return date where one exists. Loss dates for Britain and Ireland are mostly well bounded. Dates for single "last individual" events, such as the Scottish wolf in 1680 versus 1743, are folklore-laden and should be labelled that way.

### Cited Findings

**Britain and Ireland**
- Wolf in England: extinct during Henry VII's reign (1485–1509). Scotland: the official "last" was killed by Sir Ewen Cameron in 1680. The 1743 MacQueen wolf is considered doubtful folklore. Ireland: the last wild wolf is said to have been killed in 1786. — [Wikipedia: Wolves in Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolves_in_Ireland); [The Scotsman](https://www.scotsman.com/arts-and-culture/books/exploring-the-history-of-when-the-wolf-last-roamed-scotland-4579593); [Countryfile](https://www.countryfile.com/wildlife/britains-extinct-beasts)
- Brown bear in Britain disappeared "between 500 & 1000 CE". Eurasian lynx "around 400 CE". Eurasian beaver 1526. Beaver reintroduction began with a government trial in 2009, about 500 years later. — [Wildlife Trusts](https://www.wildlifetrusts.org/extinct-british-wildlife); [Woodland Trust](https://www.woodlandtrust.org.uk/blog/2020/08/the-uks-extinct-animals-can-we-bring-them-back/); [Wikipedia list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_extinct_animals_of_the_British_Isles)
- Birds in Britain:
  - Common crane lost by the early 1600s. It has recently returned.
  - Capercaillie lost in the mid-17th or mid-18th century (sources differ) and later brought back to Scotland.
  - Great bustard lost in the 1840s. It is being re-established on Salisbury Plain.
  - White-tailed eagle wiped out in the early 20th century. Reintroduction began in Scotland in 1975 and in England in 2019.
  - Great bittern lost in 1886 and recolonised in 1911.
  - Sources: [Wildlife Trusts](https://www.wildlifetrusts.org/extinct-british-wildlife); [Discover Wildlife](https://www.discoverwildlife.com/animal-facts/animals-making-a-comeback-in-britain)
  - Note the conflict on capercaillie: the Wildlife Trusts list gives "mid 17th century" and another source says "mid-18th century". The standard literature date is about 1785 in Scotland, with reintroduction in 1837, but I did not verify this.

**North America**
- Bison: pre-Columbian estimates range from 30 to 60 or 65 million and are contested. Hornaday's 1889 count was about 1,091, of which 541 were in captivity, 256 were wild plains bison and about 294 were wood bison. The "541" figure often quoted is the captive count, not the wild count. — [Wikipedia: The Extermination of the American Bison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Extermination_of_the_American_Bison); [Univ. Arizona Rangelands PDF](https://repository.arizona.edu/bitstream/handle/10150/639069/11258-10800-1-PB.pdf?sequence=1&isAllowed=y)
- California grizzly: last reliable wild sightings in 1924. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_grizzly_bear); [Backpacker](https://www.backpacker.com/stories/issues/environment/the-california-grizzly-vanished-100-years-ago-it-could-be-coming-back/)
- Whooping crane: the last migratory flock at Aransas, Texas fell to 15 birds in 1941. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whooping_crane); [Audubon](https://www.audubon.org/magazine/these-historic-photos-show-groundbreaking-campaign-save-last-whooping-cranes)
- California condor: the last wild bird was captured in 1987, leaving 27 in captivity. All living condors descend from these 27. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_condor); [Bloom Biological](http://bloom.bio/news/2021/4/16/the-last-wild-condor); [NPS Grand Canyon](https://www.nps.gov/grca/learn/nature/condor-reintroduction-and-recovery.htm)

**Asia, Middle East and Europe**
- Cheetah in India: the Maharaja of Surguja shot the last three in 1947 in Koriya (Chhattisgarh). The species was declared extinct in 1952. Eight *African* cheetahs from Namibia arrived at Kuno NP on 17 Sept 2022. The Asiatic subspecies survives only in Iran. — [VOA](https://www.voanews.com/amp/cheetahs-back-in-wild-in-india-after-seven-decades/7001041.html); [Lonely Planet](https://www.lonelyplanet.com/articles/india-reintroducing-cheetahs); [BirdGuides](https://www.birdguides.com/news/cheetah-reintroduced-to-india/)
- Tigers: the last Bali tiger specimen was shot on 27 Sept 1937. The Caspian tiger persisted until the 1970s (other sources give the 1950s). The last Javan tiger evidence was footprints in Meru Betiri in 1976. — [Wikipedia Bali tiger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bali_tiger); [Caspian tiger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caspian_tiger); [Javan tiger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Javan_tiger)
- Arabian oryx: the last wild herd was killed or captured on 18 Oct 1972. It was reintroduced in Oman from 1982. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabian_oryx); [Arabian oryx reintroduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabian_oryx_reintroduction); [PMC genetics review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8924751/)
- Przewalski's horse: declared Extinct in the Wild by the end of 1969, later reintroduced to Mongolia, China and Kazakhstan. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Przewalski's_horse); [PMC review 2022](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9686875/)
- Lions: gone from Greece and the rest of Europe by about 80–100 AD. The last lion in Iraq died in 1918. The last in Iran was seen by railway workers in 1942. — [What is missing?](https://www.whatismissing.org/timeline/lions-europe-asia-the-middle-east/overview); [ResearchGate figure](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Last-lion-sightings-and-extinctions-according-to-the-written-record-The-framed-area-is_fig2_250007417)

### Inferences
- **Implementation idea:** for each extirpated taxon, store a polygon of the historical range plus a `lost` year range (earliest to latest plausible) and an optional `returned` year. Voices come from living populations and are tagged RECORDED (conspecific). For 17th-century Scotland, wolves draw on living European *Canis lupus* howls, cranes on living *Grus grus*, and capercaillie on the living species.
- **What they sounded like.** These notes are from general natural-history knowledge and were not source-verified this pass. Confirm against Birds of the World, xeno-canto or Macaulay.
  - Wolf: long howls with a fundamental around 150–1,000 Hz, often in choruses at dusk and night in winter and early spring.
  - Common crane: loud, rolling bugled duets that carry kilometres, at spring dawn.
  - Bittern: a very low "boom", fundamental roughly 150–200 Hz, repeated 3–5 times at dawn and dusk in spring. It was folk-famous in the fens.
  - Capercaillie: a quiet lek display in four phases (clicks, a "cork-pop", then whetting and gurgling) before dawn in April–May. Its low frequency and low volume make it a good "faint" voice.
  - Great bustard: display is largely silent, apart from occasional grunts and bark-like calls. It is a visual species and adds little to the soundscape.
  - White-tailed eagle: yelping "klee-klee" series, often duetted.
  - Brown bear and lynx: rare vocalisations. Bears huff and growl. Lynx give caterwauling rut calls in Feb–March, a good seasonal "trace".
  - Beaver: tail-slaps on water, gnawing, and occasional whines from kits. It is the sound of a changed hydrology rather than a voice.
  - Bison: low grunts and bellows in the rut (July–Aug). Large herds made a roar audible from far away, according to historical accounts I did not verify here.
  - Whooping crane: bugling unison calls.
  - Condor: largely silent (hisses and grunts). Its presence is wing-rush and not voice.
  - Lion: roaring bouts at dusk and night that carry about 5–8 km.
  - Tiger: "aaonh" roars, chuffing and prusten.
  - Cheetah: chirps and purrs, not roars.
  - Walrus: bell-like knocks and whistles underwater, bellows on haul-outs.
  - Gray whale: low moans and knocks.
- Grading by knowledge: extirpated species with living conspecifics are "fully known voices" (RECORDED). Their faintness should come from *presence probability at that date*, not from uncertainty about how they sound.
- Several items stand at the extinct/extirpated boundary and belong on both sides: Bali and Javan tigers (extinct subspecies) and the Atlantic gray whale (an extinct population).

### Gaps (unverified in this session; dates are from memory, so check them)
- Wolves elsewhere:
  - Contiguous US: largely extirpated by the 1930s–1940s, except Minnesota.
  - Mexican wolf: extinct in the wild by about the 1970s–80s, with releases from 1998.
  - Western and central Europe: Denmark about 1813, Germany about 1904, with natural return since about 2000.
- Jaguar in the US Southwest: the last known female was shot in Arizona in 1963, and males have been photographed in AZ/NM since 1996.
- Eastern elk: Pennsylvania about 1867, with the subspecies declared extinct in 1880.
- Sea otter: fur trade from 1741, protected in 1911 when an estimated 1,000–2,000 remained.
- Walrus in the Gulf of St Lawrence (Magdalen Islands): extirpated by the late 1700s.
- Atlantic gray whale: extinct around the 17th–18th century.
- Lions in India outside Gir: largely gone by the 1880s. The Gir population was about 20 or fewer around 1913, a figure that is disputed.
- Capercaillie: Scotland about 1785, reintroduced in Taymouth in 1837.
- Crane in Britain: return to the Norfolk Broads in 1979, and the Great Crane Project releases from 2010.

## 3. Bird population decline: numbers for an optional "density" setting

### Takeaway
There are rigorous biome-level multipliers for North America (1970–2017), continental totals for Europe (1980–2017) with a strong farmland signal, and one study (Morrison 2021) that translated the declines directly into acoustic diversity and intensity. Morrison's abstract gives no headline percentage for the acoustic change, so a density multiplier should be driven by the abundance numbers and not by an invented soundscape "loudness %".

### Cited Findings

**North America: Rosenberg et al. 2019, *Science* 366:120–124, Table 1 (1970–2017, 529 species)**

**Europe**

The two studies use different species sets and geographies (Rigal includes non-EU countries). — [CNRS](https://www.cnrs.fr/en/node/7688); [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10235941); [CREAF](https://www.creaf.cat/en/articles/intensive-farming-main-cause-bird-population-decline-europe)

**Insects and the biophony**
- Hallmann et al. 2017 (*PLOS ONE*): flying insect biomass in 63 German protected areas fell **76% seasonally and 82% in midsummer** over 27 years (1989–2016). — [Sussex repository](https://sro.sussex.ac.uk/id/eprint/70873/); [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2017-10-percent-decrease-total-insect-biomass.html)
- Bernie Krause, Lincoln Meadow (Sierra Nevada, about 6,700 ft):
  - He recorded in June 1988 before "selective logging" and exactly one year later under the same conditions.
  - The density and diversity of the biophony, including mountain quail, finches, sparrows, sapsuckers and frogs, had collapsed.
  - It had not recovered on about 15 later visits.
  - Sources: [TED Ideas](https://ideas.ted.com/listen-to-the-haunting-impact-that-humans-have-had-on-the-natural-world/); [MIT Press Reader](https://thereader.mitpress.mit.edu/everything-is-wrong-bernie-krauses-concept-of-biophony); [NPR](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/216100454)

### Inferences
- **Suggested density multipliers (proportion of the historical value).**
  - North America, using Rosenberg: grassland ×0.47, boreal ×0.67, western forest ×0.70, eastern forest ×0.83, arctic ×0.77, aridland ×0.83, coasts ×0.85, wetlands ×1.13, shorebirds ×0.63, aerial insectivores ×0.68 (swallow, swift and nightjar layers), waterfowl ×1.56.
  - Europe, using Rigal/PECBMS: farmland about ×0.43, all species about ×0.75. Using Burns: EU/UK all about ×0.82.
  - Interpolate linearly from 1970 (NA) or 1980 (EU) to about 2017. After 2017, extend the trend using the 2025 State of the Birds (continued decline, ducks now falling). Before 1970, **there is no systematic baseline**, so a slider reaching back to 1500 needs a separate, explicitly INFERRED ramp. Tie it to documented collapses: passenger pigeon, bison, whooping crane, Eskimo curlew, market hunting in 1870–1918.
- Morrison shows that sound loss is not proportional to count loss. The multiplier should thin *songbird complexity* (warblers, larks, thrushes) more than *loud simple callers* (corvids, gulls), and it should keep waterfowl.
- In Europe the loss is weighted toward farmland (house sparrow, skylark). Applied to a British farmland dawn in 1980 compared with 2020, the change should be clearly audible.

### Gaps
- Morrison 2021: exact year spans (roughly NA 1996–2017 and Europe 1998–2018), site counts per continent, and per-region effect sizes were not retrieved because the full text was paywalled or behind a captcha. **Do not quote a specific % soundscape decline without the paper.**
- Rigal 2023: exact per-habitat percentages were not extracted. Commonly cited values, which I did not verify, are farmland −56.8%, urban −27.8%, forest −17.8%, all −25.3%. Check the [PMC text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10235941).
- I did not find post-2017 biome-level updates for North America in 2025–2026 (such as an updated "3 Billion Birds" analysis). The State of the Birds 2025 is species-list based.

## 4. Archives, precedents and ethics

### Takeaway
Recordings of now-extinct birds are few and concentrated. The Macaulay Library holds most of the iconic ones. For some culturally central species the "recording" is a human imitation: the huia, recorded by Hēnare Hāmana in 1948/49. There are strong precedents for speculative reconstruction (Te Papa 2006, Sandia 1997, Humeau), but reconstructions are often presented without explicit iwi consultation or clear uncertainty labels. For Māori and Hawaiian taonga species, Te Mana Raraunga and CARE imply that consultation and attribution are expected, not optional.

### Cited Findings

**Archives**
- Macaulay Library (Cornell) holds more than 1 million audio recordings, some from as early as 1929. Among likely-extinct birds it has recordings of Bachman's warbler (1954, Allen & Kellogg), ivory-billed woodpecker (1935, Allen), bridled white-eye, Kauaʻi ʻōʻō, kāmaʻo (large Kauaʻi thrush) and poʻouli. — [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/digital-sound-archives-can-bring-extinct-birds-briefly-back-to-life-176115); [EarthSky](https://earthsky.org/earth/extinct-bird-songs-woodpecker-warbler-kauai-oo/)
- Kauaʻi ʻōʻō: David Boynton made the last known recording in 1987 of a solitary male, whose mate was likely lost after Hurricane Iwa in 1982. The recording is in the Macaulay Library. Its "cultural afterlife" includes widespread online circulation and radio features. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaua%CA%BBi_%CA%BB%C5%8D%CA%BB%C5%8D); [Hawaiʻi Public Radio "Manu Minute"](https://www.hawaiipublicradio.org/podcast/manu-minute/2020-12-16/manu-minute-the-last-kauaˊi-ˊoˊo); [BirdNote/KNKX](https://www.knkx.org/birdnote/2014-12-31/birdnote-rare-sounds-saved-by-macaulay-library)
- **Huia:** in 1948 or 1949 Robert Batley and Hēnare Hāmana of Moawhango recorded Hāmana's imitation of the male and female calls on a disc, at a Wellington studio. Sources differ on the studio (2YA or Columbus). Hāmana had guided the 1909 huia search. The original disc is at Whanganui Regional Museum, and Ngā Taonga holds 1960s tape copies. Ngā Taonga "frequently receives requests from overseas artists" to use it, and reuse enquiries go to sound@ngataonga.org.nz. — [Ngā Taonga](https://www.ngataonga.org.nz/blog/nz-history/the-call-of-the-huia/); [NZ Geographic](https://www.nzgeo.com/stories/huia); [Ngā Taonga catalogue: Re-creation of huia calls](https://ngataonga.org.nz/search-use-collection/search/26325)
- Other archives (Tierstimmenarchiv Berlin, British Library, FNJV, xeno-canto) were not verified this session for extinct-species holdings. See Gaps.

**Reconstructions and artistic precedents**
- Te Papa commissioned recreations of five extinct NZ birds' calls, made Dec 2005 to Mar 2006 by musicians Jeremy Coubrough and Bevan Smith with Te Papa scientists, for an April 2006 exhibition.
  - For the moa they used emu and cassowary analogues, tracheal-ring anatomy, and objects such as a ceramic fish and a Nerf gun, pitched down and filtered. The Haast eagle followed in Te Taiao (2019).
  - The article does not mention iwi consultation. It cites Herries Beattie's early-20th-century interviews with South Island kaumātua about moa calls.
  - Its conclusion: "we will likely never know."
  - Sources: [The Spinoff Apr 2025](https://thespinoff.co.nz/pop-culture/22-04-2025/how-a-nerf-gun-and-a-ceramic-fish-helped-bring-to-life-the-lost-call-of-the-moa); [Te Papa collections topic](https://collections.tepapa.govt.nz/topic/1444)
- Parasaurolophus: a skull found in 1995 in New Mexico was CT-scanned, and Sandia and the NM Museum simulated a low, resonant, pitch-varying call, announced in 1997. — [Sandia news release](https://newsreleases.sandia.gov/scientists-use-digital-paleontology-to-produce-voice-of-parasaurolophus-dinosaur/); [Sandia Lab News 19 Dec 1997](https://www.sandia.gov/labnews/1997/12/19/dinosaur-story/)
- Marguerite Humeau's *Back Here Below / Resuscitating Prehistoric Creatures* (about 2011–12) built speculative vocal tracts for Lucy and a mammoth. — [NPR 2011](https://www.npr.org/2011/07/23/138644143/helping-mammoths-roar-again); [Dezeen](https://www.dezeen.com/?p=226206)
- A dodo "song" reconstruction was admitted by its maker to be "a fantasy", built from dove analogues. — [Earbirding blog](https://earbirding.com/blog/?p=316) (secondary source, low authority)
- *Songs of Disappearance* (Bowerbird Collective with BirdLife Australia, 3 Dec 2021) presented 53 threatened Australian birds from David Stewart's recordings. It debuted at #5 on the ARIA chart and reached #2, and proceeds went to BirdLife Australia. — [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/how-our-album-of-birdsong-recordings-rocketed-to-2-on-the-aria-charts-177070); [The Music Network](https://themusicnetwork.com/songs-of-disappearance-aria-top-five/)
- Katie Paterson's *Vatnajökull (the sound of)* (2007–08) was a live phone line to a microphone in Jökulsárlón lagoon. It received about 3,200 calls from 47 countries in its first week. — [Katie Paterson](https://katiepaterson.org/?p=198); [NPR 2007](https://www.npr.org/2007/07/29/12317438/artist-records-glaciers-sounds-from-the-deep)
- Marcus Coates' *Dawn Chorus* (2007) slowed birdsong by up to 20 times so human singers could imitate it, then sped the footage back up. It is a 14- or 7-channel video installation. — [Marcus Coates](https://marcuscoates.co.uk/projects/68-dawn-chorus); [Arts Council Collection](https://artscouncilcollection.org.uk/artwork/dawn-chorus)

**Indigenous data sovereignty and cultural context**
- Te Mana Raraunga, the Māori Data Sovereignty Network, was founded in 2015. Its 2018 principles are Rangatiratanga (authority), Whakapapa (relationships), Whanaungatanga (obligations), Kotahitanga (collective benefit), Manaakitanga (reciprocity) and Kaitiakitanga (guardianship). Māori data includes information "about or from Māori people, our language, culture, resources or environments". — [Te Mana Raraunga principles](https://www.temanararaunga.maori.nz/principles-of-maori-data-sovereignty); [TMR 2018 PDF](https://online.op.ac.nz/assets/LearningTeachingDevelopment/TMR+Maori+Data+Sovereignty+Principles+Oct+2018.pdf)
- The CARE Principles (Collective benefit, Authority to control, Responsibility, Ethics) come from the Global Indigenous Data Alliance and were published in the *Data Science Journal* in 2020. They build on Te Mana Raraunga, the US Indigenous Data Sovereignty Network, Maiam nayri Wingara and others. — [Carroll et al. 2020, Data Science Journal](https://account.datascience.codata.org/index.php/up-j-dsj/article/view/dsj-2020-043)
- Hawaiʻi: Kamehameha's ʻahu ʻula was made mainly of yellow mamo feathers with some red ʻiʻiwi, and no other cloak with so many mamo feathers is known. It came to Bishop Museum in 1893 as one of three sacred ʻahu ʻula of the Kamehameha line. Rare birds were a sacred resource, and catchers sometimes released birds after plucking. — [Bishop Museum "Wings"](https://www.bishopmuseum.org/online-learning-center/wings/); [Hawaiʻi Alive](https://www.hawaiialive.org/resource/bird-catching)

### Inferences (recommendations for respectful practice)
1. **Consult before voicing taonga species.** For Aotearoa this means huia, kōkako, moa, whēkau (laughing owl), piopio and others. For Hawaiʻi it means ʻōʻō, mamo, kāmaʻo and others. Contact Ngā Taonga for the Hāmana recording, the relevant iwi (Hāmana's Moawhango community and Ngāti Huia–linked iwi for huia), and Hawaiian cultural practitioners or Bishop Museum. Record who was asked, what they said, and any conditions, and show a credit line such as "voiced with the guidance of …". If consultation is not possible, the default should be *silence or a non-voiced marker* at those places rather than a synthesis.
2. **Use a separate IMITATION tag with a human credit.** Hāmana's whistles are human knowledge of the bird and not the bird. Credit the imitator by name ("after Hēnare Hāmana, 1948/49") and do not present the result as a huia recording.
3. **Keep synthesis clearly labelled "imagined" wherever it goes beyond evidence.** Te Papa's own makers say "we will likely never know". Show the analogue species used (e.g. "moa, inferred from cassowary and emu") in the provenance line.
4. **Use Indigenous names first, or paired,** with diacritics (ʻōʻō, kōkako, huia). Use place names from the relevant Indigenous gazetteers where the artwork displays places.
5. **Protect sensitive locations.** The night parrot recordings were withheld for four years to protect birds. "Secret traces at last-known places" must not reveal locations for CONTESTED or refound species (night parrot, possible kōkako sites, Louisiana ivory-bill sites). Blur them to about 50–100 km or to the region.
6. **Give something back** (CARE: Collective benefit, and Manaakitanga). Link each voice to the archive and recordist (Macaulay catalogue numbers, Ngā Taonga IDs). Consider directing viewers to conservation or cultural organisations, following the *Songs of Disappearance* model of donating proceeds to BirdLife Australia.
7. **Avoid "resurrection" rhetoric in the framing text.** The sounds are memory and research, not de-extinction. That matters especially where companies market de-extinction (e.g. ivory-bill genetic-engineering pitches reported by [ABC News](https://abcnews.com/US/ivory-billed-woodpecker-extinct-genetic-engineering-company-aims/story?id=114352833)).
