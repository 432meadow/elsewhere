# Lost birds of Europe, Africa, Asia, Australia and the Indian and Pacific Ocean islands that are missing from the existing catalogue

Scope note. Lost since c. 1500; Hawaiʻi (incl. Laysan/Midway) and Aotearoa New Zealand (incl. Chathams, Kermadecs, Macquarie's NZ-affiliated fauna where noted) excluded. Taxa already implemented or already catalogued in `_already_covered.md` are not re-researched unless new sound evidence turned up. All bounding boxes are `[lon0, lat0, lon1, lat1]` and **every one was estimated by the researcher from the place names in the sources** (marked "est.") — none was taken from a published range polygon.

Method note. Web search quota was exhausted early in the session (WebSearch 200/200 used; Brave rate-limited; DuckDuckGo blocked after one query). The sweep was therefore done by downloading the wikitext of Wikipedia's "List of extinct bird species since 1500" and c. 150 species pages, the Search for Lost Birds list, Birds of the World (BoW) free introduction pages (extinct-species accounts are paywalled; lost/CR ones are partly open), and non-English Wikipedias (de, es, ja, ko). Macaulay Library and xeno-canto could not be queried, so "recorded" below means "the species/population has living or captive birds that are known to be recordable", not "I verified a catalogue number", unless stated.

Provenance tiers used: **RECORDED** (real recordings of the taxon or of the same species elsewhere exist or must exist because the species is alive); **DESCRIBED** (an eyewitness put the sound into words); **INFERRED** (nothing known; living model only).

## 1. Which lost bird taxa in these regions are absent from the covered list?

### Takeaway
About 110 lost bird taxa (species, subspecies and regionally extirpated populations) in the assigned regions are in neither the implemented nor the previously-catalogued lists; only around 20 of them have any voice evidence at all, and eight stand out as clearly worth adding (Javan lapwing, Andalusian buttonquail, northern bald ibis in Europe/Near East, crested ibis in Japan/Korea, the Mascarene "boeuf" booby, Rodrigues rail, Wake Island rail, Siberian crane in India). No confirmed post-1500 bird extinction was found for Palau, Yap, Chuuk, Pitcairn/Henderson or (apart from the Tanna ground dove) Vanuatu.

### Inferences
- The earlier report's "Borodino thrush" does not exist as a taxon: it is almost certainly a conflation of the "Bonin island thrush" and the "Borodino bush warbler (*Cettia diphone restrictus*, Daito islands)", which appear on adjacent lines of a widely copied list of Japan's extinct birds — [Facts and Details](https://factsanddetails.com/japan/cat26/sub164/item891.html). Borodino = Daitō; the warbler's type locality is "Minami-daito-jima, Borodino Islands" — [Birds of the World](https://birdsoftheworld.org/bow/species/jabwar/cur/introduction).
- The biggest untapped group is not the long-extinct island birds (almost all voiceless) but regional extinctions of species that are still alive and recordable elsewhere (torillo, Waldrapp, toki, kōnotori, Siberian crane, sacred ibis in Egypt, åga on Guam, Javan pied myna). They let the artwork use a real acoustic model with no speculation, on a range where the sound is truly gone.

### Gaps
- BirdLife Data Zone and IUCN Red List were not queried directly (search quota); status labels below come from Wikipedia taxoboxes, BoW and the Lost Birds list.
- recentlyextinctspecies.com was sampled for only two taxa; it gave bibliography but no voice text.
- I could not check whether any Easter Island (Rapa Nui), Nauru or Kiribati land-bird losses post-date 1500; none appears in the list apart from Coues's gadwall and the Kiritimati sandpiper (already catalogued).
- Australian *regional* extirpations of extant species (e.g. bush stone-curlew and magpie goose in the south-east) were not researched; they may be worth a separate pass.

## 2. For each: is the voice RECORDED, DESCRIBED, or only INFERRED?

### Takeaway
Real recordings exist only where the species survives elsewhere or in captivity (ibises, stork, cranes, buttonquail, francolin, Abbott's booby, Mariana crow, the Javan cage-birds); a handful of extinct taxa have genuine eyewitness words (Javan lapwing, Rodrigues rail, Rodrigues owl, Mascarene booby, Mascarene grey parakeet, Wake rail, Aldabra brush warbler, Waldrapp per Gessner); everything else, including every item the earlier report listed as "not researched" except the Rodrigues rail, is inference from relatives.

### Cited Findings

**The earlier report's "not researched" items**
- Mascarene coot: INFERRED. Dubois (1674) and others describe tame "water-hens" covering the pools at Saint-Gilles and Étang de St Paul, gone by 1667; no sound — [Mascarene coot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mascarene_coot)
- Réunion swamphen "oiseau bleu": INFERRED. Accounts give colour, size, running speed and the Plaine des Cafres habitat, nothing on voice; the species is formally "hypothetical" (no bones) — [Réunion swamphen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Réunion_swamphen)
- Réunion kestrel: INFERRED. Dubois mentions it took domestic chickens; no voice — [Réunion kestrel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Réunion_kestrel)
- Rodrigues rail: DESCRIBED. Tafforet (1726): "Their cry is a continual whistling. When they see somebody pursuing them they produce another sort of noise from their bodies, like that of a person who has hiccups and with the stomach tensed." Pingré (1761) heard of none surviving — [Rodrigues rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rodrigues_rail)
- Rodrigues pigeon: INFERRED. Leguat: so tame that "we have had fifty about our table"; nested on offshore islets; no voice — [Rodrigues pigeon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rodrigues_pigeon)
- Mauritius night heron: INFERRED. Only Leguat's 1693 "great flight of bitterns" — [Mauritius night heron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mauritius_night_heron). Rodrigues night heron: INFERRED; Leguat and Tafforet describe tameness, running and lizard-eating only — [Rodrigues night heron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rodrigues_night_heron)
- Réunion owl: INFERRED (bones only) — [Réunion scops owl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Réunion_scops_owl). Rodrigues owl: DESCRIBED (behaviourally). Tafforet (1726): "they stay almost always in the trees, and when they feel the good weather; they sing at night; and always the same song on the contrary when they feel the bad weather they are not heard" — [Rodrigues scops owl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rodrigues_scops_owl)
- Palau, Yap, Chuuk: nothing to voice — no post-1500 extinctions listed — [Wikipedia list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_extinct_bird_species_since_1500)
- Daitō bush warbler: first collected 1922 on Minami-daitō by Orii; no record after a 1984 sighting on Kita-daitō and considered extinct; yet a 2008 survey describes nests and eggs — [ja.wikipedia ダイトウウグイス](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ダイトウウグイス). Voice INFERRED/RECORDED-as-species (Japanese bush warbler, whose "distinctive breeding song" is a national herald of spring) — [Japanese bush warbler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_bush_warbler)
- "Borodino thrush": not a real taxon (see Inferences in section 1).
- Tonga: all INFERRED (Tongatapu rail and Vavaʻu rail known from a lost specimen and drawings) — [Tongatapu rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tongatapu_rail); [Vavaʻu rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vava%27u_rail)
- Pitcairn: nothing post-1500. Vanuatu: Tanna ground dove INFERRED; the only field note is Forster's "a dove of a new [kind] was shot" — [Tanna ground dove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanna_ground_dove)

**Other DESCRIBED voices of extinct or probably extinct taxa**
- Javan lapwing: the richest description found. Late-1920s notes (August Spennemann) give "several different rattling calls … reminiscent of toy trumpets, and often given during evening, both from ground and in flight, e.g. 't[r]et t[r]et t[r]et t[r]ett[r]t!', with faintly audible 'r' sounds, then 'tat tat tat tat - - tahhhhht!' alternated with 'trrrek - terrrr!' or just 'trrrrrrr!'; a repeated 'krahhh!' and 'ahhh!', or 'trahhhhwu' before take-off; an angry 'krihhh!' in flight; and a non-rattling 'tetetititit' in group context"; "vocalizations thought to be closest to *V. miles*"; local people in E Java reported "krek, krek, krek" — [Birds of the World: Javan Lapwing](https://birdsoftheworld.org/bow/species/sunlap1/cur/introduction), after [van Balen & Nijman 2007](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0959270907000810)
- Mascarene booby: "was called a Boeuf (= bull or ox) due to its call being comparable to a bull bellowing (Tafforet 1725-26; Pingré 1763)" — search-result excerpt attached to [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/230057973_The_biology_of_Abbott%27s_Booby_Sula_abbotti) (see Gaps: the citing paper itself was not opened)
- Mascarene grey parakeet: Dutch sailors (1598–1602): "if one is caught and made to cry out, soon hundreds of the birds fly around ones' ears"; "squeezed it till it screamed, there came all the others"; a 1601 verse says the parrot is spared "that he may peep and howl, And thus his fellows to imprisonment decoy" — [Mascarene grey parakeet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mascarene_grey_parakeet)
- Wake Island rail: "Its call consisted of a gentle cluck or a low chattering sound"; Wetmore (1923) found it "very curious, but quick to flee into cover" — [Wake Island rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wake_Island_rail). The species monograph has a "Vocalizations" section that I could not open — [Olson & Rauzon 2011](https://doi.org/10.1676/11-029.1)
- Aldabra brush warbler: "The song was never recorded but the call was a nasal, three-syllable chirrup"; "most readily located by its chirruping call" (Sinclair & Langrand 2003) — [Aldabra brush warbler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aldabra_brush_warbler)
- Waldrapp, 16th century: Gessner reports that it arrives with the storks, "that its voice is ka ka or kä kä and that it utters these sounds chiefly when it has young, that is in the time after Pentecost" (my translation of the German) — excerpt of the paper "Conrad Gessner's 'Waldrapp'" at [docplayer.org](https://docplayer.org/42027515-Conrad-gessner-s-waldrapp.html) (search snippet only; page fetch failed)
- Manipur bush quail: "Both sexes give clear, whistled 'whit-it-it-t-t', with notes becoming higher, louder and running together" — [Birds of the World: Manipur Bush-Quail](https://birdsoftheworld.org/bow/species/mabqua1/cur/introduction)
- Red-throated lorikeet (kulawai): "short, high-pitched shrill notes uttered while feeding or in flight" — [Birds of the World](https://birdsoftheworld.org/bow/species/retlor1/cur/introduction)
- Siau scops owl: "a rolling, two-note 'Ook-grrrrah' that lasts c. 0·7 seconds and is repeated regularly" (Eaton et al. 2016) — [Birds of the World](https://birdsoftheworld.org/bow/species/sulsco2/cur/introduction); but the species is "only known from a single holotype from 1866", with a purported 2017 video — [Siau scops owl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siau_scops_owl)
- Buff-breasted buttonquail: female "booming 'ooom-oom-oom', which becomes gradually louder … higher-pitched and shorter, being repeated up to 20 times and lasting up to 30 seconds", male "deep, rapid 'chu-chu-chu'" — [Birds of the World](https://birdsoftheworld.org/bow/species/bubbut1/cur/introduction); targeted surveys 2018–2022 found none while finding four other buttonquail species, "suggesting that some reports" were misidentifications — [Buff-breasted buttonquail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buff-breasted_buttonquail)
- Coxen's fig parrot: "In flight its call is harsher and more staccato than that of the little lorikeet"; silent when feeding — [Coxen's fig parrot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxen%27s_fig_parrot)
- New Caledonian owlet-nightjar: voice "unknown"/"not described", but in 1996 Daniel Letocart "heard unusual calls comparable to that of an Australian Owlet-Nightjar" in the Tchamba headwaters; similar calls reported by residents in 1996 and 1998 — [New Caledonian owlet-nightjar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Caledonian_owlet-nightjar); [BoW](https://birdsoftheworld.org/bow/species/nconig1/cur/introduction)
- Raiatea starling (Forster 1774): "a soft, fluting voice" — [Raiatea starling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raiatea_starling) (probably the taxon already implemented as "raiatea thrushes"; listed here only to prevent duplication)

**RECORDED (species alive elsewhere or in captivity), with published voice descriptions**
- Common/Andalusian buttonquail: "The female calls with a deep hoom-hoom-hoom and the male replies kek-kek-kek" — [Common buttonquail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_buttonquail); the female "emits a sound that recalls the lowing of cattle" — [A. López Baena](https://www.albertolopezbaena.me/naturaleza-muerta/la-coleccion/torillo-andaluz-turnix-sylvaticus/); the female gives the advertising/territorial song, males are much quieter — [es.wikipedia Turnix sylvaticus](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turnix_sylvaticus)
- Northern bald ibis: "gives guttural hrump and high, hoarse hyoh calls at its breeding colonies, but is otherwise silent"; male advertises "giving low rumbling calls" — [Northern bald ibis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_bald_ibis); pairs bow to each other "with loud Chrup-Chrup calls" — [de.wikipedia Waldrapp](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waldrapp)
- Crested ibis: Japanese sources give "tāa", "guā", "ka' ka'" — a turbid, crow-like, nasal voice; Ono Ranzan's *Honzō kōmoku keimō* says flocks calling were very noisy — [ja.wikipedia トキ](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/トキ). Korean: "ttaok ttaok" or "kkak-kkak", a hoarse crow-like sound — [ko.wikipedia 따오기](https://ko.wikipedia.org/wiki/따오기). "Generally solitary and quiet, vocalizing mainly when taking flight" — [Crested ibis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crested_ibis)
- Oriental stork: courtship by throwing the head back and bill-clattering (クラッタリング) — [ja.wikipedia コウノトリ](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/コウノトリ)
- Siberian crane: "very different from the trumpeting of most cranes and is a goose-like high pitched whistling toyoya"; "most calls have a dominant frequency of about 1.4 kHz"; pairs duet, the female giving the higher "loo" of a "doodle-loo" — [Siberian crane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siberian_crane)
- Black francolin: "a loud ringing 'klik cheek-cheek-cheerakik', 'kik-kik-kik' or 'kwee-kweeeee-kwee' … mornings and evenings and almost all day during the breeding season"; male calls from a mound or low branch "and is soon joined by other birds answering from all directions" — [Black francolin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_francolin)
- Abbott's booby (model/same species as the Mascarene "boeuf"): "very vocal, with a wide range of calls" — [Abbott's booby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbott%27s_booby)
- Mariana crow: "a high-pitched series of one to three caw or hi sounds, or a series of longer caw sounds with a nasal aaa element"; alarm "a rapid series of sharp caws"; also "squalling, guttural sounds" — [Mariana crow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariana_crow)
- Micronesian megapode: "a loud 'keek', song often a duet with one bird beginning a rising and accelerating 'keek-keek-keek-keek' … culminating in a loud 'kee-keer-kew' (Palau) or 'keek-keer-keet' (Marianas), the other answering with a rising cackle that slows near the end" — [Micronesian megapode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micronesian_megapode)
- Bali myna: "a variety of sharp chattering calls and an emphatic tweet" — [Bali myna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bali_myna)
- Rufous bristlebird (model for the extinct western subspecies): loud singers with "repertoires of between 12 and 30 different song-types", no sharing between sexes — [Rufous bristlebird](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rufous_bristlebird)
- Cebu flowerpecker (refound 1992; not lost): "seep-seep-seep tik-tik-tik-tik", the tik "like … striking together two pebbles" — [Birds of the World](https://birdsoftheworld.org/bow/species/cebflo1/cur/introduction)

**Explicitly unknown voices**
- White-eyed river martin: "silent when wintering, and its breeding vocalisations are unknown" — [White-eyed river martin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White-eyed_river_martin); "Nothing recorded" — [BoW](https://birdsoftheworld.org/bow/species/wermar2/cur/introduction)
- Sulu bleeding-heart: "vocalizations have never been described" — [Sulu bleeding-heart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulu_bleeding-heart). Negros fruit dove: "Voice unknown" — [BoW](https://birdsoftheworld.org/bow/species/nefdov1/cur/introduction). Ua Pou monarch: "Insufficient information; probably very like that of *P. mendozae*" — [BoW](https://birdsoftheworld.org/bow/species/uapmon1/cur/introduction)
- No voice statement at all was found for: Amsterdam wigeon, Marianne white-eye, spectacled cormorant, Réunion fody, Mascarene teal and sheldgeese, Tasmanian emu, Norfolk thrush and triller, Macquarie parakeet, Daito wren and tit, Ryukyu kingfisher, Tanna and thick-billed ground doves, red-moustached fruit dove, Eiao/Nuku Hiva monarchs, Marquesas swamphen, Mangareva reed warbler, Moorea sandpiper, Coues's gadwall, Guam/Pagan/Aguiguan reed warblers, Guam rufous fantail, pied raven, Cyprus dipper — respective Wikipedia pages, e.g. [Norfolk thrush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norfolk_thrush), [Spectacled cormorant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectacled_cormorant), [Nightingale reed warbler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nightingale_reed_warbler)

### Inferences
- For the "recorded" tier I did not verify any specific archive catalogue number. That recordings exist for northern bald ibis, crested ibis, Oriental stork, Siberian crane, common buttonquail, black francolin, Abbott's booby, Mariana crow, Micronesian megapode, Bali myna and Javan pied myna is my inference from the fact that these are living, studied (and mostly captive-bred) birds; the report writer should word it as "recordable / recordings of the living species exist" rather than cite an archive.
- For the Aldabra brush warbler, the Wikipedia phrase "the song was never recorded" most likely means "never documented", i.e. no tape exists. I found no evidence of any sound recording made by Prŷs-Jones in 1974–76.
- The Siau scops owl "Ook-grrrrah" description sits oddly with a species known from one 1866 skin; it should be treated as unverified (possibly from an unconfirmed modern encounter) until the Eaton et al. 2016 source is read.

## 3. Dossiers for the viable candidates (names, range, habitat, timing, last record, status, acoustic detail, model, sensitivities)

### Takeaway
Fourteen candidates have enough for a grounded voice; eight are strong. The full field set the brief asked for is given per candidate below; where a field is my own estimate or background knowledge it is labelled and repeated under Inferences.

### Cited Findings

**3.1 Javan lapwing — *Vanellus macropterus*** (Indonesian name not verified)
- Provenance: DESCRIBED (rich, 1927–28 field notes); call transcriptions quoted in section 2 — [BoW](https://birdsoftheworld.org/bow/species/sunlap1/cur/introduction)
- Range: two discrete areas of Java — north coast of the west, south coast of the east; Spennemann's sites Poponcol and Tegallurung lie c. 75 km east of the previous easternmost north-coast locality — [van Balen & Nijman 2007](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0959270907000810). Bbox est.: W Java north coast `[106.6, -6.6, 108.4, -5.9]`; E Java south coast `[112.6, -8.5, 114.4, -8.0]`
- Habitat: "open areas near freshwater ponds and densely overgrown swamps with rushes/sedges", wet cattle pasture, abandoned paddyfields with fishponds backed by mangroves — [BoW](https://birdsoftheworld.org/bow/species/sunlap1/cur/introduction)
- Timing: calls "often given during evening, both from ground and in flight"; breeding May–June in W Java, adults mobbing Brahminy kites into July–August; territorial even in November in E Java — [BoW](https://birdsoftheworld.org/bow/species/sunlap1/cur/introduction)
- Abundance: mostly pairs or singles; groups of 4–6 (six is the largest recorded); "observed daily" and common at Spennemann's sites in 1927–28 — [van Balen & Nijman 2007](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0959270907000810)
- Last: "last recorded in 1940" — [van Balen & Nijman 2007](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0959270907000810); BoW gives 1938–39 on the south coast; Lost Birds gives last documented 1939 — [Search for Lost Birds](https://www.searchforlostbirds.org/birds). Status: Critically Endangered, "almost certainly extinct" — [BoW](https://birdsoftheworld.org/bow/species/sunlap1/cur/introduction)
- Model: masked lapwing *Vanellus miles* (stated closest vocally) — [BoW](https://birdsoftheworld.org/bow/species/sunlap1/cur/introduction)
- Synthesis cues: harsh rattling notes with rolled r; accelerating series ending in one long note; drawn "krahhh/ahhh"; a fast non-rattling "tetetititit" for groups; dusk emphasis; toy-trumpet timbre.

**3.2 Andalusian buttonquail / torillo andaluz — *Turnix sylvaticus sylvaticus***
- Provenance: RECORDED as a species (extant in Morocco, sub-Saharan Africa, Asia); voice quoted in section 2.
- Range lost: western Mediterranean coasts of Portugal, Andalusia, Sicily, Libya, Tunisia, Algeria, Morocco; now a single population in <5,000 ha of market gardens on the Atlantic coast of Morocco — [es.wikipedia](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turnix_sylvaticus). Bbox est. (Andalusian littoral, Doñana–Cádiz): `[-7.5, 36.0, -5.3, 37.4]`; Sicily est. `[12.4, 36.6, 15.7, 38.3]`
- Last: last physical evidence from hunters' bags around El Rocío (Doñana) in the late 1970s–early 1980s; 1990s reports rested on "positive listenings" without recordings — [es.wikipedia](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turnix_sylvaticus). Declared extinct in Spain 2018, 37 years after the last documented record — [SEO/BirdLife](https://atlasaves.seo.org/ave/torillo-andaluz/); described as the first bird extinction in Europe in a century and a half — [es.wikipedia](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turnix_sylvaticus)
- Behaviour: role-reversed; female brighter, sings and holds territory, males incubate — [A. López Baena](https://www.albertolopezbaena.me/naturaleza-muerta/la-coleccion/torillo-andaluz-turnix-sylvaticus/)
- Current: on 9 Sept 2026 eighteen chicks were flown from Morocco to the Jerez zoo to begin captive breeding for reintroduction — [El País, 17 Sept 2026](https://elpais.com/clima-y-medio-ambiente/2026-09-17/aves-de-marruecos-para-recuperar-una-especie-extinguida-en-espana-el-periplo-de-18-torillos-hasta-llegar-a-jerez.html)
- Synthesis cues: female a deep, soft, repeated "hoom" likened to distant cattle lowing (the Spanish name means "little bull"); male a dry "kek-kek-kek".

**3.3 Northern bald ibis / Waldrapp, Klausrapp, Steinrapp (German); kelaynak (Turkish) — *Geronticus eremita*, lost European and Near-Eastern populations**
- Provenance: RECORDED as a species; plus a 16th-century DESCRIPTION from the lost population (Gessner: "ka ka / kä kä", mostly when it has young, after Pentecost; arrives with the storks) — [docplayer excerpt](https://docplayer.org/42027515-Conrad-gessner-s-waldrapp.html)
- Historic names Schopfibis, Klausrapp, Steinrapp, Klausrabe, Waldhopf; Gessner's is the first ornithological description — [de.wikipedia](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waldrapp)
- Range lost: Danube and Rhône valleys and mountains of Spain, Italy, Germany, Austria, Switzerland; nested on cliffs and castle battlements — [Northern bald ibis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_bald_ibis). Bbox est. (Alpine/Danubian core): `[6.0, 45.5, 16.5, 49.0]`. Birecik, Turkey est.: `[37.9, 36.9, 38.1, 37.1]`. Palmyra, Syria est.: `[38.0, 34.3, 38.6, 34.8]`
- Last: Europe — extinct for centuries (1504 Salzburg protection decree); Birecik wild migrants: three returned in 1989, one in 1990; Atlas migratory colonies gone by 1989 — [Northern bald ibis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_bald_ibis)
- Season: migratory; in Turkey a festival was held annually to celebrate the birds' return north — [Northern bald ibis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_bald_ibis)
- Status: Endangered; c. 147 breeding pairs wild in Morocco (2018) — [Northern bald ibis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_bald_ibis). Reintroduced migratory colonies now exist at Burghausen, Kuchl, Überlingen and Grünau (Waldrappteam) — [de.wikipedia](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waldrapp)
- Cultural: the Syrian birds were described as "the last living descendants of those depicted in Egyptian hieroglyphs" — [Northern bald ibis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_bald_ibis)
- Synthesis cues: colonial; guttural "hrump", high hoarse "hyoh", low rumbling by advertising males, "chrup-chrup" greeting duets at the nest ledge; silent away from the colony.

**3.4 Crested ibis — toki (朱鷺, Japan); ttaogi (따오기, Korea) — *Nipponia nippon*, lost Japanese/Korean/Russian populations**
- Provenance: RECORDED as a species (China, and reintroduced birds); voice words in section 2.
- Range lost: Japan (last Honshu stronghold Noto Peninsula; last of all, Sado Island), Korean peninsula (winter visitor), Russian Far East (Amur/Ussuri), most of China from Jilin to Fujian and Gansu — [ja.wikipedia](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/トキ). Bbox est.: Honshu/Sado `[130.5, 31.0, 142.0, 41.5]`; Sado alone `[138.2, 37.8, 138.6, 38.35]`; Korea `[125.0, 34.0, 129.6, 39.0]`
- Last: 11 wild birds in 1958 (6 Sado, 5 Noto); last Noto bird captured 1970, died 1971; last five wild Sado birds captured 11–23 Jan 1981; "Kin" died 10 Oct 2003 — [ja.wikipedia](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/トキ). Korea: last capture 1937 (Hamhung); sightings 1965, 1966 and 1978 (Panmunjom) — [ja.wikipedia](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/トキ)
- Status: Japan moved it from "Extinct in the Wild" to Critically Endangered in 2019 after releases; 2019 totals c. 2,600 China, 600 Japan, 363 South Korea — [ja.wikipedia](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/トキ); [ko.wikipedia](https://ko.wikipedia.org/wiki/따오기). First wild breeding of reintroduced birds in South Korea 2021 — [Crested ibis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crested_ibis)
- Habitat/behaviour: probes paddies and marsh mud for loach, crabs, frogs; nests in pines/oaks; small flocks outside breeding — [ko.wikipedia](https://ko.wikipedia.org/wiki/따오기)
- Cultural: Korea's 1925 children's song "Ttaogi" (words Han Jeong-dong, music Yun Geuk-yeong) — [ko.wikipedia](https://ko.wikipedia.org/wiki/따오기); an Akita folktale explains its nasal voice (a carpenter carved the bird and forgot to open its nostrils); Niigata bird-scaring songs name the toki among the "most hated birds" of the fields — [ja.wikipedia](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/トキ)
- Synthesis cues: hoarse, nasal, crow-like "taa / guaa / ka-ka"; given mostly at take-off and in flocks; "very noisy" in numbers.

**3.5 Mascarene booby "boeuf" — *Papasula abbotti nelsoni***
- Provenance: DESCRIBED (named for a bull-like bellow; Tafforet 1725–26, Pingré 1763 — see section 2); the species survives on Christmas Island (RECORDABLE model, same species).
- Range: Mauritius and Rodrigues. Bbox est.: Rodrigues `[63.30, -19.80, 63.50, -19.65]`; Mauritius `[57.30, -20.53, 57.80, -19.98]`
- Last: accounts 1668 (Mauritius), 1725–26 and 1761 (Rodrigues); extinct by the end of the 18th century — [Hume 2023](https://www.biotaxa.org/Zootaxa/article/view/zootaxa.5270.3.5)
- Habitat/behaviour (from the living form): nests near the top of emergent forest trees; pairs for life; one chick every two or three years; "very vocal" — [Abbott's booby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbott%27s_booby)
- Status: extinct subspecies (named 2023); species Endangered — [Abbott's booby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbott%27s_booby)

**3.6 Rodrigues rail / Leguat's gelinotte — *Erythromachus leguati***
- Provenance: DESCRIBED (Tafforet 1726: "continual whistling"; hiccup-like alarm — section 2).
- Range: Rodrigues. Bbox est. `[63.30, -19.80, 63.50, -19.65]`
- Behaviour: flightless, fat, fast runner; ate tortoise eggs; attacked anything red (Leguat 1708); "aggressive species" (Hume) — [Rodrigues rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rodrigues_rail)
- Last: present 1726; none reported to Pingré in 1761; extinct mid-18th century — [Rodrigues rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rodrigues_rail)
- Model: the already-implemented red rail of Mauritius is its sister-island counterpart; any living whistling rail can carry the "continual whistling".

**3.7 Wake Island rail — *Hypotaenidia (Gallirallus) wakensis***
- Provenance: DESCRIBED thinly ("gentle cluck or a low chattering sound") — [Wake Island rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wake_Island_rail)
- Range: Wake Atoll (Wake, Wilkes, Peale islets). Bbox est. `[166.58, 19.26, 166.67, 19.33]`
- Behaviour: flightless; "small groups of birds engaged in cooperative nesting and prolonged parental care", defending eggs and young from hermit crabs and rats — [Olson & Rauzon 2011 abstract](https://doi.org/10.1676/11-029.1); courtship late July, nesting mid-August; very curious — [Wake Island rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wake_Island_rail)
- Last: "Extinction of the rail occurred between 1942 and 1945 as a result of direct predation by thousands of starving Japanese troops and habitat destruction" — [Olson & Rauzon 2011 abstract](https://doi.org/10.1676/11-029.1); last record 1945 — [recentlyextinctspecies.com](https://recentlyextinctspecies.com/gruiformes-crakes-cranes-rails-etc/gallirallus-wakensis). Photographs exist (Torrey Lyons, Peale I., 1939) — [Rauzon et al., Atoll Research Bulletin 561](https://repository.si.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/25021f86-d2ab-4c54-b40d-ba7239815f65/content)
- Model: Guam rail, whose "most common vocalizations are short 'kip' notes, but also screeches during the breeding season" — [Guam rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guam_rail)

**3.8 Siberian crane in India (central flyway) — *Leucogeranus leucogeranus***
- Provenance: RECORDED as a species; acoustic numbers in section 2 (flute-like "toyoya", ~1.4 kHz dominant; duet).
- Range lost: wintering at Keoladeo (Bharatpur), formerly south to Nagpur and east to Bihar — [Siberian crane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siberian_crane). Bbox est.: Keoladeo `[77.48, 27.12, 77.56, 27.21]`; historical Indian winter range `[75.0, 21.0, 86.0, 28.5]`
- Last: only a pair arrived in winter 1998; "they left after the 2001-2002 winter never to return" — [The Wire Science](https://science.thewire.in/society/history/keoladeo-ghana-siberian-cranes-contemplating-extinction/); a 2026 report says the last pair arrived in 2001 — [Bhaskar English](https://www.bhaskarenglish.in/local/rajasthan/news/siberian-cranes-absent-from-bharatpur-hunting-along-migration-route-blamed-birds-moving-to-iran-india-declining-136889149.html)
- Western flock (Iran): down to four in 2002, later a single bird — [Siberian crane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siberian_crane); The Wire's headline "Omid No More" indicates that bird too is gone — [The Wire Science](https://science.thewire.in/society/history/keoladeo-ghana-siberian-cranes-contemplating-extinction/)
- Season: winter visitor only (Arctic breeder).

**3.9 Rodrigues owl — *Otus murivorus*** (B)
- DESCRIBED behaviourally: sang at night, "always the same song", only in fine weather; lived in trees, ate small birds and lizards — [Rodrigues scops owl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rodrigues_scops_owl). Extinct mid-18th century (not reported by Pingré 1761). Bbox as Rodrigues above. Model: a monotonous scops-owl note.

**3.10 Aldabra brush warbler — *Nesillas aldabrana*** (B)
- DESCRIBED: nasal three-syllable chirrup; shy, in dense undergrowth, found by call — [Aldabra brush warbler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aldabra_brush_warbler)
- Range: a 10 ha coastal strip of Malabar (Middle) Island, Aldabra. Bbox est. `[46.25, -9.40, 46.50, -9.33]`
- History: discovered 1967; six more birds (all males) ringed 1975; one male in 1983; extinction confirmed 1986; IUCN Extinct since 1994 — [Aldabra brush warbler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aldabra_brush_warbler)

**3.11 Mascarene grey parakeet — *Psittacula bensoni*** (B)
- DESCRIBED: a held bird's scream summoned "hundreds"; "peep and howl" — [Mascarene grey parakeet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mascarene_grey_parakeet). Mauritius and Réunion; fattest June–September on Réunion; extinct 1764. Model: echo parakeet.

**3.12 Oriental stork in Japan/Korea — kōnotori (コウノトリ); hwangsae (황새) — *Ciconia boyciana*** (A-/B+)
- RECORDED mechanical sound (bill-clattering). Lost: Japan 1971, South Korea 1971; later reintroduced (Toyooka; Yesan, with first wild-born Korean chicks 2016) — [ja.wikipedia](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/コウノトリ); [Oriental stork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriental_stork). Bbox est. Tajima/Wakasa: `[134.5, 35.2, 136.0, 35.8]`

**3.13 Black francolin in Sicily and at Lake Amik — *Francolinus francolinus* ssp.** (B+)
- RECORDED as a species; loud ringing advertising call (section 2). Sicilian form gone c. 1869; Lake Amik form (*billypayni*) 1960s — [Wikipedia list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_extinct_bird_species_since_1500). Bbox est.: Sicily `[12.4, 36.6, 15.7, 38.3]`; Amik `[36.2, 36.2, 36.6, 36.5]`

**3.14 Mariana crow (åga) and Micronesian megapode on Guam** (B+)
- RECORDED as species on Rota / Palau and northern Marianas; voices in section 2. ### Inferences
- Sensitivities and consultation (researcher's judgement, not sourced):
  - Tasmanian emu — Palawa words and dance are living cultural heritage; voicing it on lutruwita/Tasmania should involve the Tasmanian Aboriginal community, and Robinson's spellings should not be used as a title without that.
  - Marshall Islands annañ — exists only as Marshallese oral tradition; treat like the Choiseul pigeon (skipped earlier on Indigenous-informant grounds) unless consultation happens.
  - Wake Island rail — the extinction is bound up with the starvation of the Japanese garrison and the killing of POWs; needs a sober framing, no community consultation obviously required.
  - Crested ibis and Oriental stork — national symbols in Japan and Korea with active reintroductions; low risk, but the piece should not imply they are still extinct there (they are "returned"). Same for the Waldrapp in Bavaria/Austria and the torillo (reintroduction just starting in 2026).
  - Guam (åga, sasangat) — CHamoru names and an ongoing colonial/military-ecological grievance (brown tree snake); the catalogue already voices Guam birds, so follow whatever protocol was used there.
  - Bali myna / Javan songbirds — the cause of loss is the song-contest and cage-bird trade; voicing them as "lost from the forest but singing in cages" is accurate and pointed, not sensitive.
- Suggested living models where the sources name none (researcher's judgement): Réunion swamphen → Madagascar/African swamphen; Mascarene coot → red-knobbed coot (the page says it was "more likely derived from the red-knobbed coot"); Réunion kestrel → Mauritius kestrel; night herons → black-crowned night heron; Rodrigues pigeon → Malagasy turtle dove / pink pigeon; Amsterdam wigeon → Eurasian wigeon (whistle) at reduced size; Marianne white-eye → Seychelles/Mayotte white-eye; spectacled cormorant → red-faced/pelagic cormorant colony groans; Norfolk thrush → island thrush (already modelled for Lord Howe); Daito wren and tit → Eurasian wren and varied tit; Tanna and thick-billed ground doves → Santa Cruz ground dove; red-moustached fruit dove → white-capped fruit dove; Marquesan monarchs → Fatu Hiva / Tahiti monarch; Guam reed warbler → Saipan reed warbler; Guam rufous fantail → the surviving Saipan/Rota rufous fantail; western rufous bristlebird → Coorong rufous bristlebird; white-eyed river martin → African river martin; Tasmanian emu → mainland emu drumming (as done for the island emus).
- Rough acoustic placement from my own background knowledge (unverified this session): buttonquail "hoom" is very low (a few hundred Hz), soft and ventriloquial; Abbott's booby gives deep, resonant bellows/grunts at the nest; stork clatter is broadband, c. 8–12 clacks per second; crested-ibis and bald-ibis notes are harsh and low-pitched, under ~1.5 kHz.

### Gaps
- Indonesian/Javanese vernacular name of the Javan lapwing; Chamorro names of the Guam fantail and megapode; Thai name of the river martin; Marquesan names of the fruit dove and monarchs — not verified in any fetched source (I recall "trulek jawa", "chichirika", "sasangat", "nok chao fa ying Sirindhorn", "kuku", but none is cited).
- Exact last-date of the Syrian Palmyra bald ibis colony and of the Sicilian buttonquail population — not found in fetched text.
- Season and time-of-day data are absent for most island taxa (Rodrigues rail, Wake rail, Aldabra warbler).
- No frequency measurements were found for any candidate other than the Siberian crane (1.4 kHz).
- Conflicting dates that the report writer should keep visible: Javan lapwing 1938–39 / 1939 / 1940; white-eyed river martin 1968 (documented) / 1978 (reliable) / 1980 / "late 1980s?"; Wake rail 1942–45 vs "1945"; Aldabra brush warbler 1983 (last seen) vs c. 1984 vs 1986 (confirmed); spectacled cormorant c. 1840 vs c. 1850; Tasmanian emu "mid-19th century" vs c. 1865 vs 1873 captives; Daitō bush warbler "extinct" vs 1984 sighting vs 2008 nests; Korean crested ibis 1978 (ja.wikipedia) vs the 1979 date often quoted; Manipur bush quail 1907 (documented) / 1932 / 2006; red-throated lorikeet 1977 (documented) / 1993 / 2001?; Ua Pou monarch 1922 (documented) / 1975 / c. 1986 / 2010 unconfirmed; Coues's gadwall 1874 vs c. 1900; Réunion kestrel c. 1672 vs c. 1700; Rück's blue flycatcher — the brief calls it "refound" but the Lost Birds list still shows it lost since 1918.

## 4. Ranking of candidates (A = recorded or richly described and clearly worth adding; B = described thinly; C = inferred only)

### Takeaway
Eight A candidates, about fifteen B, and the remainder (roughly 85 taxa) are C; the single best new find is the Javan lapwing, whose 1920s call transcriptions are detailed enough to synthesise directly, followed by the Andalusian "little bull" buttonquail and the Mascarene "boeuf" booby, both named for their voices.

### Inferences
- Within A, items 2, 3, 4 and 8 are regional extinctions of living species and need no speculative sound design; items 1, 5, 6 and 7 are true extinctions with words to work from. If only a few can be added, I would take 1 (Javan lapwing), 5 (boeuf), 2 (torillo) and 3 (Waldrapp) for the strength of evidence and the fact that three of the four are literally named after their sound.
- Items 3, 4 and Oriental stork are in the middle of successful reintroductions; framing them as "silent here for N years, now returning" is more accurate than "lost".
- Among C taxa, the most evocative non-acoustic material is: the Réunion fody (so numerous that "the houses are full of them, as ours are with flies"), the Rodrigues pigeon (fifty tame birds around Leguat's table), the Mauritius sheldgoose (flocks of 40–100, "very tame"), and the Mascarene coot ("water-hens" covering the pools in 1665, gone by 1667) — all could be voiced as abundance soundscapes using living congeners, if the project accepts pure inference — [Réunion fody](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Réunion_fody); [Rodrigues pigeon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rodrigues_pigeon); [Mauritius sheldgoose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mauritius_sheldgoose); [Mascarene coot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mascarene_coot)

### Gaps
- Ranking would change if the paywalled primary sources yield more: Olson & Rauzon (Wake rail), Prŷs-Jones (Aldabra warbler), Hume 2023 (boeuf), and any 1960s–80s Guam tapes of the rufous fantail or reed warbler could each move a taxon up a tier.
