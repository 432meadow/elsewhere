# Regional extirpations of still-living birds ("kin" voices) and candidates for the thin regions of the lost-sound catalogue

Conventions used throughout
- Boxes are `[lon0, lat0, lon1, lat1]` = west, south, east, north. **Every box in this file was estimated by me** from the place names in the cited sources; none is taken from a published range polygon. Treat them as rough placement aids.
- "Lost" = last breeding / last confirmed record in that region. "Back" = recolonised or reintroduced. A voice that is back may not belong in the mode; it is flagged each time.
- Rank: **A** = firm date + well-described or measured voice + still gone; **B** = returned, reintroduced or only partially lost; **C** = poorly dated or voice too weak/undocumented.
- A number of findings come from search-engine summaries of the linked page rather than from a full read of the page. These are marked "(search summary)". They should be re-checked before being quoted in public text.

## 1. Which bird voices have been extirpated from a country or region since ~1500, with a firm date and a distinctive, measured voice? (dated list with boxes, voice, abundance, culture)

### Takeaway
There are about a dozen bird "kin" voices that combine a firm loss date with a properly measured or at least sharply described sound; the strongest are the greater prairie-chicken's boom (≈299 Hz, 1.9 s) across the Canadian prairies and the eastern Midwest, the corncrake's rasp over lowland England, Wales and Northern Ireland, the Siberian crane's 1.4 kHz whistle in India (last 2002), the whooping crane across the northern prairies (last nests 1894 / 1922), and the thick-billed parrot in Arizona (1938). Most of the famous European cases (bittern, crane, capercaillie, white-tailed eagle, white stork) are firmly dated but have returned, so they are B-rank "absence with an end date" rather than standing silences.

### Cited Findings

#### Europe

**Corncrake (*Crex crex*) — lowland Britain and Northern Ireland**
- Voice, measured: each call is two syllables; each syllable has 14–22 amplitude pulses, pulse-to-pulse duration 7–13 ms (pulses 3–5 ms, gaps 4–8 ms); minimum frequency 1.2 ± 0.31 kHz, maximum 8.1 ± 0.83 kHz, energy quartiles 3.4 / 5.0 / 6.1 kHz; natural source level 95 ± 5 dB at 1 m; calling studied 22:00–04:00 — [Budka & Osiejuk 2014, PLoS One](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4121243). Note: this corrects the brief's "~2 pulses" — it is 2 syllables, each a burst of 14–22 pulses, with most energy between about 3.4 and 6.1 kHz.
- Carries up to 1.5 km; "may be repeated more than 20,000 times a night, with a peak between midnight and 3 am" — [Wikipedia: Corn crake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corn_crake)
- Loss: bred in all parts of Britain and Ireland in the Victorian era; vanished from southern England and Wales by the 1940s; survived in the north of mainland Britain until the 1970s; mainland populations "dwindled away" in the 1980s–1990s. Decline tracked the arrival of horse-drawn mowers area by area (search summary) — [RSPB science blog](https://community.rspb.org.uk/ourwork/b/science/posts/what-has-thirty-years-of-conservation-done-for-scottish-corncrakes)
- Ireland: about 4,000 pairs in the 1968–72 atlas; 1,200–1,500 pairs in 1978; 903–929 in 1988; 189 calling males in the Republic in 1993; "virtually extinct in Northern Ireland"; Irish population down 96% since the 1970s (search summary) — [NPWS corncrake framework](https://www.npws.ie/sites/default/files/publications/pdf/A%20Framework%20for%20Corncrake%20Conservation%20to%202022%20(Nov2015).pdf); [Northern Ireland Priority Species](https://www.habitas.org.uk/priority/species.asp?item=23); [Irish Times 2022](https://www.irishtimes.com/environment/2022/08/08/farmers-help-to-save-cornrakes-the-bird-whose-call-was-the-sound-of-summer-for-generations/)
- England status today: listed as "extirpated in most England/Wales", with "ongoing reintroduction" — [Wikipedia: List of extinct animals of the British Isles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_extinct_animals_of_the_British_Isles)
- Netherlands: 33 territories in 1996, at least 500 by 1998 (so not a clean loss there) — [Wikipedia: Corn crake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corn_crake)
- Culture: Andrew Marvell's "Upon Appleton House" (1651), John Clare's "The Landrail"; proverbial for a grating voice; songs by The Pogues and The Decemberists — [Wikipedia: Corn crake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corn_crake). The Irish Times headline calls it "the bird whose call was the sound of summer for generations" — [Irish Times](https://www.irishtimes.com/environment/2022/08/08/farmers-help-to-save-cornrakes-the-bird-whose-call-was-the-sound-of-summer-for-generations/)
- Boxes (est.): southern England + Wales `[-5.5, 50.0, 1.8, 53.0]` (gone by 1940s); northern mainland England `[-3.6, 53.0, 0.2, 55.8]` (gone 1970s–1990s); Northern Ireland `[-8.2, 54.0, -5.4, 55.3]` (virtually extinct, 1990s).

**Eurasian bittern (*Botaurus stellaris*) — Britain, 1886–1911 (back)**
- Last eggs taken in Norfolk 1868; last proof of breeding (downy young) 1886, Norfolk; booming heard again in the Broads in 1900; breeding proved again at Sutton Broad, Norfolk, 1911 (search summary) — [British Birds: Breeding Bitterns in Britain](https://britishbirds.co.uk/journal/article/breeding-bitterns-britain); [RSPB England](https://community.rspb.org.uk/ourwork/b/rspb-england/posts/rspb-england-bird-of-the-decade-bittern)
- Numbers after return: about 80 booming males by 1954; 11 males in 1997; 228 booming males in 2021 — [Wikipedia: Eurasian bittern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurasian_bittern); [RSPB England](https://community.rspb.org.uk/ourwork/b/rspb-england/posts/rspb-england-bird-of-the-decade-bittern)
- Voice: boom mostly 100–150 Hz (studied in the 100–200 Hz band); a boom has a first and a main element, and the first boom's main element is preceded by bill-clapping and a series of "pumps"; sequence of pumps and booms varies between individuals (search summary) — [Frontiers in Robotics and AI 2022](https://frontiersin.org/journals/robotics-and-ai/articles/10.3389/frobt.2022.854572/text); [Ornis Fennica](https://ornisfennica.journal.fi/article/download/133651/82197); [Gilbert/McGregor: individually distinctive bittern booms](https://bioacoustics.info/article/individually-distinctive-bittern-booms-potential-census-tool)
- "Easily audible from a distance of five kilometres on a calm night"; given mainly January–April; folk names "bog-bull", "butter bump", "barrel-maker" — [Wikipedia: Eurasian bittern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurasian_bittern)
- Box (est.): Norfolk Broads / East Anglian fens `[0.0, 52.2, 1.8, 53.0]`.

**Common crane (*Grus grus*) — Britain c. 1600–1979 (back); other countries**
- Britain: extinct as a breeder by 1600; recolonisation traced to 15 September 1979, two birds near Hickling Broad, Norfolk; first young raised 1982 (search summary) — [Wikipedia: Cranes of Great Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cranes_of_Great_Britain)
- Ireland: a pair bred in 2021 "for the first time on the island in 300 years"; Hungary: extirpated as breeder "by 1952"; Italy: "around 1920"; Spain: last bred 1954, a pair bred again 2017; Austria: gone 1885, back 2018; Denmark: back 1953 "about a century after it had disappeared" — [Wikipedia: Common crane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_crane)
- Voice: "loud trumpeting call, given in flight and display", "piercing and can be heard from a considerable distance"; pairs give a synchronised unison call — [Wikipedia: Common crane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_crane)
- Culture: central to Irish folklore despite extinction; sacred to Hephaestus; the Rajasthani song "Kurja" — [Wikipedia: Common crane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_crane)
- Boxes (est.): East Anglian fens `[-0.5, 52.2, 1.8, 53.2]`; Ireland `[-10.0, 51.5, -6.0, 55.3]`; Hungary `[16.1, 45.7, 22.9, 48.6]`; Po plain/Veneto `[10.5, 44.5, 13.5, 46.0]`.

**Western capercaillie (*Tetrao urogallus*) — Scotland 1770–1785 (reintroduced); Ireland 18th century (still gone)**
- Scottish native population extinct between 1770 and 1785; present birds are an introduced Swedish stock. Ireland: "common until the 17th century, but was extirpated in the 18th century" — [Wikipedia: Western capercaillie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_capercaillie). Listed as "1780s, reintroduced" for the British Isles — [Wikipedia: British Isles extinct list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_extinct_animals_of_the_British_Isles)
- Voice: "a series of double-clicks like a dropping ping-pong ball, which gradually accelerate into a popping sound like a cork coming off a champagne bottle, which is followed by scraping sounds"; given "at the very beginning of dawn", March–June — [Wikipedia: Western capercaillie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_capercaillie)
- Measured: a low-frequency component with fundamental 28.7 ± 1.2 Hz (25.6–31.6 Hz) overlaps 96% of the "whetting" phase and is hardly audible beyond several metres — [Hart et al. 2020, PeerJ](https://peerj.com/articles/9189). A separate study reported no infrasound in the vocalisations but strong sub-20 Hz energy from flutter-jumps (search summary) — [J. Ornithology 2005](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10336-005-0003-y)
- Boxes (est.): Scottish Highlands `[-5.5, 56.3, -2.5, 57.8]`; Ireland `[-10.0, 51.5, -6.0, 55.3]`.

**White-tailed eagle (*Haliaeetus albicilla*) — Scotland 1916/1918, Ireland 1898 (both back)**
- Scotland's last breeding 1916; last British bird shot in Shetland 1918; Ireland's last breeding 1898; reintroduction to Rum from 1975 (82 Norwegian birds 1975–1985) (search summary) — [RSPB](https://www.rspb.org.uk/whats-happening/return-a-celebration-of-white-tailed-eagles); [BirdGuides: Back from the brink](https://www.birdguides.com/articles/back-from-the-brink--no-13). Ireland reintroduction 2007; England 2019 — [Wikipedia: White-tailed eagle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White-tailed_eagle)
- Voice: male "gri-gri-gri" or "krick-krick-krick", female deeper "gra-gra-gra-gra"; 15–30 calls per sequence, rising in tempo and pitch — [Wikipedia: White-tailed eagle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White-tailed_eagle)
- Boxes (est.): Scotland incl. Shetland `[-7.6, 55.5, -0.8, 60.9]`; Ireland `[-10.5, 51.4, -5.5, 55.4]`.

**White stork (*Ciconia ciconia*) — Sweden c. 1955, Switzerland 1950, Belgium 1895, Denmark 2008, Netherlands 1991 (mostly back)**
- Sweden: "the last pair failed to breed around 1955"; Switzerland: last wild individual 1950; Belgium: last wild individual 1895; Netherlands: 1991; Denmark: a few tens of pairs in 1974 "and none in 2008"; Britain: no nest between 1416 (St Giles, Edinburgh) and 2020; since reintroduced to many regions — [Wikipedia: White stork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_stork)
- Voice: "noisy bill-clattering, which has been likened to distant machine gun fire", amplified by the throat pouch as a resonator; grows louder the longer it lasts — [Wikipedia: White stork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_stork)
- Boxes (est.): Skåne, Sweden `[12.8, 55.4, 14.4, 56.3]`; Swiss plateau `[6.8, 47.0, 9.6, 47.8]`; Denmark `[8.1, 54.8, 12.6, 57.5]`.

**Northern bald ibis / Waldrapp (*Geronticus eremita*) — central Europe c. 1630–1645; Turkey wild 1989–1992; Syria 2015; Algeria late 1980s**
- Gessner described and illustrated Swiss birds in 1555; "vanished around 1630–1645" from Graz/Steiermark and Salzburg; "disappeared from Europe over 300 years ago". Birecik (Turkey): about 3,000 birds in the 1930s, 400 in 1982, single birds returning 1989–1990, "extinct in the wild in Turkey as of 1992". Palmyra (Syria): 7 birds found 2002, none returned 2015. Algeria: last colony gone in the late 1980s — [Wikipedia: Northern bald ibis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_bald_ibis)
- Voice: "guttural hrump and high, hoarse hyoh calls at its breeding colonies, but is otherwise silent" — [Wikipedia: Northern bald ibis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_bald_ibis)
- Culture: Egyptian hieroglyph, "a holy bird and a symbol of brilliance and splendour"; in Birecik religious protection and an annual festival for its return — [Wikipedia: Northern bald ibis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_bald_ibis)
- Boxes (est.): Alpine arc Switzerland–Austria–S Germany `[7.0, 46.5, 16.0, 48.5]`; Birecik `[37.9, 36.9, 38.1, 37.1]`; Palmyra `[38.1, 34.4, 38.5, 34.7]`.

**Black francolin (*Francolinus francolinus*) — Sicily, Spain, Tuscany, Greece; gone by the mid-19th century**
- Introduced and naturalised from antiquity in Greece and from the 13th century in Sicily and Aragon; disappeared between the end of the 18th and the first half of the 19th century; the very last Italian birds were shot near Gela, Sicily (search summary) — [University of Pisa](https://old.unipi.it/index.php/english-news/item/5902-the-history-of-the-black-francolin-introducted-in-tuscany-by-lorenzo-the-magnificient); introductions "began at least 800 years ago", populations (Spain incl. royal estates, Balearics, Sicily, Tuscany, Algeria, Greece) "became extinct before 20th century"; recorded in Prado still lifes and in Cervantes and Quevedo — [EBD-CSIC on Forcina et al., Integrative Zoology](https://www.ebd.csic.es/en/outreach/news/black-francolins-history-reflects-historical-dominance-crown-aragon-mediterranean)
- Voice: "a loud ringing 'klik cheek-cheek-cheerakik'", heard mornings and evenings and "almost all day during the breeding season", from a mound, rock or low branch — [Wikipedia: Black francolin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_francolin)
- Caveat: these western Mediterranean populations were themselves human introductions (court game birds), so this is the loss of an 800-year naturalised voice, not a native one.
- Boxes (est.): Sicily `[12.4, 36.6, 15.6, 38.3]`; eastern Spain (Aragon–Valencia) `[-1.5, 38.0, 1.0, 42.0]`.

**Andalusian buttonquail / torillo (*Turnix sylvaticus sylvaticus*) — Spain; last 1981, declared extinct 2018**
- Last certain Spanish bird killed near Doñana on 3 December 1981 (skin at Estación Biológica de Doñana); declared extinct by Spain in 2018; survives in about 4,675 ha of coastal farmland in Morocco (search summary) — [MaghrebOrnitho 2018](https://magornitho.org/2018/11/andalusian-buttonquail-declared-extinct-spain/); [BirdGuides](https://www.birdguides.com/news/first-european-bird-extinction-for-170-years/?eid=230226)
- IUCN declared it extinct in Europe in 2021, "the first bird species to have become extinct in Europe since the great auk in 1852" — [Wikipedia: Common buttonquail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_buttonquail)
- Voice: female gives "a deep hoom-hoom-hoom", male replies "kek-kek-kek" — [Wikipedia: Common buttonquail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_buttonquail)
- Box (est.): coastal Andalusia / Doñana `[-7.0, 36.0, -5.5, 37.5]`.

**Black grouse (*Lyrurus tetrix*) — Denmark, c. 2000–2001**
- Formerly in Denmark; the Danish Ornithological Society has considered it extinct since 2001; bred on Jutland heaths such as Kongenshus Heath (search summary) — [Wikipedia: Black grouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_grouse); [Hærvejsmarchen](https://haervejsmarchen.dk/about-us/the-black-grouse). Only isolated remnants in Germany, France, Belgium and the Netherlands — [Wikipedia: Black grouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_grouse)
- Box (est.): Jutland heaths `[8.1, 55.3, 10.0, 57.5]`.

**Great bustard (*Otis tarda*) — Britain 1832, Sweden mid-19th century, Poland 1986**
- Britain: "the last bird was shot in 1832"; reintroduction on Salisbury Plain from 2004, over 100 birds by 2020 — [Wikipedia: Great bustard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_bustard). Sweden: extinct in the mid-19th century (search summary) — [SLU thesis](https://stud.epsilon.slu.se/7672/). Poland: last brood at Parnica, West Pomerania, 1986 (search summary) — [CMS Poland report](https://european-bustard.cms.int/sites/default/files/document/Poland.pdf). Western Germany lost it in the 19th century; about 4,000 in eastern Germany in the 1940s; it persists in Brandenburg (search summary) — [Brandenburg LfU](https://lfu.brandenburg.de/sixcms/media.php/land_bb_test_02.a.189.de/gro%C3%9Ftrappe_engl.pdf)
- Voice: "usually silent"; "deep grunts when alarmed", "booming, grunting and raucous noises" in display — [Wikipedia: Great bustard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_bustard)
- Boxes (est.): English chalk and brecks `[-2.5, 51.0, 1.5, 54.2]`; Skåne `[12.8, 55.4, 14.4, 56.3]`; NW Poland `[14.2, 52.5, 19.0, 54.5]`.

**Bearded vulture (*Gypaetus barbatus*) — Alps 1913/1930s (back 1987), Carpathians 1927**
- Shot in the Alps as late as 1913, "completely locally extinct in the Alps by the 1930s"; last Carpathian birds shot 1927; over 200 released in the Alps 1987–2015 — [Wikipedia: Bearded vulture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bearded_vulture)
- Voice: largely silent, "shrill whistles in their breeding displays and a falcon-like cheek-acheek call" at the nest — [Wikipedia: Bearded vulture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bearded_vulture)
- Boxes (est.): Alps `[6.0, 44.0, 14.0, 47.5]`; Carpathians `[22.0, 45.0, 26.5, 48.0]`.

**Smaller British losses (weaker voices or returned)**

- Turtle dove: UK population down 98% since the 1968–72 atlas (99% between 1967 and 2020); about 125,000 pairs around 1970, 2,100 pairs in 2021; range contracted 51%, away from the west and north (search summary) — [BTO](https://www.bto.org/our-work/science/publications/papers/status-uk-breeding-european-turtle-dove-streptopelia)

**Sweden**

- Black stork was lost as a Swedish breeder during the 20th century (search summary) — [SLU / AGRIS: Black Stork Back](https://agris.fao.org/search/fr/records/6474b064f2e6fe92b3634363)

#### Asia

**Siberian crane (*Leucogeranus leucogeranus*) — India (Keoladeo), last 2002; Iran, one bird**
- About 75 birds wintered at Bharatpur in 1974; a single pair in 1992; "the last bird was seen in 2002". Western flock: four birds in 2002, then one male, "Omid", wintering in Iran since 2006–07. Western/central birds bred in the Ob, Konda and Sossva basins — [Wikipedia: Siberian crane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siberian_crane)
- Voice: "very different from the trumpeting of most cranes", "a goose-like high pitched whistling toyoya", dominant frequency about 1.4 kHz; unison calls differ markedly between pairs — [Wikipedia: Siberian crane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siberian_crane)
- Culture: for Yakuts and Yukaghirs "a sacred bird associated with sun, spring and kind celestial spirits ajyy"; in Yakut epics shamans turn into white cranes — [Wikipedia: Siberian crane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siberian_crane)
- Boxes (est.): Keoladeo, Rajasthan `[77.45, 27.10, 77.60, 27.25]`; Ob–Konda–Sosva breeding grounds `[62.0, 59.5, 70.0, 65.5]`.

**Crested ibis / toki (*Nipponia nippon*) — Japan (wild 1981, last bird 2003, back 2008); Korea 1979 (back)**
- Last five wild Japanese birds captured on Sado in 1981; the last Japanese-born bird died in 2003 (search summary) — [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/explore/savingearth/?p=8187). "The last wild crested ibis in Japan died in October 2003"; 10 birds released on Sado 25 September 2008; nearly 500 on Sado by June 2022. Korea: last seen 1979 near the DMZ; first Korean wild breeding 2021. China: rediscovered 1981 in Shaanxi (Yangxian) — [Wikipedia: Crested ibis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crested_ibis)
- Voice: a loud "Ta-a" or "Car" resembling a large-billed crow; a quiet "gwa, gwa" between mates or before a flock takes off (search summary) — [Bird Research News (Japan)](https://www.bird-research.jp/1_shiryo/seitai/toki.pdf)
- Boxes (est.): Sado `[138.2, 37.8, 138.6, 38.35]`; Korean peninsula `[126.0, 34.5, 129.5, 38.5]`.

**Oriental stork (*Ciconia boyciana*) — Japan 1971 (back 2005); Korea (back)**
- The last wild bird died after rescue in Toyooka, Hyogo, in 1971; reintroduced at Toyooka in 2005; 558 in the wild across 13 prefectures (search summary) — [Asia News Network](https://asianews.network/once-extinct-in-japan-oriental-white-storks-steadily-breeding-amid-nationwide-efforts). First wild hatchling in 40 years in May 2007; Korea: first wild-born chicks 2016, about 130 wild birds — [Wikipedia: Oriental stork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriental_stork)
- Box (est.): Toyooka basin `[134.5, 35.3, 135.0, 35.7]`.

**Red-crowned crane / tancho (*Grus japonensis*) — Honshu, lost during Meiji (1868–1912); Hokkaido rediscovery 1924**
- In the Edo period it was found through eastern Japan, wintering on tidal flats in Tohoku and Kanto; Meiji-era hunting and wetland loss removed it; believed extinct in Japan until about 10 birds were found in the Kushiro wetlands in 1924; now about 1,800 (search summary) — [Nippon.com](https://www.nippon.com/en/japan-data/h02739/). "A permanent population no longer survives" on Honshu; a wintering site in paddy fields now within Tokyo persisted until the 19th century; said to live 1,000 years — [Wikipedia: Red-crowned crane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red-crowned_crane)
- Box (est.): eastern Honshu (Tohoku–Kanto) `[139.0, 35.0, 142.0, 41.0]`.

**Sarus crane (*Antigone antigone*) — Thailand 1968 (back 2011), Philippines late 1960s, Pakistan late 1980s**
- Last seen in the wild in Thailand in 1968; captive programme from 1989 with Cambodian birds; first release 2011 at Huai Chorakhe Mak, Buriram; over 150 released (search summary) — [South China Morning Post](https://scmp.com/news/asia/southeast-asia/article/3204646/how-thailand-brought-rare-eastern-sarus-cranes-back-brink-extinction)
- Philippines subspecies extinct in the late 1960s; Pakistan: "have not been seen there since the late 1980s"; Malaysia listed as extirpated (no date) — [Wikipedia: Sarus crane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarus_crane)
- Voice: "loud, trumpeting calls" from a windpipe coiled in the sternum; pairs duet in unison — [Wikipedia: Sarus crane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarus_crane)
- Culture: Valmiki cursed a hunter for killing a sarus and was moved to compose the Ramayana; symbol of marital fidelity; in Gujarat newly-weds are taken to see a pair — [Wikipedia: Sarus crane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarus_crane)
- Boxes (est.): central and NE Thailand `[99.0, 13.0, 105.0, 18.0]`; Luzon `[120.0, 15.0, 122.5, 18.5]`.

**Green peafowl (*Pavo muticus*) — Peninsular Malaysia, Bangladesh, northern Vietnam, most of Thailand and China**
- Extinct in Peninsular Malaysia; no longer present in Bangladesh; NE Indian records doubtful; in Thailand confined to northern and western river basins; extinct in northern Vietnam; no longer widespread in Yunnan; 5,000–10,000 wild birds c. 1995 — [Wikipedia: Green peafowl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_peafowl)
- Voice: males call at dawn and dusk, "a loud often repeated ki-wao"; females an equally loud "AOw-aa" — [Wikipedia: Green peafowl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_peafowl)
- Culture: emblem of the Konbaung dynasty and symbol of the Sun; national bird of Myanmar; on the National League for Democracy flag — [Wikipedia: Green peafowl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_peafowl)
- Boxes (est.): Peninsular Malaysia `[100.0, 1.3, 104.3, 6.7]`; SE Bangladesh `[91.5, 21.0, 92.7, 23.5]`.

**Giant ibis (*Thaumatibis gigantea*) and white-shouldered ibis (*Pseudibis davisoni*) — Thailand, Mekong delta**
- Giant ibis: "a loud, ringing call, frequently repeated around dawn or dusk, a-leurk a-leurk"; "still fairly common in the Mekong Delta until the 1920s"; now northern Cambodia with a few in southern Laos; about 100 pairs; national bird of Cambodia — [Wikipedia: Giant ibis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giant_ibis). Extinct in Thailand (formerly Ratchaburi, Prachuap Khiri Khan, Trang, Satun); no records after the 1960s until rediscovery in Laos in 1993 (search summary) — [EDGE of Existence](https://www.edgeofexistence.org/species/giant-ibis); [BirdLife factsheet](https://datazone.birdlife.org/species/factsheet/giant-ibis-thaumatibis-gigantea/text)
- White-shouldered ibis: Thailand was once its stronghold; no records there since 1937; functionally extinct in Thailand, Myanmar and southern China (search summary) — [Wikipedia: White-shouldered ibis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White-shouldered_ibis); [Thai National Parks](https://thainationalparks.com/species/white-shouldered-ibis)
- Boxes (est.): peninsular and SW Thailand `[98.5, 6.5, 100.5, 14.0]`; Mekong delta `[104.5, 8.5, 107.0, 11.5]`.

**Great Indian bustard (*Ardeotis nigriceps*) — 90% of range**
- Once in Punjab, Haryana, Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Odisha, Andhra Pradesh, Rajasthan, Gujarat, Maharashtra, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu; "extirpated in 90% of its former range"; under 250 birds in 2008, about 150 in 2018 — [Wikipedia: Great Indian bustard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Indian_bustard)
- Voice: "a resonant deep, booming call that may be heard for nearly 500m"; the Maharashtra name "hoom" comes from the boom, the northern name "hookna" from the barking alarm — [Wikipedia: Great Indian bustard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Indian_bustard)
- Box (est.): Deccan and north-west plains `[72.0, 12.0, 80.0, 31.0]`.

**Gyps vultures — India, 1993–2002**
- White-rumped vulture fell 99.7% between 1993 and 2002; Indian and slender-billed about 97.4%; about 40 million Gyps in South Asia in the early 1980s against 19,000 in 2017; diclofenac identified 2003, veterinary use banned in India 11 March 2006; Parsi sky burial disrupted — [Wikipedia: Indian vulture crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_vulture_crisis)

#### Africa

- **North African ostrich**: once from Ethiopia and Sudan through the Sahel to Senegal and Mauritania, north to Egypt and southern Morocco; now in only 6 of 18 countries; Tunisia: returned in 2014 "after 127 years of being extinct" (so lost c. 1887); Morocco (Souss-Massa): reintroduced after being "extinct for 50 years" — [Wikipedia: North African ostrich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_African_ostrich). Box (est.) southern Tunisia `[8.0, 31.0, 11.0, 35.0]`.
- **Southern ground hornbill**: a deep booming four-note "hoo hoo hoo-hoo"; audible about 4 km, up to 5 km; chorus heard at 3 km (search summary) — [IUCN Hornbill Specialist Group](https://iucnhornbills.org/southern-ground-hornbill); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_ground_hornbill). In South Africa a 50% decline in range and over 10% in numbers across three decades (2005 assessment) (search summary) — [News24](https://www.news24.com/scitech/news/rare-bird-faces-extinction-20050221). Dawn calls taken as a sign of rain; elsewhere "a sign, or bringer, of death, destruction, loss, and deprivation" — [Wikipedia: Southern ground hornbill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_ground_hornbill). Box (est.) eastern South Africa `[26.0, -34.0, 32.0, -23.0]`.
- **Northern bald ibis**: last Algerian colony late 1980s — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_bald_ibis)

#### Americas

**Greater prairie-chicken (*Tympanuchus cupido pinnatus*) — Canada (last 1987) and the eastern Midwest**
- Boom, measured: lowest fundamental of its calls, mean 299 Hz (SD 13); longest duration, mean 1.89 s (SD 0.18); the boom sits in a narrow band of roughly 270–325 Hz; the low-frequency chorus "travels a long distance over the landscape" (search summary) — [The Condor 2018](https://bioone.org/journals/the-condor/volume-120/issue-1/CONDOR-17-56.1/Male-Greater-Prairie-Chickens-adjust-their-vocalizations-in-the-presence/10.1650/CONDOR-17-56.1.full); [Acoustical Society lay paper](https://acoustics.org/tag/mating/)
- Season: late March through April, on short-grass "booming grounds"; males inflate neck air sacs and snap their tails — [Wikipedia: Greater prairie-chicken](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_prairie-chicken)
- Canada: populations on the Canadian prairies peaked in the early 1900s "at an estimated one million or more breeding birds"; in Manitoba it was common "to bag from 60 to 150 in one day". Ontario: Walpole Island birds until the mid- to late 1920s, Manitoulin Island gone by the 1970s through hybridisation with sharp-tailed grouse. Manitoba: mostly gone by 1950. Alberta: "virtually disappeared … after the mid-1950s". Saskatchewan: infrequent to the 1960s, rare in the 1970s–80s, unverified sightings 1991–92. "Last reported in 1987" for Canada — [COSEWIC 2009](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/greater-prairie-chicken-2009.html). Conflict: another summary gives Alberta as gone "in the 1920s" and Ontario as last sighted 1959 (search summary) — [COSEWIC 2021 rapid review](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/rroc-greater-prairie-chicken-2021.html). Listed Extirpated in Canada since 1990 — same source.
- United States: Illinois fell "from several million in the mid-19th century to 46 by 1994" — [COSEWIC 2009](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/greater-prairie-chicken-2009.html). Iowa: extirpated in the 1950s (search summary) — [Iowa DNR report](https://iowadnr.gov/Portals/idnr/uploads/Hunting/trends/2022-23%20Greater%20Prairie%20Chicken%20Report.pdf). Ohio: conflicting — "extirpated by the mid-nineteenth century", also "reported as extirpated in Ohio in 1903, and after several newer sightings, again in 1937" (search summary) — [Cincinnati Audubon](https://cincinnatiaudubon.org/birds-of-cincinnati-annotatedlist/grpc/). Kentucky: when Audubon arrived about 1808 they were so abundant that "no hunter of Kentucky deigned to shoot them", walked village streets in winter; scarce within his lifetime (search summary) — [NKyTribune](https://nkytribune.com/?p=289060). A steamboat captain saw them "by the millions" at Bird's Point in 1840 — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_prairie-chicken)
- Boxes (est.): Canadian prairies `[-114.0, 49.0, -96.0, 53.5]`; SW Ontario (Walpole) `[-83.2, 42.0, -81.5, 43.2]`; Ohio–Indiana–Kentucky `[-89.0, 36.5, -81.0, 42.0]`; Iowa `[-96.6, 40.4, -90.1, 43.5]`.

**Whooping crane (*Grus americana*) — northern prairie breeding range (1894 / 1922); Louisiana (1950, back 2011)**
- Formerly nested from NW Illinois, northern Iowa, southern and western Minnesota, the Dakotas and Montana north through Manitoba, Saskatchewan and eastern Alberta to southern Mackenzie; last US breeding record 1894 (Iowa); last Canadian prairie record 1922 (Saskatchewan) (search summary) — [Wilson Bulletin note](https://sora.unm.edu/sites/default/files/journals/wilson/v059n02/p0127-p0128.pdf); [COSEWIC 2000](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/whooping-crane-2000.html)
- Louisiana non-migratory flock: scattered by a 1940 hurricane; last bird captured March 1950 (search summary) — [Texas Parks & Wildlife](https://tpwd.texas.gov/publications/pwdpubs/media/pwd_bk_w7000_0013_whooping_crane.pdf). 21 wild and 2 captive birds by 1941; reintroduced in Wisconsin (2001) and Louisiana (2011) — [Wikipedia: Whooping crane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whooping_crane)
- Voice: calls "are loud and can carry several kilometers"; unison calls after waking in the early morning, after courtship and in territory defence — [Wikipedia: Whooping crane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whooping_crane)
- Boxes (est.): prairie–parkland breeding range `[-112.0, 41.0, -90.0, 54.0]`; SW Louisiana marshes `[-93.5, 29.5, -92.0, 30.5]`.

**Thick-billed parrot (*Rhynchopsitta pachyrhyncha*) — Arizona / New Mexico, 1938**
- "The last reliable reports of the birds in the southwestern United States were in 1935 and 1938 in the Chiricahua Mountains of Arizona"; lost to shooting, logging and development; 1980s reintroduction abandoned 1993, last released bird seen 1995 — [Wikipedia: Thick-billed parrot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thick-billed_parrot)
- Voice: "resembles a high-pitched macaw and includes a variety of harsh, rolling calls described as similar to human laughter" — same source
- Culture: bones and imagery at Wupatki Pueblo and Chaco Canyon — same source
- Boxes (est.): Chiricahua Mountains `[-109.5, 31.4, -109.1, 32.1]`; SE Arizona–SW New Mexico sky islands `[-111.0, 31.3, -108.0, 34.0]`.

**Trumpeter swan (*Cygnus buccinator*) — Ontario 1886 (back from 1982)**
- Last reported Ontario bird shot at Long Point, Lake Erie, 1886; 69 known birds in North America by 1932; restoration from 1982 (Harry Lumsden) (search summary) — [Georgina Post](https://georginapost.com/2020/03/01/no-swan-song-for-trumpeters/); [Ontario Birds](https://sora-dev.unm.edu/sites/default/files/109%20-119%20OB%20Vol%2030%20%232%20Aug2012_2.pdf). Once bred "from northwestern Indiana west to Oregon … and in Canada from James Bay to the Yukon"; over 46,000 by 2010 — [Wikipedia: Trumpeter swan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trumpeter_swan)
- Box (est.): Great Lakes–James Bay `[-95.0, 41.0, -76.0, 52.0]`.

**California condor — wild 1987 (back 1992 onward)**
- Last wild bird captured Easter Sunday 1987; releases in California 1991–92, Arizona 1996, Baja California, and the Yurok-led release at Redwood National Park in 2022; lacks a syrinx — "a few hissing or grunting sounds only heard when very close"; Yurok name "prey-go-neesh" — [Wikipedia: California condor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_condor)

**Scarlet macaw (*Ara macao cyanoptera*) — Mexico and El Salvador**
- Gone from about 98% of its Mexican range; extinct in El Salvador; disappeared from Los Tuxtlas (Veracruz) in the 1970s; absent from Palenque for more than 70 years before 92 birds were released April 2013–June 2014 (search summary) — [Tropical Conservation Science 2014](https://bioone.org/journals/Tropical-Conservation-Science/volume-7/issue-3/194008291400700301/Reintroduction-of-the-scarlet-macaw-Ara-macao-cyanoptera-in-the/10.1177/194008291400700301.full); [Mongabay 2014](https://news.mongabay.com/2014/09/reintroduction-program-ups-mexicos-scarlet-macaw-population-by-34-percent-in-one-year/). Range once reached southern Tamaulipas — [Wikipedia: Scarlet macaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scarlet_macaw)
- Voice: "very loud, high and sometimes low-pitched, throaty squawks, squeaks and screams designed to carry many kilometers"; national bird of Honduras; bred in captivity at Paquimé in the 11th century — [Wikipedia: Scarlet macaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scarlet_macaw)
- Boxes (est.): Gulf slope of Mexico `[-99.0, 17.0, -94.0, 23.5]`; El Salvador `[-90.2, 13.1, -87.7, 14.5]`.

**Harpy eagle** — vanished from El Salvador, nearly from Costa Rica; in Mexico "probably occurs only in Chiapas"; "all but wiped out" in the Atlantic Forest; quiet away from the nest ("a penetrating, weak, melancholy scream") — [Wikipedia: Harpy eagle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harpy_eagle)

**Andean condor — Venezuela, 1965 (seen again 1976; reintroduced 1993)**
- Announced extinct in Venezuela by 1965; sighted again in Mérida in 1976; five released at Páramo de Mifafí January 1993; fewer than 10 survive plus 5–10 annual visitors from Colombia; about 130 in Colombia (2021 census) (search summary) — [Bioandina](https://en.bioandina.org.ar/distribución-y-estado-de-conservación); [Caracas Chronicles](https://www.caracaschronicles.com/2020/12/05/we-can-prevent-our-andean-condors-demise/)
- Box (est.): Venezuelan Andes `[-72.0, 7.5, -70.0, 9.5]`.

#### Australia

**Bush stone-curlew (*Burhinus grallarius*) — south-east Australia**
- Voice: "The call of 'weeloo' has an eerie and plaintive tone"; "sounds like a wail or a scream in the night"; several birds join in chorus; calls more as rain approaches — [Wikipedia: Bush stone-curlew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bush_stone-curlew). "A loud eerie wailing 'wee-loo', mostly heard at night" — [NSW threatened species profile](https://threatenedspecies.bionet.nsw.gov.au/profile?id=10113)
- Loss: once across much of western, central and northern Victoria and the plains around Melbourne and Geelong; range has retracted to the north and north-west; fewer than 300 pairs on the northern Victorian plains; flocks of 50–100 before the decline; numbers halved between the mid-1980s and early 1990s; Critically Endangered in Victoria; 85% of Victorian habitat lost; Phillip Island's last record was in the 1970s (search summary) — [Phillip Island Nature Parks](https://www.penguins.org.au/assets/conservation/education/Nature-Notes/2024/2023_bush-stone-curlew_Nature-Note.pdf); [Goulburn Broken CMA](https://www.gbcma.vic.gov.au/downloads/Biodiversity/53914_Bush_Stone_Curlew_Bklt_LoRes_Web.pdf); [Odonata](https://www.odonata.org.au/bush-stone-curlew). By 1980 there were few mainland records in South Australia; Kangaroo Island is the southern stronghold (search summary) — [University of Adelaide thesis](https://digital.library.adelaide.edu.au/dspace/handle/2440/16514)
- Back: reintroduced to Mulligans Flat, Canberra, from 2014 — [Wikipedia: Bush stone-curlew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bush_stone-curlew)
- Boxes (est.): Melbourne–Geelong plains `[144.0, -38.6, 145.6, -37.4]`; Phillip Island `[145.1, -38.55, 145.4, -38.4]`.

**Magpie goose (*Anseranas semipalmata*) — Victoria and South Australia, 1911 (reintroduced from 1964)**
- "Officially designated as extinct in Victoria and South Australia since 1911"; the Bool Lagoon colony had vanished by 1911; reintroduced to Serendip in 1964 and Tower Hill in 1974; Bool Lagoon re-established after its 1968 reservation (search summary) — [Birds SA](https://birdssa.asn.au/?p=6556); [birding-aus archive](https://cgi.cse.unsw.edu.au/~cs6714/bio/archives/html/birding-aus/1998-07/msg00097.html)

- Box (est.): SE South Australia–western Victoria `[138.5, -38.7, 146.0, -35.0]`.

**Tasmanian emu — c. 1850–1865**
- "Became extinct around 1865"; elsewhere "around 1850, but this date is not very precise"; captive birds died 1873 (identity uncertain); mobs of "seventy or eighty birds"; by 1838 Gould said a month's search was needed to see one; lost to dogs, hunting, fire and fencing; subspecies status disputed — [Wikipedia: Tasmanian emu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tasmanian_emu)
- Box (est.): Tasmania `[144.5, -43.6, 148.5, -40.6]`.

**Australian bustard** — range "appears to have contracted in the southeast of the continent during the last century"; critically endangered in Victoria; display with "loud booming" and "deep roaring noises"; feathers used in ceremony and Dreaming stories attached — [Wikipedia: Australian bustard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_bustard)

### Inferences
- For the mode's logic (a voice that is still missing), the cleanest bird kin are: prairie-chicken boom on the Canadian prairies and in Ohio/Kentucky; corncrake over lowland England and Wales; Siberian crane at Keoladeo; whooping crane across the northern prairies; thick-billed parrot in the Chiricahuas; capercaillie in Ireland; buttonquail in Andalusia; black grouse in Jutland; sarus crane in Luzon; white-shouldered ibis in Thailand; Tasmanian emu.
- Returned voices (bittern, crane, white-tailed eagle, white stork, crested ibis, Oriental stork, trumpeter swan, magpie goose, sarus crane in Thailand) could still be used honestly if the mode states the gap years, e.g. "bittern, silent in Britain 1886–1911"; otherwise they do not belong.
- Three listed birds are near-silent and give little to synthesize: great bustard, bearded vulture, California and Andean condors. The northern bald ibis is firmly dated in central Europe but has only a croak and a hoarse "hyoh" at the colony.
- The Tasmanian emu could reuse the existing island-emu drumming voice, since it was a subspecies of the same bird.
- The black francolin is a special case: an introduced court game bird whose voice was part of the western Mediterranean for six centuries. It needs that framing if used.

### Gaps
- No source found here for measured frequencies of: common crane, whooping crane, sarus crane, green peafowl, bush stone-curlew, giant ibis, thick-billed parrot, scarlet macaw, black francolin, buttonquail, black grouse, turtle dove. Descriptions only.
- No extirpation year found for green peafowl in Peninsular Malaysia or Bangladesh (the BirdLife Asian Red Data Book PDF returned 404), for giant ibis in Thailand, for scarlet macaw in El Salvador, or for European roller in Sweden.
- Greater prairie-chicken US state dates are thin and conflicting (Ohio "mid-19th century" versus 1903 versus 1937; Alberta 1920s versus mid-1950s). Kentucky, Indiana, Michigan, Tennessee, Arkansas, Louisiana and Texas dates were not found. I did not verify whether Iowa's birds were later reintroduced.
- Not researched for lack of time: night heron, ruff, cirl and corn bunting, grey partridge, nightingale, golden oriole, hazel grouse, lesser kestrel, little bustard, houbara, pin-tailed sandgrouse, sociable lapwing, Bengal florican, white-winged duck, masked finfoot, spoon-billed sandpiper, Baer's pochard, Chinese crested tern, black-faced spoonbill, wattled crane (the Wikipedia page had no range-loss data), Egyptian plover, shoebill, blue swallow, sharp-tailed grouse, aplomado falcon, masked bobwhite, northern bobwhite, eastern sandhill crane, common loon, piping plover, whip-poor-will, red-cockaded woodpecker, great green and military macaws, horned guan, quetzal, Orinoco goose, black-fronted piping guan, red-billed curassow, brolga, malleefowl, plains-wanderer, eastern bristlebird, regent honeyeater, bearded vulture in South Africa and Ethiopia.

The Tasmanian emu sits within the history of the dispossession of Aboriginal Tasmanians; I found no source addressing this.

## 2. For each thin region, which lost or extirpated voices of any taxon could fill it?

### Takeaway
Mainland western Europe, China and mainland Southeast Asia can be filled well (ibis, grouse, buttonquail, francolin; gibbons and alligators; tigers, cranes, ibises and a singing rhino). Scandinavia has modest but firmly dated candidates. Siberia, Mongolia, sub-Saharan Africa, Amazonia, Canada's north and Antarctica have very little that is both firmly dated and still gone; in those regions the honest options are one or two kin voices or continued wind.

### Cited Findings

#### Mainland Europe west of Poland
- Northern bald ibis, Alps, gone c. 1630–1645 — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_bald_ibis). Box (est.) `[7.0, 46.5, 16.0, 48.5]`.
- Black grouse, Denmark, extinct since 2001 — [Wikipedia: Black grouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_grouse). Box (est.) `[8.1, 55.3, 10.0, 57.5]`.
- Andalusian buttonquail, Spain, 1981 / 2018 — [MaghrebOrnitho](https://magornitho.org/2018/11/andalusian-buttonquail-declared-extinct-spain/). Box (est.) `[-7.0, 36.0, -5.5, 37.5]`.
- Black francolin, Sicily and eastern Spain, mid-19th century — [EBD-CSIC](https://www.ebd.csic.es/en/outreach/news/black-francolins-history-reflects-historical-dominance-crown-aragon-mediterranean).
- Common crane as a breeder: Italy c. 1920, Hungary by 1952, Spain 1954–2017, Austria 1885–2018 — [Wikipedia: Common crane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_crane).
- White stork: Switzerland 1950, Belgium 1895, Netherlands 1991 (all since restored) — [Wikipedia: White stork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_stork).
- Bearded vulture: Alps 1913/1930s, back 1987 — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bearded_vulture).
- European bison: last Transylvanian animal 1790; last wild Polish animal 1921; last wild anywhere 1927 (Caucasus); released again at Białowieża 1929. #### Scandinavia
- Wolf: functionally extinct in Norway and Sweden at the end of the 1960s; a den in Swedish Lapland in 1964; first new litter in 1983 in Värmland from Finnish–Russian immigrants (search summary) — [Oryx: Does Sweden have room for its wolves?](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/38AFAF528F4417180680D80466037445/S0030605300026612a.pdf/does-sweden-have-room-for-its-wolves.pdf); [Wildlife Biology](https://bioone.org/journals/wildlife-biology/volume-15/issue-4/08-010/Survival-on-the-Border--A-Population-Model-to-Evaluate/10.2981/08-010.full). Box (est.) `[5.0, 58.0, 24.0, 69.0]`. Returned.
- Fire-bellied toad (*Bombina bombina*), Sweden: extinct in the 1960s; reintroduced from the early 1980s (established at 7 of 10 sites 1982–1993); Swedish name "klockgroda" (bell frog); the song carries far and is compared to distant church bells; call unpulsed and unmodulated, around 500–600 Hz (search summary) — [Conservation Evidence](https://conservationevidencejournal.com/individual-study/4782); [AmphibiaWeb](https://amphibiaweb.org/species/2041). Box (est.) Skåne `[12.8, 55.4, 14.4, 56.0]`. Returned.
- White stork, Sweden, last pair c. 1955 — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_stork).
- Middle spotted woodpecker, Sweden, 1982 — [British Birds](https://britishbirds.co.uk/journal/article/news-and-comments). Still gone.
- Great bustard, Sweden, mid-19th century — [SLU](https://stud.epsilon.slu.se/7672/). Still gone.
- Great snipe leks in the Scandinavian lowlands and Denmark, lost in the 19th century — [Bird-lens](https://www.bird-lens.com/?p=11099).
- Lesser white-fronted goose: Fennoscandian population at most 20 pairs / 60–80 birds; Swedish population about 15 pairs — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lesser_white-fronted_goose). Not extirpated.
- Black grouse, Denmark, 2001; crane, Denmark, absent c. 1850s–1953 — sources above.

#### Siberia
- Siberian crane, western and central flyways (Ob–Konda–Sosva): the Indian wintering flock ended 2002 and the Iranian one is a single bird — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siberian_crane). Voice measured at about 1.4 kHz.
- Dhole: the last proved Russian record is from 1978; no confirmed reports for over 30 years from Russia, Mongolia, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Uzbekistan or Afghanistan (search summary) — [IUCN Canid Specialist Group](https://canids.org/species/view/PREKQW171301); [Wikipedia: Dhole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhole). The Tian Shan dhole of the Altai and Tian Shan "most likely went extinct since 1946" — [Wikipedia: Tien Shan dhole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tien_Shan_dhole). Voice: whistles "resembling the calls of red foxes, sometimes rendered as coo-coo", used to keep the pack together in brush; screaming "KaKaKaKAA" in attack; "dholes do not howl or bark" — [Wikipedia: Dhole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhole). Boxes (est.): Altai–Sayan `[82.0, 49.0, 104.0, 55.0]`; Primorye–Amur `[127.0, 42.5, 140.0, 52.0]`.

#### Mongolia
- Przewalski's horse (takhi): last wild sighting a single stallion in 1969 in the Dzungarian Gobi near Takhiin Shar Nuruu; 16 released in 1992 at what became Khustain Nuruu; later Great Gobi B and Khomiin Tal — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Przewalski%27s_horse). Box (est.) Dzungarian Gobi `[91.0, 44.5, 95.5, 46.5]`. Returned.
- Dhole: as above — [IUCN Canid Specialist Group](https://canids.org/species/view/PREKQW171301).

#### China
- Gibbons: 535 dated records from 420 local gazetteers (Ming, Qing, Republican) place gibbons in 149 further administrative regions across 19 provinces beyond today's range; absent from 17.5% of those regions by 1600, 36.6% by 1850, 57.5% by 1900, 84.4% by 1950; decline rate steepened about thirteen-fold after 1850; isolated populations lasted a mean 42.9 years (1–172); the Hainan gibbon is down to 23–25 animals — [Turvey, Crees & Di Fonzo 2015, Proc. R. Soc. B](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4632630). Box (est.) central, southern and eastern China `[103.0, 21.5, 122.0, 34.0]`.
- Chinese alligator: range reduced by 90%, now mostly a 433 km² reserve in Anhui; about 1,000 wild in the 1970s, under 130 in 2001; no fragment holds more than 20. Spring bellowing choruses of both sexes last about 10 minutes; bellows have a pulsed structure around 35 Hz (search summary) — [Reber et al. 2015, J. Exp. Biol.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4528706); [Crocodiles of the World](https://www.crocodilesoftheworld.co.uk/animals/chinese-alligator/). Box (est.) lower Yangtze `[115.0, 28.5, 122.0, 33.0]`.
- South China tiger: range across Fujian, Guangdong, Hunan and Jiangxi (about 2,000 km by 1,500 km); 150–200 in 1982; "no wild individual has been recorded since the late 1980s" — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_China_tiger). Box (est.) `[108.0, 22.0, 120.0, 31.0]`.
- Père David's deer (milu): the Nanyuan herd was lost after the 1895 flood; conflicting wild-extinction dates of 1895, 1900 and 1939 in one article; returned to Beijing 1985, Dafeng 1986, Shishou 1993; 2,825 wild in 2020 — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%C3%A8re_David%27s_deer). Returned.
- Crested ibis: thought lost, seven found in Shaanxi in 1981 — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crested_ibis).
- Indochinese tiger in Yunnan: three animals detected 2004–2009; "probably does not survive today" — [Wikipedia: Indochinese tiger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indochinese_tiger).

#### Mainland Southeast Asia
- Tiger: Cambodia — last documented 1999–2007, declared "functionally extinct" 2016; Vietnam — "has not been recorded in the country since 1997"; Laos — last two vanished from Nam Et-Phou Louey after 2013 — [Wikipedia: Indochinese tiger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indochinese_tiger); [Mongabay: How Laos lost its tigers](https://news.mongabay.com/2019/10/how-laos-lost-its-tigers/amp/). Boxes (est.): Cambodia `[102.3, 10.4, 107.6, 14.7]`; Laos `[100.1, 13.9, 107.7, 22.5]`; Vietnam `[102.1, 8.5, 109.5, 23.4]`.
- Sumatran rhinoceros: Malaysia's last, Iman, died November 2019; formerly through Thailand and Myanmar to the eastern Himalayan foothills; about 80 left — [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/amp/news/2019/5/28/malaysia-mourns-loss-of-a-species-as-male-sumatran-rhino-dies). Measured voice: "eeps" 70 Hz–4 kHz at 57–92 dB; "whales" 100 Hz–3.2 kHz at 87 dB; "whistle-blows" 17 Hz–8 kHz at 100 dB with strong infrasound; song-like and near-constant, unlike other rhinos (search summary) — [von Muggenthaler, Acoustical Society of America](https://acoustics.org/pressroom/httpdocs/142nd/vonmuggenthaler.html). Box (est.) Peninsular Malaysia–Tenasserim `[97.5, 1.3, 104.3, 17.0]`.
- Sarus crane, Thailand, 1968–2011 — [SCMP](https://scmp.com/news/asia/southeast-asia/article/3204646/how-thailand-brought-rare-eastern-sarus-cranes-back-brink-extinction).
- White-shouldered ibis, Thailand, last 1937; giant ibis, Thailand and Mekong delta (1920s) — sources in section 1.
- Green peafowl, Peninsular Malaysia — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_peafowl).
- Irrawaddy dolphin, Laos: the last animal of the Chheu Teal transboundary pool (ID#35) died in February 2022; 17 in 1993, 7 in 2009, 3 in 2018 — [Mongabay](https://news.mongabay.com/2022/04/death-of-last-river-dolphin-in-laos-rings-alarm-bells-for-mekong-population/). Box (est.) `[105.8, 13.8, 106.1, 14.1]`.

#### Africa between the Sahara and the Karoo
- Miss Waldron's red colobus: not officially sighted since 1978, considered extinct in 2000; lived in "large, noisy" groups in the canopy of eastern Côte d'Ivoire and western Ghana; no photograph of a living animal exists; IUCN lists it as Critically Endangered (possibly a few remain in SE Côte d'Ivoire) — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miss_Waldron%27s_red_colobus); [Re:wild](https://rewild.org/lost-species/miss-waldrons-red-colobus). Box (est.) `[-4.5, 4.8, -1.5, 8.0]`.
- Western chimpanzee: extirpated in Benin, Burkina Faso and Togo; gone from the wild in the Gambia (search summary; accounts for Burkina Faso and Benin are inconsistent) — [Wikipedia: Western chimpanzee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_chimpanzee). Pant-hoot in four parts: soft "hoos", build-up, climax of screams, let-down; drumming on tree trunks — [Wikipedia: Chimpanzee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chimpanzee). Box (est.) Togo–Benin `[0.0, 6.0, 3.8, 11.5]`.
- Lion in West Africa: about 400 remain (about 250 adults) in 1.1% of the historic range; survive only in Senegal, Nigeria and the Benin–Niger–Burkina Faso border complex (search summary) — [Oxford Martin School on Henschel et al. 2014](https://www.oxfordmartin.ox.ac.uk/publications/the-lion-in-west-africa-is-critically-endangered). Lions are already in the cast, so this is an extension of range boxes.
- African wild dog: "exterminated in large parts of North and West Africa", much reduced in Central Africa, Uganda and Kenya; about 6,600 adults in 39 subpopulations; repertoire includes "twittering", "hooing" — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_wild_dog). No dates.
- Kihansi spray toad: about 17,000 in two hectares of spray zone below the Kihansi falls, Tanzania; January 2004 three seen and two males heard calling; Extinct in the Wild May 2009; test release of 48 in 2012; caused by the 1999 dam cutting flow by 90% — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kihansi_spray_toad). Box (est.) `[35.8, -8.65, 35.9, -8.55]`.
- Southern ground hornbill, eastern South Africa (range halved) and North African ostrich in the Sahel — section 1.

#### South America outside eastern Brazil and the pampas
- Jambato toad (*Atelopus ignescens*): "formerly abundant along streams, rivers and ponds of the páramo" around Quito; last seen 1988; relict population found 2016 at Angamarca, Cotopaxi — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atelopus_ignescens). Box (est.) Ecuadorian inter-Andean highlands `[-79.2, -2.0, -77.8, 0.8]`.
- Chile Darwin's frog (*Rhinoderma rufum*): coastal forests between Zapallar and Concepción; not seen since about 1981; release calls recorded from four males in 1981 show harmonics and chaotic segments (search summary) — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chile_Darwin%27s_frog). Box (est.) `[-73.5, -37.0, -71.0, -32.5]`.
- Giant otter: lost about 80% of its range; last Argentine family groups seen 1986 in Misiones; considered extinct in Uruguay; a lone animal seen on the Bermejo in 2021; releases into Iberá followed — [Mongabay 2021](https://news.mongabay.com/2021/05/giant-otter-thought-to-be-extinct-in-argentina-resurfaces-literally/); [Mongabay 2025](https://news.mongabay.com/short-article/2025/07/giant-river-otter-returns-to-argentina-after-almost-four-decades). 22 adult call types; "hah" barks, explosive snorts, "a wavering scream"; each family has its own audio signature — [Wikipedia: Giant otter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giant_otter). Boxes (est.): Misiones–Corrientes `[-59.5, -30.0, -53.5, -25.5]`; Uruguay `[-58.5, -35.0, -53.0, -30.0]`.
- Andean condor, Venezuelan Andes, 1965 — section 1.
- Harpy eagle and scarlet macaw are Central American losses rather than South American — section 1.

#### Canada's north
- Bowhead whale, eastern Canada–West Greenland: at least 11,000 when commercial whaling began; reduced to under 30%; 55,916–67,537 taken 1530–1915; now about 6,000–7,000 (search summary) — [IWC Journal of Cetacean Research and Management](https://journal.iwc.int/index.php/jcrm/article/view/623). Largely covered by the existing pre-whaling whales entry.
- Ungava Bay beluga: "very low and may be extirpated"; under about 50; Endangered since 1988; depleted by commercial hunting — [COSEWIC](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/beluga-whale/chapter-15.html). Box (est.) `[-70.5, 58.0, -64.5, 61.0]`.
- Atlantic walrus, Nova Scotia–Newfoundland–Gulf of St Lawrence: tens of thousands; hunted to extirpation by the late 18th century; now assessed as extinct — [COSEWIC](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/atlantic-walrus/chapter-1.html). Colonies of 7,000–8,000; a breeding population on Sable Island — [Wikipedia: Walrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walrus). Box (est.) `[-66.0, 43.5, -57.0, 50.5]` (this is Atlantic Canada, not the north).
- Whooping crane: breeding range once reached southern Mackenzie — [COSEWIC 2000](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/whooping-crane-2000.html).
- Muskox: Alaskan population eliminated in the late 19th or early 20th century; returned to Nunivak in 1935; bulls "roar", cows bray to calves — [Wikipedia: Muskox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muskox). Alaska, not Canada.

#### Antarctica
- Blue whale: "at least 140,000 mature whales in 1926"; 350,000–360,000 taken in the Antarctic in the first half of the 20th century; 5,000–8,000 in the Antarctic now; Southern Ocean call is an 18-second unit beginning with a 9-second 27 Hz tone; fundamentals 8–25 Hz; up to 188 dB — [Wikipedia: Blue whale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_whale).
- Antarctic fur seal: commercially exterminated at South Georgia by the 1820s; thought extinct by the late 1800s; refound 1915; a small breeding group on Bird Island in 1933; about 3.5 million at South Georgia in 2007–2009 (search summary) — [Forcada et al. 2023, Global Change Biology](https://nora.nerc.ac.uk/id/eprint/535822/). Box (est.) South Georgia `[-38.5, -55.0, -35.5, -53.9]`; South Shetlands `[-63.0, -63.5, -53.5, -61.0]`. Returned.

### Inferences
- Best fills per region (3–6 each, any taxon):
  - **Mainland western Europe**: buttonquail (Andalusia), black francolin (Sicily, eastern Spain), northern bald ibis (Alps), black grouse (Jutland), European bison (rut growl; Transylvania 1790, Poland 1921, with return dates), and breeding cranes in Italy and Hungary.
  - **Scandinavia**: fire-bellied toad bell chorus in Skåne (1960s, returned 1980s), wolf (c. 1966–1983 gap), great snipe leks in the lowlands (19th century), white stork clatter in Skåne (c. 1955), middle spotted woodpecker (1982, still gone), great bustard (mid-19th century). All in the far south; the fells and the north stay empty.
  - **Siberia**: Siberian crane on the Ob (the nearly finished western flyway) and dhole whistles in the Altai–Sayan and Primorye (last 1978). Little else is firmly documented.
  - **Mongolia**: takhi (1969–1992) and dhole. That is nearly all; an honest map leaves most of Mongolia to wind.
  - **China**: gibbon song across the south and centre (the best-documented regional loss of a loud voice anywhere in the thin regions), Chinese alligator bellowing choruses on the lower Yangtze, South China tiger, milu rut (returned), crested ibis.
  - **Mainland Southeast Asia**: tiger in Cambodia, Laos and Vietnam; Sumatran rhino song on the peninsula; sarus crane on the Thai plain; white-shouldered and giant ibis; green peafowl in Malaya; the last Lao river dolphin (2022).
  - **Africa**: Miss Waldron's red colobus (the only candidate that is a possibly global loss with a firm pair of dates), chimpanzee pant-hoots in Togo and Benin, lion roars across West Africa (extend existing boxes), Kihansi spray toad (one gorge), ground hornbill booming in eastern South Africa. Central Africa, the Congo basin and East Africa have almost nothing firmly dated.
  - **South America**: jambato toad around Quito (1988), Chile Darwin's frog (1981), giant otter on the Paraná–Uruguay (1986), Andean condor in Venezuela (near silent). Amazonia itself has no documented lost voice that I found.
  - **Canada's north**: nothing new that is firmly dated and still gone north of 60°. Ungava beluga is the only regional candidate; walrus and prairie-chicken sit further south.
  - **Antarctica**: no land or continental loss. The only material is marine: the Antarctic blue whale's 27 Hz call, and fur-seal rookeries on South Georgia and the South Shetlands that fell silent c. 1820s–1930s and have since returned.
- Gibbons in China and tigers in Indochina would be the two highest-value additions for filling large empty areas with one well-documented kin voice each.

### Gaps
- No source gathered for gibbon song acoustics, lion or wild-dog call measurements, beluga or walrus vocalisations, bowhead song, jambato call, or Père David's deer rut calls.
- Wolf extirpation dates for France and Germany, brown bear dates for the Alps, and European tree-frog losses were not researched; wolves could be extended into western Europe only once those dates are sourced.
- South Shetland sealing dates (1819–1820s) were not confirmed in a source I read; the cited paper's summary covers South Georgia.
- African wild dog, chimpanzee and green peafowl extirpations lack years. Chimpanzee status in Burkina Faso and Benin is contested in the literature.
- Asian elephant retreat in China, saiga, kulan, Mongolian beaver, spectacled cormorant, Telmatobius frogs and the chytrid-extinct Atelopus species were not researched.
- The claim that the Chinese alligator bellow is "pulsed around 35 Hz" comes from a search summary of the heliox paper and should be read in the paper before use.

### Inferences
- Non-bird candidates from section 2 that would rank A by the same test: gibbons in central and eastern China (dated by region through gazetteers; voice not yet sourced), tigers in Cambodia (2007), Laos (2013) and Vietnam (1997), Sumatran rhino in Malaysia (2019, voice measured), Miss Waldron's red colobus (1978/2000, voice unsourced), Chile Darwin's frog (1981, calls recorded).
- Non-bird B candidates: takhi (1969–1992), fire-bellied toad in Sweden (1960s–1980s), Scandinavian wolf (c. 1966–1983), giant otter in Argentina (1986–2021+), Père David's deer (c. 1900–1985), Antarctic fur seal (1820s–1930s), Kihansi spray toad (2004–2012).
- If only five additions were made to break the bird gap and the thin regions together, the evidence favours: prairie-chicken (Canadian prairies), corncrake (lowland Britain), Siberian crane (Keoladeo and the Ob), gibbons (China), tiger (Indochina).

### Gaps
- Ranks marked A- would move to A with a measured-voice source (black grouse, middle spotted woodpecker, buttonquail, thick-billed parrot) or a firmer date (capercaillie in Ireland, Tasmanian emu).
- Several C ranks are C only because a loss year was not found in this session (green peafowl in Malaysia, giant ibis in Thailand); the BirdLife Asian Red Data Book accounts would probably settle them.
- I could not confirm whether reintroduced northern bald ibis in Austria/Germany now count as an established return.
