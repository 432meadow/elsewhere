# Lost amphibians and vocal reptiles missing from the "what can no longer be heard" catalogue

Research notes, 2026-10-02. Scope: extinct (EX), extinct-in-the-wild (EW), possibly-extinct (CR-PE) and regionally extirpated amphibians and vocal reptiles lost since ~1500, excluding Hawaiʻi and Aotearoa New Zealand, and excluding the six frogs already implemented except where better call numbers were found.

Conventions used below
- Rank A = call recorded and/or published parameters exist. Rank B = described in words only. Rank C = no description; inferred from named congeners.
- Bounding boxes are `[lon0, lat0, lon1, lat1]`. **Every box marked (est.) was estimated by me** from the named localities in the cited source, not copied from a source.
- "Search-snippet" means a number reached me only through a search-engine summary of a page I could not open (AmphibiaWeb is behind a CAPTCHA; JSTOR, Smithsonian repository, zobodat, EOL, xeno-canto and Macaulay Library all refused automated access). Those numbers need checking against the primary paper before they are trusted.
- Access limits that shaped this sweep: the web-search quota ran out midway, so later work was done by fetching known URLs. Several primary papers (Jaslow 1979; Cocroft et al. 1990; Penna & Veloso 1990; Drewry & Rand 1983; Drewry & Jones 1976) could not be opened, and their numbers are reported second-hand or listed as gaps.

## 1. Which lost amphibians had their calls recorded or described? (sweep of the extinct / EW / CR-PE lists)

### Takeaway
Of ~185 amphibians on the Wikipedia list of recently extinct, possibly extinct and extinct-in-the-wild species, only about twenty have any acoustic record at all; the rest (all 17 Sri Lankan *Pseudophilautus*, Günther's streamlined frog, the spiny-knee leaf frog, almost all *Craugastor*, *Telmatobius* and most *Atelopus*) vanished unrecorded. The best-documented voices not yet in the catalogue are the Kihansi spray toad, three Venezuelan harlequin toads, the jambato, the northern Darwin's frog, the golden coquí, Poynton's forest toad, the Hula painted frog and the Mount Glorious (southern) day frog.

### Cited Findings

**Size and shape of the list**
- The Wikipedia list gives 185 taxa: 3 extinct and 10 possibly extinct salamanders; 34 extinct frogs; ~135 possibly extinct frogs; 3 extinct in the wild (Wyoming toad *Anaxyrus baxteri*, longnose stubfoot toad *Atelopus longirostris*, Kihansi spray toad *Nectophrynoides asperginis*). — [Wikipedia: List of recently extinct amphibians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_recently_extinct_amphibians)
- The 34 extinct frogs include 17 Sri Lankan *Pseudophilautus* (adspersus, dimbullae, eximius, extirpo, halyi, leucorhinus, maia, malcolmsmithi, nanus, nasutus, oxyrhynchus, pardus, rugatus, temporalis, variabilis, zal, zimmeri), three *Atelopus* (chiriquiensis, senex, vogli), three *Craugastor* (anciano, myllomyllon, omoaensis), *Boana cymbalum*, *Lithobates fisheri*, *Nannophrys guentheri*, *Oophaga speciosa*, *Phrynomedusa fimbriata*, *Ranoidea nyakalensis*, *Taudactylus diurnus*, plus the four already implemented (golden toad, both gastric-brooding frogs, sharp-snouted day frog). — [Wikipedia: List of recently extinct amphibians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_recently_extinct_amphibians)
- Scheele et al. 2019 (Science 363: 1459) attribute declines in at least 501 amphibian species to chytridiomycosis, including 90 presumed extinctions; the species list is in the supplementary material, which I could not retrieve. — [NESP summary of Scheele et al. 2019](https://www.nespthreatenedspecies.edu.au/publications-and-tools/amphibian-fungal-panzootic-causes-catastrophic-and-ongoing-loss-of-biodiversity); [paper PDF](https://www.nespthreatenedspecies.edu.au/media/khrfylk1/3-3_scheele-etal_2019_amphibian-fungal-panzootic-causes-science.pdf)

**Species on the lists with a recorded or measured call (Rank A)**
- *Atelopus*: vocalisations are known for only 23 of more than 100 species; the gap is "apparently linked to the dramatic declines". Lötters et al. 2019 tabulate which call types are known per species. Lost or presumed-lost species in that table: *A. chiriquiensis* (pulsed, pure tone, short pulsed; Jaslow 1979, Lötters et al. 1999), *A. senex* (pulsed; Cocroft et al. 1990), *A. carbonerensis* (all four types), *A. mucubajiensis* (pure tone and short pure tone), *A. tamaense* (short pure tone), *A. minutulus* (three types; Cocroft 1990), *A. nicefori* (one type; Cocroft 1990), *A. peruensis* (short pulsed; Lötters et al. 1999). — [Lötters et al. 2019, Herpetozoa 32: 267–275](https://herpetozoa.pensoft.net/article/39192/)
- *Atelopus ignescens* (jambato; presumed extinct from the late 1980s, rediscovered at Angamarca): calls described for the first time in 2024. — [Vocalizations of Atelopus ignescens, Herpetozoa 2024](https://herpetozoa.pensoft.net/article/134923/)
- *Rhinoderma rufum* (northern Darwin's frog; CR-PE): advertisement calls of eight individuals were described by Penna & Veloso 1990, audio published in Penna 2005; release calls of four males recorded in 1981 were analysed in 2021. — [Serrano, Bidart-Enriquez & Penna 2021, Amphib. Reptile Conserv. 15(2): 318–322](https://www.amphibian-reptile-conservation.org/pdfs/Volume/Vol_15_no_2/ARC_15_2_[General_Section]_318-322_e296.pdf)
- *Nectophrynoides asperginis* (Kihansi spray toad; EW 2009): full call table from a captive population. — [Arch, Richards-Zawacki & Feng 2011, J. Herpetol. 45: 45–49](http://www.rzlab.pitt.edu/Publications/Arch-et-al-2011.pdf)
- *Nectophrynoides poyntoni* (CR, possibly extinct; one collection in 2003): call given in the original description. — [Wikipedia: Nectophrynoides poyntoni, citing Menegon, Salvidio & Loader 2004](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nectophrynoides_poyntoni)
- *Anaxyrus baxteri* (Wyoming toad; EW): breeding calls recorded at Mortenson Lake NWR in June 2014 and archived publicly. — [Acoustic Atlas item 1226](https://acousticatlas.org/item/1226)
- *Eleutherodactylus jasperi* (golden coquí; CR-PE): call described by Drewry & Jones 1976 and Drewry 1986; recordings existed and were used in call-back surveys in 1976 and 1978. — [USFWS Golden coquí 5-Year Review](https://ecosphere-documents-production-public.s3.amazonaws.com/sams/public_docs/species_nonpublish/3694.pdf)
- *Latonia nigriventer* (Hula painted frog; presumed extinct 1955–2011): advertisement call measured after rediscovery. — [Wikipedia: Hula painted frog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hula_painted_frog)
- *Rheobatrachus vitellinus* (northern gastric-brooding frog): FrogID holds a call "recorded by Keith McDonald"; calling months October–November. — [FrogID: Rheobatrachus vitellinus](https://www.frogid.net.au/frogs/rheobatrachus-vitellinus/)

**Species with a verbal description only (Rank B)**
- *Taudactylus diurnus* (Mount Glorious / southern day frog; EX, last seen 1979): described by Liem & Hosmer 1973, correcting the original authors who thought it voiceless. — [Liem & Hosmer 1973, Mem. Qd Mus. 16(3): 435–457](https://archive.org/download/biostor-153355/biostor-153355.pdf)
- *Ranoidea (Litoria) nyakalensis* (mountain mistfrog; EX, last seen 1990): two verbal descriptions (Liem 1974; McDonald 1992). — [Australian Government SPRAT profile](http://www.environment.gov.au/cgi-bin/sprat/public/publicspecies.pl?taxon_id=1820)
- *Litoria castanea* (yellow-spotted bell frog; thought extinct ~1980, one population refound): "a series of loud, droning grunts, like a distant motorbike"; males call at night from open water or emergent vegetation (search-snippet of FrogID / NSW threatened-species profile). — [FrogID: Litoria castanea](https://www.frogid.net.au/frogs/litoria-castanea)
- *Eleutherodactylus eneidae* (Eneida's coquí; CR-PE): a call "composed of two different notes" is reported in the literature. — [Galvis et al., advertisement calls of three Hispaniolan Eleutherodactylus (Bioacoustics)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/311497294_The_advertisement_calls_of_three_Eleutherodactylus_species_from_Hispaniola_Anura_Eleutherodactylidae)
- *Eleutherodactylus orcutti* (Jamaican streamfrog; CR-PE): "males would vocalize in streams and waterfalls"; abundant into the 1980s, then collapsed. No description of the sound itself. — [Wikipedia: Eleutherodactylus orcutti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eleutherodactylus_orcutti)

**Species confirmed to have no acoustic record (Rank C at best, or silent)**
- Sri Lankan *Pseudophilautus*: the extinct species are "known only from museum specimens collected in the 19th and early 20th centuries"; e.g. *P. maia* has not been collected since 1876. No calls exist (search-snippet of the Zootaxa / JoTT literature). — [Wikipedia list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_recently_extinct_amphibians); [Meegaskumbura et al., Zootaxa 3002](https://www.mapress.com/zt/article/view/zootaxa.3002.1.7)
- *Nannophrys guentheri* (Günther's streamlined frog): described 1882; "it is not known where in Sri Lanka the specimens were found". — [Wikipedia: Nannophrys guentheri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nannophrys_guentheri)
- *Phrynomedusa fimbriata* (spiny-knee leaf frog): a single specimen, 1898, Alto da Serra / Paranapiacaba, São Paulo; "the natural history of this species is unknown". — [Wikipedia: Phrynomedusa fimbriata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phrynomedusa_fimbriata)
- *Litoria piperata* (peppered tree frog; not seen since 1973): "the call has never been recorded"; tadpoles unknown (search-snippet, Australian Museum). — [Australian Museum blog](https://australian.museum/blog/amri-news/the-search-for-the-peppered-tree-frog-has-begun/)
- *Incilius holdridgei* (Holdridge's toad): males have no vocal slits or sac; the species is mute (search-snippet). — [Wikipedia: Holdridge's toad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holdridge%27s_toad)
- *Atelopus vogli* (Maracay harlequin frog; EX, last seen 1959; two localities at 600–700 m on the southern slope of the Venezuelan Coastal Range): no call record; not in the Lötters call table. — [Wikipedia: Atelopus vogli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atelopus_vogli); [Lötters et al. 2019](https://herpetozoa.pensoft.net/article/39192/)
- *Lithobates fisheri* (Vegas Valley leopard frog; EX 1942): last ten specimens collected at Tule Springs on 13 January 1942; elevation 370–760 m. Archival DNA (Hekkala et al. 2011) places it with north-western Mogollon Rim populations of the extant Chiricahua leopard frog, so it is arguably extirpated from Nevada rather than extinct. No call record found. — [Wikipedia: Vegas Valley leopard frog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vegas_Valley_leopard_frog)
- *Arthroleptides dutoiti* (Du Toit's torrent frog; Mt Elgon, Kenya; seen only in 1934 and 1962; 2,100–2,200 m): no call information. — [Wikipedia: Arthroleptides dutoiti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthroleptides_dutoiti)
- *Craugastor chrysozetetes* (Quebrada de Oro, Cerro Búfalo, Honduras, 880–1,130 m), *C. anciano* (Cordillera de Celaque, Honduras), *Telmatobius niger* (Ecuador, 2,469–4,000 m, last record Lagunas de Atillo, December 1994), *Aromobates nocturnus*, *Isthmohyla calypsa* (Talamanca, 1,500–2,100 m), *Ecnomiohyla echinata* (Sierra Juárez, Oaxaca, ~2,000 m), *Boana cymbalum*, *Eleutherodactylus semipalmatus* (Haiti, 303–1,697 m): Wikipedia pages give range and habitat but no call. — [C. chrysozetetes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Craugastor_chrysozetetes); [C. anciano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Craugastor_anciano); [T. niger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telmatobius_niger); [A. nocturnus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aromobates_nocturnus); [I. calypsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isthmohyla_calypsa); [E. echinata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecnomiohyla_echinata); [B. cymbalum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boana_cymbalum); [E. semipalmatus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eleutherodactylus_semipalmatus)
- Salamanders on the list (Yunnan lake newt, Ainsworth's salamander, the Mexican *Pseudoeurycea* / *Thorius* / *Chiropterotriton*) are 13 taxa. — [Wikipedia list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_recently_extinct_amphibians)

**Status corrections that matter for the catalogue**
- *Atelopus carbonerensis* is no longer simply "likely extinct": after a 1998 observation it was unrecorded for two decades, then one individual was seen in 2014 and a pair in amplexus in 2021 (Barrio-Amorós & Torres 2023). — [Wikipedia: Atelopus carbonerensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atelopus_carbonerensis)
- *Atelopus mucubajiensis*: last observed 2004; "likely extinct". — [Wikipedia: Atelopus mucubajiensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atelopus_mucubajiensis)
- *Atelopus longirostris* (listed EW on Wikipedia) was rediscovered at Junín, Imbabura, Ecuador (Tapia et al. 2017); historical range western slopes of the northern Ecuadorian Andes, 200–2,500 m. — [Wikipedia: Atelopus longirostris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atelopus_longirostris)
- *Atelopus zeteki* (already implemented): "has not been sighted in the wild since 2009". — [Critical calls, PLOS ONE 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10434951/)

### Inferences
- The acoustic record of lost amphibians is concentrated in three clusters: Neotropical harlequin toads (*Atelopus*), Puerto Rican coquís, and Queensland stream frogs. Asia and Africa contribute only the Kihansi spray toad, Poynton's forest toad and the Hula painted frog.
- For the 17 extinct Sri Lankan shrub frogs the only honest option is Rank C (a generic living *Pseudophilautus* voice), or silence.
- The salamanders on the list are plethodontids and a newt, groups with no advertisement call; treat as silent (my inference; no source consulted says so explicitly for these species).

### Gaps
- Scheele et al. 2019 supplementary list of 90 presumed-extinct species not retrieved; the Wikipedia list was used as the working roster instead.
- IUCN Red List pages were not queried directly (dynamic site); statuses above are as reported by Wikipedia and the cited papers.
- AmphibiaWeb species accounts (the richest source of call paragraphs) were unreachable behind a CAPTCHA, so "no call found" for *Craugastor*, *Telmatobius*, *Isthmohyla*, *Boana cymbalum*, *Oophaga speciosa* and the Hispaniolan / Jamaican / Cuban *Eleutherodactylus* means "not on Wikipedia and not found by search", not "proven undescribed".
- *Oophaga speciosa* (splendid poison frog; EX, last seen 1992; western Panama, 1,140–1,410 m, diurnal, terrestrial — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Splendid_poison_frog)): a search hit pointed to xeno-canto recording XC893976 for this query, but the page is bot-protected and I could not confirm what it contains. Worth a manual look.
- Living-model call parameters for *Pseudophilautus*, *Telmatobius* and *Craugastor* were not retrieved. One search-snippet states that the congener *Craugastor gollmeri* gives "a series of single, short notes, emitted about every 30 min", a soft sound audible at 3–4 m; source paper not opened.

## 2. Call parameters, calling behaviour, range and status for each species with a known voice

### Takeaway
Nine lost amphibians now have enough published numbers to synthesise directly (Kihansi spray toad, jambato, Venezuelan yellow frog, Mucubají and Tamá harlequin toads, northern Darwin's frog release call, golden coquí, Poynton's forest toad, Hula painted frog), and three more have precise verbal descriptions from the people who heard them (southern day frog, mountain mistfrog, northern gastric-brooding frog). Better numbers were also found for three implemented voices: Panamanian golden frog, sharp-snouted day frog and southern gastric-brooding frog.

### Cited Findings

#### 2.1 Kihansi spray toad — *Nectophrynoides asperginis* — Rank A
- Call structure: "brief call notes (or tone pulses), given either in isolation, or more commonly in bouts of 2–7 call notes at a constant internote interval". — [Arch et al. 2011](http://www.rzlab.pitt.edu/Publications/Arch-et-al-2011.pdf)
- Table 1 (4 males, 206–230 notes): note duration mean 37.4 ms (SD 13.3; range 5.6–75.0); internote interval 85.0 ms (SD 12.1; 58–143); interbout interval 930.7 ms (SD 266.1; 288–2,076); fundamental = dominant frequency 4,071 Hz (SD 165; 3,576–4,494). — [Arch et al. 2011](http://www.rzlab.pitt.edu/Publications/Arch-et-al-2011.pdf)
- Harmonics: multiple, "some reaching well into the ultrasonic range (up to 40 kHz)"; second harmonic on average 25–30 dB below the fundamental; half-width of F0 averages 783 ± 131 Hz. — [Arch et al. 2011](http://www.rzlab.pitt.edu/Publications/Arch-et-al-2011.pdf)
- Loudness: "generally soft", RMS 69–72 dB SPL at ~10 cm. — [Arch et al. 2011](http://www.rzlab.pitt.edu/Publications/Arch-et-al-2011.pdf)
- Behaviour: diurnal; males call mainly when in visual contact or engaged with another toad; three males out of sight of each other called 3.1 calls/min collectively, versus 30.8 calls/min during social encounters; aggressive males take a threat posture and duet. — [Arch et al. 2011](http://www.rzlab.pitt.edu/Publications/Arch-et-al-2011.pdf)
- Setting: main Kihansi falls are 100 m high; the falls are loud enough to prevent auditory bird censuses; pre-decline density 4.27 toads per m² (second highest recorded for any anuran). No recordings of the gorge's ambient noise exist; all recordings are from captivity. — [Arch et al. 2011](http://www.rzlab.pitt.edu/Publications/Arch-et-al-2011.pdf)
- Range and status: endemic to ~2 ha of spray wetland at the base of the Kihansi waterfall, Udzungwa escarpment, Tanzania; gorge ~4 km long, north–south. Population ~17,000, peak ~20,989 in June 2003; by January 2004 only three seen and two males heard calling; listed Extinct in the Wild May 2009; cause chiefly the 1999 Kihansi Dam cutting the spray. Reintroduction trials from 2012. — [Wikipedia: Kihansi spray toad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kihansi_spray_toad)
- Bounding box (est.): `[35.84, -8.60, 35.87, -8.56]`. Last heard in the wild: January 2004.

#### 2.2 Poynton's forest toad — *Nectophrynoides poyntoni* — Rank A (thin)
- "The male advertisement call consists of pulse trains about one second long, with an interval of about 2.5–3.5 seconds. The dominant frequency is about 2.9 kHz." — [Wikipedia, citing Menegon, Salvidio & Loader 2004](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nectophrynoides_poyntoni)
- Males call near streams, mainly after sunset but sometimes from late afternoon; found at night on leaves 0.6–1.6 m above ground. Moist submontane rainforest at 1,200 m, Mkalazi Valley, Udzungwa Mountains. Known from a single collection in 2003, when it was "reasonably common"; later targeted searches neither saw nor heard it. — [Wikipedia: Nectophrynoides poyntoni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nectophrynoides_poyntoni)
- Bounding box (est.): `[35.85, -8.45, 36.00, -8.30]`. Last heard: 2003. Status CR (possibly extinct).

#### 2.3 Harlequin toads (*Atelopus*) — genus template
- Cocroft et al. 1990 defined three call types, conservative across species: pulsed calls, pure tone calls, short calls. Abstract: the lack of divergence "may be related to the rarity of sympatry among species of Atelopus and to the relative importance of visual communication". — [Cocroft, McDiarmid, Jaslow & Ruiz-Carranza 1990, Copeia 1990: 631–643](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/5223703)
- Updated durations across the genus: pulsed calls 92–1,240 ms (16 species; advertisement); pure tone calls 159–420 ms (6 species; probably aggressive); pulsed short calls 5–171 ms (11 species; release). — [Lötters et al. 2019](https://herpetozoa.pensoft.net/article/39192/)
- Shape of a pulsed call: "maximum frequency and amplitude rising gradually and reaching a climax shortly before the end. The first pulse is about two times longer than the remaining ones." — [Lötters et al. 2019](https://herpetozoa.pensoft.net/article/39192/)
- All *Atelopus* lack a tympanum; all are stream-associated; calls are "inconspicuous". — [Lötters et al. 2019](https://herpetozoa.pensoft.net/article/39192/); [A. ignescens paper](https://herpetozoa.pensoft.net/article/134923/)
- Pulse-rate anchors from congeners: *A. fronterizo* 131.69 ± 16.39 pulses/s, 19–34 pulses, 176–235 ms, dominant 2,422.5–2,605.5 Hz; *A. limosus* 151.6 ± 3.1 pulses/s, max. frequency 2,689 ± 93 Hz; *A. varius* call 413–431 ms with 48–50 pulses; *A. certus* 0.30 ± 0.046 s, 38.5 ± 6.75 pulses; *A. glyphus* 0.25 ± 0.013 s, 35.1 ± 2.77 pulses. — [A new species of Atelopus from eastern Panama, PMC8175953](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8175953/)

#### 2.4 Venezuelan yellow frog — *Atelopus carbonerensis* — Rank A
- Pulsed (advertisement) call, n = 6: duration 575 ± 38 ms (478–623); interval between calls 3,601 ± 994 ms (1,473–5,389); pulse length 14 ± 4 ms (4–32); frequency band 2,580 ± 110 to 4,020 ± 62 Hz; dominant 3,410 ± 21 Hz (3,380–3,450). — [Lötters et al. 2019, Table 3](https://herpetozoa.pensoft.net/article/39192/)
- Pulsed short (release) call, n = 8: 136 ± 25 ms (74–171); dominant 1,430 ± 44 Hz; last pulse about four times longer than the others; frequency-modulated. — [Lötters et al. 2019](https://herpetozoa.pensoft.net/article/39192/)
- Pure tone call ("whistle"), n = 11: 273 ± 59 ms (202–347); dominant 1,390 ± 37 Hz (band 890–2,000 Hz). Pure tone short call, n = 13: 21 ± 10 ms; dominant 1,330 ± 151 Hz, downward-modulated. — [Lötters et al. 2019](https://herpetozoa.pensoft.net/article/39192/)
- Recording context: La Carbonera, San Eusebio forest, Mérida State; 25 May 1986 (~10 am, 2,400 m, 17.5 °C air; mic 0.3–0.7 m) and 10 June 1987 (shortly before noon, 2,330 m). The male was in amplexus; "various other, partly non-amplectant males were emitting the same call type, which can be heard on the original tape". Daytime caller; breeding aggregations in May–June. — [Lötters et al. 2019](https://herpetozoa.pensoft.net/article/39192/)
- Range and status: cloud forest along streams, 2,000–2,800 m, Mérida Andes; abundant until the 1980s; unrecorded 1998–2014; one seen 2014, a pair in 2021. — [Wikipedia: Atelopus carbonerensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atelopus_carbonerensis)
- Bounding box (est.): `[-71.60, 8.50, -71.20, 8.80]`.

#### 2.5 Mucubají stubfoot toad — *Atelopus mucubajiensis* — Rank A (non-advertisement calls only)
- Pure tone call, n = 8: 243 ± 48 ms (159–337); frequency-unmodulated, with harmonics; band ~1,760–3,170 Hz; dominant given as 2,420 Hz (range 2,340–2,580). Pure tone short call, n = 17: 41 ± 10 ms (22–57); descending. — [Lötters et al. 2019, Table 3](https://herpetozoa.pensoft.net/article/39192/)
- Recorded at Páramo La Corcovada near Laguna La Victoria (3,000 m; 23 June 1982, 4:40 pm) and near Laguna Mucubají (3,050 m; 7 June 1989), Mérida State; daytime. — [Lötters et al. 2019, Table 2](https://herpetozoa.pensoft.net/article/39192/)
- Páramo and sub-páramo among grasses and *Espeletia* and along streams; 2,300–3,500 m; range of a few km² in the Sierra de Santo Domingo; last observed 2004. The source of the pure tone call "is uncertain and could even have been a female". — [Wikipedia: Atelopus mucubajiensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atelopus_mucubajiensis)
- Bounding box (est.): `[-70.95, 8.70, -70.70, 8.90]`.

#### 2.6 Tamá harlequin frog — *Atelopus tamaense* — Rank A (release-type call only)
- Pure tone short call, n = 15: 22 ms (13–30); band 2,290–2,850 Hz; dominant 2,590 ± 36 Hz (2,540–2,640); frequency-modulated; emitted by a handled male. Recorded Páramo de Tamá, 2,950 m, Apure State, 12 August 1987, daytime. — [Lötters et al. 2019](https://herpetozoa.pensoft.net/article/39192/)
- Sphagnum bogs in páramo and upper montane forest, 2,950–3,200 m, Venezuela–Colombia border. — [Wikipedia: Atelopus tamaense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atelopus_tamaense)
- Bounding box (est.): `[-72.50, 7.30, -72.30, 7.50]`.

#### 2.7 Jambato — *Atelopus ignescens* — Rank A (presumed extinct ~1988–2016; one known population)
- "Low-intensity sounds, rendering them difficult to discern at close range"; the authors say they recall "rodent whispering". Three call types: tonal, short, pulsed. — [Herpetozoa 2024](https://herpetozoa.pensoft.net/article/134923/)
- Across all calls: duration 370 ± 461 ms (27–1,716); interval 7,466 ± 5,193 ms; 11.38 ± 7.19 calls/min; 1.56 ± 0.89 notes per call; note duration 66 ± 73 ms; dominant frequency 1.66 ± 0.07 kHz (min 1.50, max 1.78 kHz); up to 11 harmonics visible, the second at 3.32 kHz, the eleventh at 16.71 kHz. — [Herpetozoa 2024](https://herpetozoa.pensoft.net/article/134923/)
- Pulsed calls: 13–15 pulses; pulse duration 7.67 ± 4.26 ms (3–21); inter-pulse interval 4.76 ± 1.33 ms; 90.2 ± 24.7 pulses/s (62.5–142.9). Tonal calls are >100 ms and mostly downward-modulated; short calls ≤100 ms, downward-modulated. — [Herpetozoa 2024](https://herpetozoa.pensoft.net/article/134923/)
- Recorded 8 December 2021, 10:00–12:00, from grassland and herbs at ground level, Angamarca, Cotopaxi (1°7′51.31″S, 78°54′16.85″W; 2,962 m). Recordings are in Fonoteca Zoológica (Madrid). Peters (1973) had described the voice anecdotally as a slight, low sound hard to hear even at close range. — [Herpetozoa 2024](https://herpetozoa.pensoft.net/article/134923/)
- History: once "the most common, abundant, and widely distributed of the high Andean species in Ecuador"; crashed in the early 1980s and was believed extinct. — [Herpetozoa 2024](https://herpetozoa.pensoft.net/article/134923/)
- Bounding box (est., historical inter-Andean Ecuador): `[-79.20, -2.00, -78.00, 0.70]`.

#### 2.8 Chiriquí harlequin frog — *Atelopus chiriquiensis* — Rank A in the literature, numbers only partly retrieved
- Three vocalisations: buzzes (pulsed call; advertisement and territorial), chirps (short call) and whistles (pure tone; given in fights). Buzzing males within a couple of metres turn to face each other and advance while alternating buzzes; fights can last half an hour. — [Wikipedia: Atelopus chiriquiensis, citing Jaslow 1979](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atelopus_chiriquiensis)
- Jaslow (1979) noted whistles "in close-range encounters of *A. chiriquiensis* males, suggesting that they are aggressive calls". — [Lötters et al. 2019](https://herpetozoa.pensoft.net/article/39192/)
- Search-snippet numbers, origin not verified (probably a supplementary comparison table): pulsed call of 26–32 pulses, 0.386 s long, 73.86 pulses/s. — reported by search summary alongside [PMC8175953](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8175953/); primary source [Jaslow 1979, J. Herpetol. 13: 141–145](https://doi.org/10.2307/1563919) not opened.
- Diurnal; bred May–July, early wet season; stream margins in lower montane wet forest and rainforest, 1,400–2,500 m; Cordillera de Talamanca of Costa Rica and western Panama (Chiriquí, Bocas del Toro). Not seen in Costa Rica since 1996, nor in Panama since the late 1990s. Status EX. — [Wikipedia: Atelopus chiriquiensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atelopus_chiriquiensis)
- Bounding box (est.): `[-83.90, 8.60, -82.20, 9.80]`.

#### 2.9 Pass stubfoot toad — *Atelopus senex* — Rank A in the literature, numbers not retrieved
- A pulsed call was recorded and analysed by Cocroft et al. 1990. — [Lötters et al. 2019, Table 1](https://herpetozoa.pensoft.net/article/39192/)
- Costa Rica only: Cordillera Central and Cordillera de Talamanca, 1,100–2,200 m; stream margins in premontane and lower montane rainforest; formerly abundant; last seen 1986. — [Wikipedia: Atelopus senex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atelopus_senex)
- Bounding box (est.): `[-84.30, 9.40, -83.60, 10.30]`.

#### 2.10 Northern Darwin's frog — *Rhinoderma rufum* — Rank A
- Advertisement call: described by Penna & Veloso 1990 from eight individuals; relative to *R. darwinii*, *R. rufum* had more notes, a higher dominant frequency and longer calls and notes. Audio in Penna (2005). An unpublished comparison (Bidart-Enríquez et al., in prep.) finds the two species' advertisement calls "rather similar". — [Serrano et al. 2021](https://www.amphibian-reptile-conservation.org/pdfs/Volume/Vol_15_no_2/ARC_15_2_[General_Section]_318-322_e296.pdf)
- Penna & Veloso's sonograms came from frogs obtained in October 1980 near Concepción. — [Chile MMA species sheet](https://clasificacionespecies.mma.gob.cl/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/Rhinoderma_rufum_P07.pdf)
- Search-snippet (AmphibiaWeb account, not opened): mating call "a rapid 'pip, pip, pip, pip', with long pauses between repetitions"; dominant frequency about 3,000 Hz, at the upper limit for frogs of the austral temperate forest. — [AmphibiaWeb: Rhinoderma rufum](https://amphibiaweb.org/species/4323)
- Release call (4 males, lab, 20 °C, recorded 1981; Fonoteca Zoológica code 12943): "mid-pitched squeaks repeated in sustained trains", decaying to isolated notes. 43% of calls single notes; multi-note calls 2–13 notes (mean ~3.4); note rate 1.78–7.81 notes/s (mean 4.36); note duration 0.014–0.202 s (individual means 0.047–0.090 s); harmonic 1 at 474–1,895 Hz (mean band 628–817 Hz), harmonic 2 at 646–3,747 Hz (mean 1,213–1,530 Hz; usually the dominant), harmonic 3 at 991–3,661 Hz (mean 1,758–2,210 Hz); 97% of notes contain chaotic segments (chaos 9–100% of note); centre frequency of chaotic notes mean 1,238 Hz. — [Serrano et al. 2021, Table 1](https://www.amphibian-reptile-conservation.org/pdfs/Volume/Vol_15_no_2/ARC_15_2_[General_Section]_318-322_e296.pdf)
- Last record: four males captured near Paredones (34°38′39.6″S, 71°54′15.8″W) in 1981. — [Serrano et al. 2021](https://www.amphibian-reptile-conservation.org/pdfs/Volume/Vol_15_no_2/ARC_15_2_[General_Section]_318-322_e296.pdf)
- Range: central Chile, 33°30′S to 37°50′S; wet southern-beech forest at 50–500 m; humid shaded ravines, under stones and in wet vegetation near water. 223 sites surveyed 2008–2012, none seen or heard; modelled extinction date 1982 (95% CI 1980–2000); much of the range converted to pine and eucalypt plantation. — [Wikipedia: Chile Darwin's frog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chile_Darwin%27s_frog); [Soto-Azat et al. 2013, PLOS ONE](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3680453/); [MMA sheet](https://clasificacionespecies.mma.gob.cl/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/Rhinoderma_rufum_P07.pdf)
- Bounding box (est. from the stated latitudes): `[-73.30, -37.80, -71.20, -33.50]`. Status CR (Possibly Extinct).

#### 2.11 Golden coquí — *Eleutherodactylus jasperi* — Rank A (frequency and note count published)
- "The call of the golden coquí consists of two to six notes of the same tone and has been described as a 'tweet-tweet-tweet' … with a frequency of 5 kHz during nighttime" (Drewry 1986; Joglar 1998; Rivero 1998). Calling sequences typically last 10 to 120 seconds. — [USFWS 5-Year Review](https://ecosphere-documents-production-public.s3.amazonaws.com/sams/public_docs/species_nonpublish/3694.pdf)
- Search-snippet of Drewry & Jones 1976: the first call of a sequence has one to two notes, successive calls increase to a maximum of four to six notes and hold there; notes are "relatively pure tones of about 5 kHz". — [Wikipedia: Golden coquí](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_coqu%C3%AD)
- Calls are "less intense than most other frog species in the same habitat"; the species kept calling between midnight and dawn when other species subside; "unless it is unusually cold or dry, some males typically call for at least part of the night on most nights"; neighbouring males "join together in loose synchronization of call sequences". — [USFWS 5-Year Review](https://ecosphere-documents-production-public.s3.amazonaws.com/sams/public_docs/species_nonpublish/3694.pdf)
- Frequency niche among relatives: 5 kHz in *E. jasperi*, ~7 kHz in *E. gryllus*, ~8 kHz in *E. juanariveroi*. — [GBIF: Eleutherodactylus juanariveroi](https://www.gbif.org/species/119362963)
- Calling site: water-filled leaf axils of dense bromeliad clusters (*Vriesea*, *Hohenbergia*, *Guzmania*) on trees, rock edges and the ground; ~24 ha of described habitat on Cerro Avispa, Monte el Gato and the Sierra de Cayey. — [USFWS species page](https://www.fws.gov/species/golden-coqui-eleutherodactylus-jasperi)
- Elevation 647–785 m per Wikipedia; 700–850 m per USFWS. Last confirmed 1981 (5-Year Review, Wikipedia); the USFWS species page says "last time seen was in 1976". One unconfirmed call was heard on Cerro Avispa on 16 June 1986 from a bromeliad 3–4 m up a tree. — [USFWS 5-Year Review](https://ecosphere-documents-production-public.s3.amazonaws.com/sams/public_docs/species_nonpublish/3694.pdf); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_coqu%C3%AD); [USFWS species page](https://www.fws.gov/species/golden-coqui-eleutherodactylus-jasperi)
- Bounding box (est.): `[-66.15, 18.06, -66.00, 18.14]`.

#### 2.12 Eneida's coquí — *Eleutherodactylus eneidae* — Rank B
- Call composed of two different notes (listed with *E. coqui*, *E. portoricensis*, *E. antillensis*, *E. locustus* as two-note callers). — [Galvis et al., Bioacoustics](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/311497294_The_advertisement_calls_of_three_Eleutherodactylus_species_from_Hispaniola_Anura_Eleutherodactylidae)
- Status CR (IUCN 2021); mainly terrestrial; adult 1.0–1.2 inches. — [Wikipedia: Eneida's coquí](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eleutherodactylus_eneidae)

#### 2.13 Mount Glorious / southern day frog — *Taudactylus diurnus* — Rank B (primary description)
- "Contrary to Straughan and Lee (1966), it was found that the males of this species do call. Their call is a very soft chuckling sound repeated 1–2 or 4–5 times in quick succession. It is hardly audible in the field, probably because of its infrequent call and the loud background noises of rushing water current. The call could clearly be heard when males are placed in an aquarium in a quiet room. It takes about 4–5 minutes from one to the next call of one individual. Lack of a vocal sac may be the main cause of the softness of the call." — [Liem & Hosmer 1973](https://archive.org/download/biostor-153355/biostor-153355.pdf)
- Active only by day; rocky mountain creeks in rainforest at 500–600 m on Mt Glorious and wet sclerophyll forest at 400–500 m at Kondalilla; on rocks near water, creek banks and seepage ledges; dives and stays submerged up to 15 minutes; breeding peak late November to late April. — [Liem & Hosmer 1973](https://archive.org/download/biostor-153355/biostor-153355.pdf)
- Wikipedia gives 350–800 m, breeding late October to May with a January–March peak in warm weather during or after heavy rain. Disappeared from the D'Aguilar Range in 1975, the Blackall Range in 1978 and the Conondale Range in early 1979; declared extinct. FrogID gives peak calling October–December. Type locality Green's Falls, Mount Glorious (27°20′S, 152°46′E). — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Glorious_day_frog); [FrogID](https://frogid.net.au/frogs/taudactylus-diurnus); [recentlyextinctspecies.com](https://recentlyextinctspecies.com/anura/taudactylus-diurnus)
- Bounding box (est.): `[152.40, -27.50, 153.00, -26.40]`.

#### 2.14 Mountain mistfrog — *Ranoidea (Litoria / Mosleyia) nyakalensis* — Rank B

#### 2.15 Hula painted frog — *Latonia nigriventer* — Rank A (extant; lost 1955–2011)

#### 2.16 Wyoming toad — *Anaxyrus baxteri* — Rank A (recording exists; no published numbers found)
- "The mating call of the Wyoming toad has a low frequency along with a slow pulse rate, but the duration is longer than that of other types of toads." — [Wikipedia: Wyoming toad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wyoming_toad)
- Public recording: breeding calls at Mortenson Lake NWR, recorded ~10:30 pm (a companion item is dated 5 June 2014, 10:00 pm), 57 °F, humidity 67.8%, wind 2 mph, clear, half moon, elevation 7,289 ft. — [Acoustic Atlas](https://acousticatlas.org/item/1226)

Status EW (IUCN), with releases at Mortenson Lake. — [Wikipedia: Wyoming toad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wyoming_toad); [Wyoming Biodiversity Institute](https://www.wyobiodiversity.org/index.php/community-science/rocky-mountain-amphibian-project/monitoring-information/wyomings-amphibians/wyoming-toad-anaxyrus-baxteri)

#### 2.17 Better numbers for voices already implemented
- **Panamanian golden frog (*Atelopus zeteki*)**, 13 captive males, 260 calls: single utterances of up to 50 pulses, the first few pulses spaced wider than the rest; call duration 390–500 ms (one colony) and 470–650 ms (the other); pulse interval 7.9–10.1 ms and 8.8–11.1 ms (reported as "fundamental frequency" 99–126 Hz and 89–113 Hz); first dominant frequency 2,149–2,280 Hz and 1,678–2,390 Hz; second dominant band 4,759–7,900 Hz; some calls have gaps equal to 2–4 pulses. Daily vocal activity peaks 07:00–09:00 with a second peak 16:00–18:00; wild frogs were observed starting to call around 05:00 in the suspected breeding season. — [Critical calls, PLOS ONE 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10434951/)
- **Sharp-snouted day frog (*Taudactylus acutirostris*)**: "Sharp pitched tapping sound repeated 3–4 times in quick succession. Its loudness is between *T. diurnus* and *T. eungellensis*." - **Northern gastric-brooding frog (*R. vitellinus*)**: males "called from the water's edge during summer. The call was loud, consisting of several staccato notes. - **Rabbs' fringe-limbed treefrog (*Ecnomiohyla rabborum*)**: "warm up" owl-like calls of three to five notes followed by a single "grrrrrck" (a bark); calls only at night, in bouts of about one to two minutes whose intervals shorten; intensifies two to three days either side of full moon; peak mid-March to May (calls year-round); from a branch or vine near the tree hole or its mouth; cloud forest at 900–1,150 m above El Valle de Antón; last heard in the wild 2007 (one male, not seen). ### Inferences
- Implied pulse rates (my arithmetic): *A. zeteki* 7.9–11.1 ms pulse interval ≈ 90–127 pulses/s; *A. varius* 48–50 pulses in 413–431 ms ≈ 116 pulses/s; *A. chiriquiensis* 0.386 s × 73.86 /s ≈ 28.5 pulses, consistent with the "26–32" in the same snippet, which suggests the snippet reads "26–32 pulses per call".
- The *A. carbonerensis* table prints "pulse rate/sec 0.48", which cannot be a rate per second for a 575 ms call built of 14 ms pulses. Treat that cell as unreliable; a 575 ms call at a genus-typical 75–150 pulses/s would hold roughly 45–85 pulses (my estimate).
- *A. carbonerensis* has an unusually high advertisement dominant (3.41 kHz) beside low whistles (~1.4 kHz); *A. ignescens* sits at 1.66 kHz. A single "generic Atelopus buzz" would misrepresent both.
- The Kihansi toad's world was a wall of waterfall noise with thousands of 4.1 kHz ticks at 69–72 dB at 10 cm, audible only within centimetres to a metre; the natural rendering is waterfall first, toads only on close approach. The published recordings are captive, so any wild chorus texture is reconstruction.
- For *R. rufum* the only measured numbers are for the release call (a handling squeak). The advertisement "pip-pip-pip-pip" at ~3 kHz rests on a search-snippet plus the qualitative comparison with *R. darwinii*; obtaining Penna & Veloso 1990 would upgrade it.
- *T. diurnus* called once every 4–5 minutes and was inaudible over stream noise; like the golden toad, it belongs in the "nearly silent" register.
- Suggested ranks — A: Kihansi spray toad, *A. carbonerensis*, *A. ignescens*, *A. mucubajiensis*, *A. tamaense*, golden coquí, Poynton's forest toad, Hula painted frog, *R. rufum*, Wyoming toad (recording), pool frog. A-pending-paper: *A. chiriquiensis*, *A. senex*. B: southern day frog, mountain mistfrog, northern gastric-brooding frog, Eneida's coquí, Jamaican streamfrog, yellow-spotted bell frog, Chinese giant salamander. C: *A. vogli* (model: *A. cruciger*, whose pure tone calls run 180–290 ms per Lötters et al. 2019), Vegas Valley leopard frog (model: Chiricahua leopard frog), all *Pseudophilautus*, *Craugastor*, *Telmatobius*.

### Gaps
- Jaslow 1979 and Cocroft et al. 1990: dominant frequencies and pulse counts for *A. chiriquiensis*, *A. senex*, *A. minutulus*, *A. nicefori* not retrieved (JSTOR / Smithsonian repository refused access). The Smithsonian handle is https://repository.si.edu/handle/10088/4501.
- Penna & Veloso 1990 (J. Herpetol. 24: 23–33): numeric advertisement-call parameters for *R. rufum* not retrieved.
- Drewry & Rand 1983 (Copeia 1983: 941–953) and Drewry & Jones 1976: the per-species table for Puerto Rican *Eleutherodactylus* was not retrieved, so note duration, note rate and exact frequencies for *E. jasperi*, *E. eneidae* and *E. karlschmidti* remain unknown to me beyond "5 kHz, 2–6 notes" for *jasperi*.
- Wyoming toad: no published pulse rate, dominant frequency or call duration found. The Acoustic Atlas MP3 is public and could be measured directly; I did not download it.
- Chiricahua leopard frog call parameters (model for the Vegas Valley frog) not retrieved; Wikipedia's page has no call section.
- *Litoria castanea*: no measured parameters; the verbal description reached me only as a search-snippet.
- Elevation of the Kihansi spray wetlands and exact coordinates of the gorge and of the Mkalazi Valley were not sourced; the boxes are my estimates.
- Year last heard is often not distinguished from year last seen in the sources; only the Kihansi toad (two males heard January 2004), Rabbs' treefrog (heard 2007) and the golden coquí (unconfirmed call 1986) have explicit "heard" dates.

## 3. Vocal reptiles lost since ~1500

### Takeaway
No extinct reptile has a recorded voice, and for the extinct geckos there is not even a contemporary description of sound. The strongest reptile candidates are regional losses of living, loud species: Chinese alligator bellowing choruses on the lower Yangtze, crocodile roars on rivers where crocodiles were exterminated, and the mating bellows of giant tortoises on islands that lost them (Pinta, Floreana, the Mascarenes).

### Cited Findings

**Chinese alligator (*Alligator sinensis*) — CR; near-extinct in the wild; Rank A**
- Repertoire: a long-distance signal (bellowing, high SPL) and short-distance signals (tooting, bubble blowing, hissing, mooing, head slapping, whining; low SPL). All have "a very low dominant frequency, less than 500 Hz", interpreted as an adaptation to densely vegetated habitat. — [Wang et al. 2007, J. Acoust. Soc. Am. (PubMed 17550196)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17550196/)
- Bellowing choruses: usually solitary but chorus "in certain waters during the mating season"; both sexes; animals stay immobile and bellow roughly synchronously; choruses last about 10 minutes with abrupt onset and offset; playbacks did not attract animals, so the chorus is thought to gather individuals for mating. — [Wang et al. 2009, J. Acoust. Soc. Am. (PubMed 19813817)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19813817/)
- Bellow structure: "relatively chaotic vocalizations with very little harmonic signal"; fundamental not measurable; "a pulsed structure around 35 Hz". Bellows are given year-round by both sexes, most in the mating season. — [Reber et al. 2015, J. Exp. Biol.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4528706/)
- Across alligatorids, bellow dominant frequency 35.5–250 Hz; produced in air, head at an oblique angle, tail arched; "deep, throaty, sonorous"; may be repeated and given in chorus. — [Roars, Rumbles, and Resonance: review of crocodylian acoustic signals, Ecol. Evol. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12828176/)
- Range collapse: historically widespread in the Yangtze system and "very abundant in the lower Yangtze"; by the late 1980s confined to small ponds in six counties of south-eastern Anhui; area of occupancy ~5 km²; ~1,000 wild in the 1970s, 130–150 in 1999, fewer than 130 in 2001; at least 20,000 in captivity; reintroductions since 2007. — [Wikipedia: Chinese alligator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_alligator)
- Bounding box (est., historical lower and middle Yangtze): `[112.00, 28.50, 121.50, 32.50]`.

**Other crocodilian extirpations — Rank A/B for the species, voice inferred for the lost populations**
- Nile crocodile (*Crocodylus niloticus*): historic range "extended to the Mediterranean coast in Libya, Tunisia, Algeria, Morocco and across the Red Sea in the Palestine region and Syria", and from the Nile Delta along the whole Nile; extinct "in the last few centuries in Syria, Israel and Tunisia"; apparently extirpated as a breeding species from Egypt in the 20th century, since re-established around Lake Nasser. Males attract females "by bellowing, slapping their snouts in the water, blowing water out of their noses". — [Wikipedia: Nile crocodile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nile_crocodile)
- Levant specifics: Nahal Taninim ("crocodile stream", Wadi Zarqa) on Israel's Carmel coast is named for the Nile crocodiles of the nearby Kebara swamps; "the last crocodile was hunted in 1912" and is a mounted specimen now shown at Tel Aviv University's natural history museum. Another account gives 1905, killed by residents of Jisr az-Zarqa. — [Wikipedia: Nahal Taninim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nahal_Taninim) (search-snippet); contradicted on the date by [Science for the People: On Killing Crocodiles](https://magazine.scienceforthepeople.org/online/on-killing-crocodiles/) (search-snippet). Bounding box (est.): `[34.88, 32.50, 34.98, 32.60]`.
- Saltwater crocodile (*C. porosus*): "locally extinct in Cambodia, China, Seychelles, Thailand and Vietnam"; the Seychelles population, ~3,000 km from the next, was "extirpated … roughly 200 years ago"; until the 18th century it may have occurred on the lower Pearl River, Macau, Han and Min rivers and Hainan. — [Wikipedia: Saltwater crocodile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saltwater_crocodile)
- Gharial (*Gavialis gangeticus*): by 1976 confined to 2% of its historical range with fewer than 200 animals; common in the Indus in the early 20th century, almost extinct there by the early 1980s, none seen in 2008–09 surveys; locally extinct in Bhutan and Myanmar. The male's ghara "enables the males to emit a hissing sound that can be heard 75 m away". A "pop" signal was measured by Ajji & Lang 2025. — [Wikipedia: Gharial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gharial); [crocodylian review 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12828176/)
- Siamese crocodile (*C. siamensis*): thought extinct or nearly so in the wild in 1992; rediscovered in the Cardamom Mountains in 2000; 200–400 in Cambodia, remnants in Thailand, Vietnam, Laos. Only its hatchling distress call has published measurements. — [Wikipedia: Siamese crocodile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siamese_crocodile); [crocodylian review 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12828176/)
- Adult bellows/roars have been observed in *A. mississippiensis*, *A. sinensis*, *C. intermedius*, *C. mindorensis*, *C. niloticus*, *G. gangeticus* and *Melanosuchus niger*, but measured only in the two alligators and the black caiman. Mugger, saltwater, Cuban and Philippine crocodiles have no published acoustic measurements at all. Crocodylians are most vocal in the mating season and at night. — [crocodylian review 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12828176/)

**Giant tortoises — Rank B (living kin), C for the extinct island forms**
- Galápagos tortoise males "vocalise with hoarse bellows and grunts", described as "rhythmic groans", during mating — one of the few sounds they make; Darwin noted "a deep hiss" as a startled animal dropped. — [Wikipedia: Galápagos tortoise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gal%C3%A1pagos_tortoise)
- A comparative review lists *Chelonoidis nigra* as giving a "roar/bellow" when mounting (De Sola 1930; Evans 1949; Van Denburgh 1914) and the Aldabra tortoise a "groan/bellow" (Ernst & Barbour 1989). Mounting calls of Testudinidae are harmonic with frequency and amplitude modulation, and fundamental frequency falls with body size. — [Galeotti et al. 2005, Herpetological Journal 15: 61–71](https://www.thebhs.org/publications/the-herpetological-journal/volume-15-number-1-january-2005/380-01-do-mounting-vocalisations-in-tortoises-have-a-communication-function-a-comparative-analysis/file)
- Measured mounting calls of the largest species in that study (call duration; interval between calls; fundamental): *Geochelone sulcata* 255 ± 9 ms; 1.47 ± 0.09 s; 114.0 ± 4.0 Hz. *Chelonoidis carbonaria* 84 ± 2 ms; 0.29 ± 0.02 s; 109.9 ± 0.4 Hz. *C. denticulata* 907 ± 19 ms; 1.77 ± 0.32 s; 230.0 ± 18.6 Hz. *C. chilensis* 278 ± 23 ms; 2.15 ± 0.12 s; 209.2 ± 2.1 Hz. *Astrochelys radiata* 248 ± 2 ms; 1.03 ± 0.02 s; 115.8 ± 0.4 Hz. No giant tortoise was measured. — [Galeotti et al. 2005](https://www.thebhs.org/publications/the-herpetological-journal/volume-15-number-1-january-2005/380-01-do-mounting-vocalisations-in-tortoises-have-a-communication-function-a-comparative-analysis/file)
- Losses: Pinta tortoise (*C. n. abingdonii*) extinct 24 June 2012 with Lonesome George; Floreana tortoise extinct mid-19th century; Galápagos tortoises fell from over 250,000 in the 16th century to ~15,000 in the 1970s. — [Wikipedia: Galápagos tortoise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gal%C3%A1pagos_tortoise); [Wikipedia: Pinta Island tortoise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinta_Island_tortoise)
- Mascarene giant tortoises (*Cylindraspis*, on Mauritius, Réunion, Rodrigues): most species gone by 1795, the last individuals reputedly around 1840. — [Wikipedia: Cylindraspis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylindraspis)
- Bounding boxes (est.): Pinta `[-90.85, 0.53, -90.70, 0.62]`; Floreana `[-90.52, -1.36, -90.34, -1.22]`; Mauritius `[57.30, -20.55, 57.80, -19.95]`; Réunion `[55.20, -21.40, 55.85, -20.85]`; Rodrigues `[63.30, -19.80, 63.50, -19.65]`.

**Extinct geckos — Rank C**
- Rodrigues giant day gecko (*Phelsuma gigas*): Leguat described "another sort of nocturnal lizard of grayish colour … as big and as long as one's arm"; arboreal, on latan palms; last collected 1842 on Île aux Frégates off Rodrigues. Neither Leguat's passage nor the page mentions a voice. — [Wikipedia: Rodrigues giant day gecko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rodrigues_giant_day_gecko)
- Rodrigues day gecko (*Phelsuma edwardnewtoni*): extinct, date given as 1917. — [Wikipedia: Rodrigues day gecko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phelsuma_edwardnewtoni)
- Delcourt's giant gecko (*Gigarcanum delcourti*): one unlabelled mounted specimen in Marseille, in the collection since at least the 1870s; 37 cm snout–vent; now attributed to New Caledonia rather than New Zealand; no natural-history data. — [Wikipedia: Gigarcanum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gigarcanum)
- Living model for *Gigarcanum*: *Rhacodactylus leachianus* makes "a loud growling noise, and local people call it 'the devil in the trees'"; clucking at night associated with mate calling; a light whistle under mild stress; bird-like chirping in threat display. — [Wikipedia: Rhacodactylus leachianus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhacodactylus_leachianus)
- Jamaican giant gecko (*Tarentola albertschwartzi*): one specimen, accessioned 1884, "possibly extinct"; no voice data. — [Wikipedia: Tarentola albertschwartzi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarentola_albertschwartzi)

### Inferences
- A Chinese alligator chorus is the most defensible reptile addition: measured structure (chaotic, ~35 Hz pulsing, energy below 500 Hz), measured social form (~10-minute synchronous choruses, both sexes, abrupt start and stop, mating season), and a clear lost range along the lower Yangtze.
- For Pinta, Floreana and the Mascarenes, a tortoise mating bellow is Rank C: a slow series of low harmonic groans, one per ~1–2 s, with a fundamental at or below ~110 Hz. The frequency is my extrapolation from the stated inverse size–frequency relationship and the 110–115 Hz values of the far smaller *G. sulcata* and *C. carbonaria*; no giant tortoise was measured in the source.
- Nile crocodile roars for the Levant and lower Nile would have to borrow alligator bellow measurements; adult crocodile roars have been observed but not measured.
- Extinct geckos are speculative. *Phelsuma* is a largely quiet day-gecko genus (my general knowledge, not sourced), so Leguat's nocturnal *P. gigas* cannot be assumed vocal; *Gigarcanum* is on firmer ground because its closest large relative is conspicuously loud, but its provenance is a single unlabelled specimen.

### Gaps
- The date of the last Levantine crocodile (1912 vs 1905) rests on search-snippets of two pages I did not open; the Nile Delta / lower Nile extirpation has no date beyond "the 20th century".
- Wang et al. 2007's bellow duration, exact dominant frequency and SPL values were not retrieved (abstract only).
- No acoustic measurements of Galápagos or Aldabra tortoise mounting calls were found; De Sola 1930, Evans 1949 and Van Denburgh 1914 were not opened.
- Liénard's 1842 account of *P. gigas* was not consulted, so whether he mentions a call is unknown.
- Mugger, Orinoco, Cuban and Philippine crocodile regional losses were not researched; the 2026 review confirms none of them has published acoustic measurements.

### Inferences
- Three of these (Kihansi toad, southern day frog, golden coquí) are "barely audible" voices; together with the already-implemented golden toad they form a coherent set where the habitat sound carries the scene and the animal is a trace.
- The harlequin toads give the catalogue an Andean day-chorus it currently lacks, and the published tables distinguish species well enough to avoid a single generic buzz.
- Upgrading the implemented Panamanian golden frog with the 2023 PLOS ONE numbers (390–650 ms, up to 50 pulses at ~8–11 ms intervals, ~1.7–2.4 kHz, dawn peak) is low-effort and well sourced.

### Gaps
- Obtaining four papers would close most of the remaining numeric gaps: Jaslow 1979; Cocroft et al. 1990; Penna & Veloso 1990; Drewry & Rand 1983.
- Fonoteca Zoológica (Madrid) holds the *R. rufum* release calls (code 12943) and the *A. ignescens* recordings, and Penna (2005) contains *R. rufum* advertisement calls; none was listened to or measured here.
