# Lost birds of the Americas and Atlantic islands missing from the "elsewhere" lost-voices catalogue

Scope: EX / EW / CR(PE) / "lost" / regionally extirpated bird taxa, lost since ~1500, of North, Central and South America, the Caribbean, Galápagos and the Atlantic islands. Hawaiʻi and Aotearoa NZ excluded. Taxa already implemented or already catalogued (per `_already_covered.md`) are only mentioned where new sound evidence turned up.

Method notes for the report writer:
- The sweep was done against the full wikitext of Wikipedia's "List of extinct bird species since 1500" (species section and "possibly extinct subspecies" section), the Search for Lost Birds list, and ~170 individual Wikipedia species pages pulled as raw text; primary 19th-century descriptions were read directly from archive.org full texts (Bryant 1887, Ridgway 1876, Gosse 1847) and from Auk/Condor PDFs (Nelson 1898, Abbott 1933, Dennis 1948, Tanner 1964, Johnston 1969) retrieved through the Wayback Machine because sora.unm.edu now redirects.
- Not reachable: Biodiversity Heritage Library (bot challenge), BirdLife Data Zone (page body does not render), Macaulay Library and xeno-canto (catalogue numbers below come from secondary sources, not from the archives themselves), eBird. WebSearch quota ran out part-way; remaining searches used a second search tool one query at a time.
- ALL bounding boxes `[lon0, lat0, lon1, lat1]` in this file were estimated by me from general geography, not taken from a source. Treat them as rough.
- Provenance tiers used: RECORDED (a real recording of the lost taxon exists), DESCRIBED (words by someone who heard it), INFERRED (only a living relative).

## 1. Which lost bird taxa in this region are absent from the covered list?

### Takeaway
About 75 lost bird taxa (species plus distinctive subspecies and regional extirpations) in the region are neither implemented nor in the earlier "catalogued" list; most are obscure island subspecies with no sound evidence, but roughly fifteen have a real recording or a usable first-hand description. The single richest untapped cluster is Guadalupe Island (Mexico), where one 1887 paper gives first-hand voice notes for six lost taxa.

### Inferences
- Of the Lost Birds entries, those absent from both the implemented and catalogued lists are: Cuban kite, Guanacaste hummingbird, Semper's warbler, St Kitts bullfinch, Zapata rail, coppery thorntail, Duida grass-finch, kinglet calyptura, Paria barbtail, purple-winged ground dove, Rio de Janeiro antwren, saffron-breasted redstart, Vilcabamba brushfinch, white-faced redstart, Cayenne nightjar. Several of these (Duida grass-finch, Paria barbtail, the two tepui redstarts) are "lost" only in the sense of no documentation for 10+ years in rarely visited places, not presumed extinct, so they are weak fits for a mode about voices that can no longer be heard.
- "Least vermilion flycatcher" in the brief is the same bird as the already-catalogued San Cristóbal flycatcher (*Pyrocephalus dubius*, last reliable sighting 1987) — not a separate gap.
- St Helena's and the Macaronesian scops owls' extinctions sit right at or before the 1500 cut-off; they are borderline for the mode's "since ~1500" rule.

### Gaps
- I could not open the BirdLife Data Zone or IUCN Red List to verify current categories directly; statuses below are as reported by Wikipedia or the cited papers.
- recentlyextinctspecies.com (REPAD) was only spot-checked (Semper's warbler, Amak song sparrow, Grand Cayman thrush via search), not swept page by page; it may list further obscure subspecies.
- Regional extirpations of still-living species were not swept systematically (no list source exists); those noted are ones encountered. Obvious unexamined candidates with loud voices include whooping crane (lost from most of its range), trumpeter swan in the east, scarlet macaw in El Salvador and much of Mexico, yellow-eared parrot in Ecuador, red siskin on Trinidad.

## 2. For each: is its voice RECORDED, DESCRIBED, or only INFERRED?

### Takeaway
Only two fully lost taxa in the gap list have real recordings (purple-winged ground dove, Bahama nuthatch); a third recording-backed group is regional extirpations of living species. First-hand verbal descriptions exist for about a dozen more, the best being Bryant's and Palmer's Guadalupe notes; everything else is inference from relatives, and several "lost" taxa have a voice that was never described at all.

### Cited Findings

RECORDED (real recordings of the lost taxon itself)
- Purple-winged ground dove: "the first recording of the species vocalizations which we obtained from interviews with aviculturists" — from Carlos Keller, 1992, archived as Macaulay Library ML273900, "a low-quality recording of four Purple-winged Ground Dove calls"; captive birds. — [Lees et al. 2021, Frontiers in Ecology and Evolution](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/ecology-and-evolution/articles/10.3389/fevo.2021.624959/full)
- Bahama nuthatch: vocalization recordings by David Pereira, 26 June 2018, West Grand Bahama (xeno-canto XC614665, per search result); a Bahama male vocalization extracted from 2012 video (P. Merritt) was analysed spectrographically. — [xeno-canto XC614665](https://www.xeno-canto.org/614665); [Levy & Cox 2020, Avian Conservation and Ecology](https://www.ace-eco.org/vol15/iss2/art15/)
- Regional extirpations with recordings of the living species: Galápagos rail (six acoustic records on Floreana itself, 2025), black-capped petrel (vocalizations detected over Dominica 2015), white-necked crow (xeno-canto recordings exist), thick-billed parrot, Bermuda petrel. — [Charles Darwin Foundation](https://www.darwinfoundation.org/en/news/all-news-stories/galapagos-rail-rediscovered-on-floreana/); [BirdsCaribbean 2015](https://www.birdscaribbean.org/2015/07/rediscovery-of-black-capped-petrels-on-dominica/); [White-necked crow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White-necked_crow)
- Near-lost but extant, with recordings: Stresemann's bristlefront ("handful of ... audio recordings"), Belem curassow (alarm calls, XC395276), blue-eyed ground dove, cherry-throated tanager, Táchira antpitta (first sound recordings made by the rediscovery expedition, though xeno-canto had none as of late 2024), Santa Marta sabrewing (one recording as of mid-2022). — [Stresemann's bristlefront](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stresemann%27s_bristlefront); [Belem curassow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belem_curassow); [Tachira antpitta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tachira_antpitta); [Santa Marta sabrewing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santa_Marta_sabrewing)

INFERRED ONLY: every other taxon in section 1 (model species given in section 4).

### Inferences
- No reconstruction or "re-synthesis" recordings were encountered for any of these taxa; the only risk of confusing a real recording with something else is the purple-winged ground dove tape, which the paper itself says is low quality and possibly slowed (see dossier).
- The Guadalupe set is unusual in that one observer, in one season (Dec 1885–Apr 1886), heard six taxa that are all now gone, on an island already covered in the catalogue only by its caracara and petrel.

### Gaps
- Sick's (1997) verbal description of the purple-winged ground dove's call is cited by Lees et al. but its wording was not retrieved.
- Whether the 2021 St Kitts sonogram has been analysed or archived is unknown.
- I could not confirm from Macaulay or xeno-canto directly how many Bahama nuthatch recordings exist or their catalogue numbers beyond XC614665.

## 3. Status of the named candidates to check

### Takeaway
Of the candidates named in the brief, the ones that turn out to be both uncovered and usable are purple-winged ground dove, Bahama nuthatch (not named but found), the Guadalupe flicker/towhee/kinglet, Grand Cayman thrush, Semper's warbler, kinglet calyptura, Tristan moorhen and Jamaican wood rail; several others are invalid taxa, still alive, or have no sound evidence whatsoever.

### Cited Findings
- Guadalupe caracara and storm petrel (catalogued earlier): primary voice descriptions now found — see dossiers 4.1 and 4.2. — [Ridgway 1876](https://archive.org/details/bulletinofunited21876geol); [Bryant 1887](https://archive.org/details/bulletinofcalifo021887cali)
- Guadalupe flicker and towhee ("not researched" earlier): both have first-hand voice descriptions — dossiers 4.3, 4.4. — [Bryant 1887](https://archive.org/details/bulletinofcalifo021887cali)
- Texas Henslow's sparrow ("not researched"): known only from a 105-acre brushfield near Houston, gone after devegetation for industrial development in the 1980s; described as *houstonensis* (Arnold 1983) but now treated as within the variation of the nominate subspecies (Browning 1990). — [Henslow's sparrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henslow%27s_sparrow)
- Cuban macaw relatives: Gundlach reported the Cuban macaw "vocalized loudly like its Central American relatives"; the last pair on Isla de la Juventud was reportedly shot near La Vega in 1864. Jamaican macaws: Hill wrote that "their harsh scream, heard in the hushed mountain solitudes" betrays them and that they journey "in pairs" from ridge to ridge (he suspected they were visitors from Mexico). — [Cuban macaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuban_macaw); [Gosse 1847](https://archive.org/details/birdsofjamaica00goss)
- Brace's emerald: one male shot 13 July 1877 about three miles from Nassau; no voice information. Gould's emerald: one specimen of unknown origin. — [Brace's emerald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brace%27s_emerald); [Gould's emerald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gould%27s_emerald)
- Jamaican pauraque: voice never described. — [Jamaican poorwill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamaican_poorwill)
- Imperial woodpecker (implemented): no recording; the 1956 Rhein film is the only footage; voice accounts in dossier 4.14. — [Imperial woodpecker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_woodpecker)
- Colombian grebe (catalogued): still abundant on Lake Tota in 1945, c. 300 birds by 1968, last confirmed 1977 (three birds); no voice information found. — [Colombian grebe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colombian_grebe)
- Hooded seedeater: now redirected to yellow-bellied seedeater — a single specimen that "bounced among full species status and other interpretations"; not a usable taxon. Tumaco seedeater: redirects to chestnut-throated seedeater (lumped). — [Yellow-bellied seedeater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellow-bellied_seedeater); [Chestnut-throated seedeater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chestnut-throated_seedeater)
- Kinglet calyptura: rediscovered Oct 1996 (two birds seen 27–30 Oct on the edge of Serra dos Órgãos National Park), not reliably recorded since. — [Birds of the World intro](https://birdsoftheworld.org/bow/species/kincal1/cur/introduction); [Kinglet calyptura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinglet_calyptura)
- Rio de Janeiro antwren: holotype collected 1988 (Lost Birds gives 1981), a few later sightings at REGUA; BirdLife/HBW treat it as a colour morph of silvery-flanked antwren. — [Rio de Janeiro antwren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rio_de_Janeiro_antwren); [Search for Lost Birds](https://www.searchforlostbirds.org/birds)
- Stresemann's bristlefront: not extinct but nearly — a single female seen 12 and 14 Dec 2018 in Bahia; the single documented bird "was last seen in fall 2020". Song "starts with clicks followed by a trill", like slaty bristlefront but lower pitched; responds to playback. — [BirdGuides](https://www.birdguides.com/news/lifeline-for-stresemanns-bristlefront/); [American Bird Conservancy](https://abcbirds.org/news/stresemanns-bristlefront-get-additional-habitat-protection/); [Stresemann's bristlefront](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stresemann%27s_bristlefront)
- Cherry-throated tanager: extant (rediscovered 1998); call "péuuu" or "peéyr", clear and far-carrying, sequences 0.3–1 s. Blue-eyed ground dove: extant; song "a series of evenly-spaced, soft cooing notes" at about 1.5 notes per second. Táchira antpitta and Santa Marta sabrewing: refound. All out of scope as "lost". — [Cherry-throated tanager](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cherry-throated_tanager); [Blue-eyed ground dove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue-eyed_ground_dove)
- Turquoise-throated puffleg (catalogued): six 19th-century specimens, one unconfirmed sighting 1976; no voice information. Guanacaste hummingbird: holotype only. — [Turquoise-throated puffleg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turquoise-throated_puffleg); [Guanacaste hummingbird](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guanacaste_hummingbird)
- Semper's warbler: dossier 4.9.
- St Helena hoopoe, cuckoo, crake, rail: known from subfossils; hoopoe "last seen around 1550 ... though it may have lasted into 1640"; cuckoo known from a single humerus. No sound evidence. — [Saint Helena hoopoe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Helena_hoopoe); [Saint Helena cuckoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Helena_cuckoo)
- Ascension crake: Peter Mundy, June 1656 — "a strange kind of fowle, much bigger then our sterlings ... eies red like rubies, wings very imperfitt ... They were taken running, in which they are exceeding swift" — a visual account with no sound. — [Ascension crake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ascension_crake)
- Bermuda flicker: John Smith 1623 lists "Wood-pickars" among Bermuda's birds, alongside small birds "lately beene destroyed by the wilde Cats". Bermuda towhee: Strachey's "Sparrowes fat and plumpe like a Bunting, bigger then ours" may refer to it. — [Bermuda flicker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bermuda_flicker); [Bermuda towhee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bermuda_towhee)
- Tristan moorhen: dossier 4.12.
- Madeiran wood pigeon: already rare when Ernst Schmitz lived on Madeira (1896–1906); none found in May 1924 or later. Scops owls: extinction attributed to 15th-century settlement. Azores bullfinch is extant (contact call "a distinctive short, flute-like, melancholic whistle"). — [Madeiran wood pigeon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madeiran_wood_pigeon); [Azores bullfinch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azores_bullfinch)
- Cape Verde kite: "almost certainly extinct"; taxonomic validity questioned. — [Red kite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_kite)
- Galápagos: see dossier 4.19 (rail) and the Floreana list in section 1.

### Inferences
- Hooded seedeater, Tumaco seedeater, Rio de Janeiro antwren, Texas Henslow's sparrow, Amak song sparrow and Bogotá sunangel-type single-skin hummingbirds are taxonomically shaky; adding them risks giving a voice to something that was never a distinct bird. If the artwork wants the Houston or Amak story, it should be framed as a lost local population, not a lost form.
- Stresemann's bristlefront, cherry-throated tanager and blue-eyed ground dove belong to a different category ("can barely still be heard") from the mode's premise.

### Gaps
- No source found giving any sound for Brace's emerald, Guanacaste hummingbird, coppery thorntail, Cayenne nightjar, St Helena or Ascension rails, Bermuda hawk/owl.
- Conflicting last dates for Rio de Janeiro antwren (1981 vs 1988 holotype, later REGUA sightings) not resolved.

## 4. Candidate dossiers

### Takeaway
Nineteen dossiers follow, ordered roughly by strength of sound evidence. Measured acoustic parameters exist only for the purple-winged ground dove and Bahama nuthatch; for all others the synthesis has to work from verbal descriptions plus a named living model.

### Cited Findings

#### 4.1 Guadalupe caracara — "quelili" / "calalie" — *Caracara lutosa* (catalogued earlier; new sound evidence)
- Range: Guadalupe Island, Mexico; "abundant on every part of the island" in 1875; "Their range extends over the entire island, from beach to summit". bbox [-118.42, 28.85, -118.18, 29.20] (my estimate). — [Ridgway 1876](https://archive.org/details/bulletinofunited21876geol); [Bryant 1887](https://archive.org/details/bulletinofcalifo021887cali)
- Voice (Palmer 1875): "When surprised or wounded, they emit a loud, harsh scream, something like that of the Bald Eagle. In fighting among themselves, they make a curious gabbling noise; and under any special excitement the same sounds are given forth, with an odd motion of the head, the neck being first stretched out to its full length and then bent backward till the head almost rests upon the back. The same odd motions are made and similar noises emitted when the birds are about to make an attack upon a kid." Group attacks happened "with great noise and flapping of their wings". — [Ridgway 1876](https://archive.org/details/bulletinofunited21876geol)
- Voice (Bryant 1885–86): "As far as my observations went, the birds were entirely silent, but the agent informed me that when perchance a rifle ball carried away a wing or a foot, the unfortunate bird would scream long and loudly." — [Bryant 1887](https://archive.org/details/bulletinofcalifo021887cali)
- Abundance and decline: great numbers in 1875; only four seen in two days in January 1885; the island agent shot them at drinking pools. Local names "Queleli" (Bryant) / "Calalie" (Palmer). Breeding thought to be late May–June, on cliffs. — [Bryant 1887](https://archive.org/details/bulletinofcalifo021887cali); [Ridgway 1876](https://archive.org/details/bulletinofunited21876geol)
- Last: Rollo Beck, afternoon of 1 December 1900 — "Of 11 birds that flew toward me 9 were secured." One unconfirmed sighting 1903; gone by 1906. — [Abbott 1933](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://sora.unm.edu/sites/default/files/journals/condor/v035n01/p0010-p0014.pdf); [Guadalupe caracara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guadalupe_caracara)
- Cultural note: "one of the few species that were intentionally rendered extinct by humans", by goat herders' shooting and poisoning. — [Guadalupe caracara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guadalupe_caracara)
- Tier: DESCRIBED. Model: crested caracara *Caracara plancus*.

#### 4.2 Guadalupe storm petrel — *Hydrobates macrodactylus* (catalogued earlier; new sound evidence)
- Voice (Bryant, Jan–Mar 1886): birds came "only on the darkest nights or, if any were flying during moonlight, they were entirely silent. After the setting of the moon ... even though as late as four o'clock in the morning, they would make their appearance with their peculiar call. ... they seemed to call hurriedly, 'here's-a-letter,' 'here's-a-letter,' and then from the darkness came the reply from another that I supposed to be at rest, 'for you,' 'for you.'" In the hand: "Seldom did a bird make a sound when seized, but occasionally a cry like that of a bird in distress would escape them. One individual, however, while being unearthed, kept up the peculiar night-call". His companion first took them for "little owls"; flight "as erratic as that of a bat". — [Bryant 1887](https://archive.org/details/bulletinofcalifo021887cali)
- Habitat and season: burrows one to three feet long under boulders and fallen branches on the pine ridge, south to the small cypress grove; fifty eggs taken 4–5 March; breeding slotted between the winter-breeding Ainley's and summer-breeding Townsend's storm petrels; almost all young gone by mid-June. — [Bryant 1887](https://archive.org/details/bulletinofcalifo021887cali); [Guadalupe storm petrel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guadalupe_storm_petrel)
- Abundance: Bryant dug about eighty burrows in two days; still "abundant" in 1906 but cat-killed remains everywhere; last breeding record 1912; 2000 survey at the right season found none. Status CR (Possibly Extinct). bbox as 4.1, concentrated on the northern ridge. — [Guadalupe storm petrel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guadalupe_storm_petrel)
- Tier: DESCRIBED. Model: Leach's storm petrel (in the field the two "could not be told apart"). — [Guadalupe storm petrel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guadalupe_storm_petrel)

#### 4.3 Guadalupe flicker — "carpentero" — *Colaptes auratus rufipileus*
- Voice (Bryant): a pair bobbing heads at each other gave "a low chuckling sound"; a tame female came close "whistling 'quit-tu,' 'quit-tu'"; "In addition to the familiar scythe-whetting notes they have the peculiar 'wake-up' call and its rapid prelude of monosyllables. By imitating this call I decoyed a distant female to within short range ... stopping at short intervals to call and listen for a reply." He also heard "the sounding taps of a woodpecker at work". — [Bryant 1887](https://archive.org/details/bulletinofcalifo021887cali)
- Habitat, abundance, season: "not rare in the restricted area of the large cypress grove, but apart from this locality less than a dozen were seen"; a few among palms near the east beach and one in the northern pines; Palmer (1875): "Rare, and apparently only found in the [pi]ne woods of the north end" (the scan's OCR reads "ne woods"; "pine" is my reading). In pairs by 16 March; six fresh eggs on 7 April; nests 3–15 ft up in half-dead cypress or palm stumps. Exceptionally tame. — [Bryant 1887](https://archive.org/details/bulletinofcalifo021887cali); [Ridgway 1876](https://archive.org/details/bulletinofunited21876geol)
- Last recorded 1906; not found 1911 or 1922; subspecies "may be invalid"; mainland flickers have begun recolonising. bbox as 4.1. — [Wikipedia list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_extinct_bird_species_since_1500)
- Tier: DESCRIBED. Model: red-shafted northern flicker (call "a sustained laugh, ki ki ki ki"; drums on resonant objects). — [Northern flicker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_flicker)

#### 4.4 Guadalupe towhee — *Pipilo maculatus consobrinus*
- Voice (Bryant): the male "mounted his throne on the topmost branch of a cypress and ... sounded his morning trill. This song closely resembles that of *P. maculatus megalonyx*, but has one important variation which was almost invariably given ... a single quick note, somewhat like a bluebird's, given immediately before the trill, as though it was the click or chuck of the machinery that released the sound which followed. At a distance, when the trill could be distinctly heard, the single quick chuck would pass unnoticed." — [Bryant 1887](https://archive.org/details/bulletinofcalifo021887cali)
- Voice (Palmer 1875): "When startled, they emit a short whistle of three or four syllables"; they scratch in fallen leaves with a two-footed backward jump. — [Ridgway 1876](https://archive.org/details/bulletinofunited21876geol)
- Habitat and abundance: by 1886 "found only in the large cypress grove"; singing birds were always males and only two females were seen; 1875: "Not abundant; found chiefly in the woods of the central portion", in brushwood, fallen logs, fences. Dawn singer. Extinct c. 1900 (not found 1897). bbox as 4.1. — [Bryant 1887](https://archive.org/details/bulletinofcalifo021887cali); [Wikipedia list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_extinct_bird_species_since_1500)
- Tier: DESCRIBED. Model: spotted towhee.

#### 4.5 Guadalupe kinglet — "canaria" — *Corthylio calendula obscurus*
- Voice (Bryant): "In December I found them in full song and as common as in April ... Their song is indescribably sweet and musical, and of wonderful power for so small a bird, commencing with a few low, quick notes, as though the singer were merely trying his voice, then bursting into a full animated warble, it ends in a dissyllabic measure, accented on the first syllable, and usually repeated from three to six times. One remarkably fine songster repeated the final dissyllable eight or ten times. Only once did I hear the metallic click". The male also has "a peculiar, low song" sung close to the nest. — [Bryant 1887](https://archive.org/details/bulletinofcalifo021887cali)
- Habitat: most numerous in the large cypress grove, also the smaller grove and the pines; nests over 20 ft up in cypress tops, building from mid-February, eggs 24 March. Palmer saw a singing male raise its red crown only while the notes lasted. Not recorded since 1953. bbox as 4.1. — [Bryant 1887](https://archive.org/details/bulletinofcalifo021887cali); [Ridgway 1876](https://archive.org/details/bulletinofunited21876geol); [Wikipedia list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_extinct_bird_species_since_1500)
- Tier: DESCRIBED. Model: ruby-crowned kinglet — song in three parts: high notes (zee-zee-zee), two to five low trills (turr), then a repeated three-note "galloping" phrase (tee-da-leet). — [Ruby-crowned kinglet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruby-crowned_kinglet)
- Related, already catalogued: Guadalupe Bewick's wren — in 1886 confined to an area "sixty by three hundred feet" of pine windfall; "A frightened female uttered a few 'twit' 'twits' of alarm, but with this exception they were utterly silent." — [Bryant 1887](https://archive.org/details/bulletinofcalifo021887cali)

#### 4.6 Purple-winged ground dove — *Paraclaravis geoffroyi*
- Range and habitat: Atlantic Forest of SE Brazil, far eastern Paraguay and Misiones, Argentina (range c. 644,000 km²); nomadic follower of masting *Guadua* bamboo — in Argentina "takuarusu (*Guadua chacoensis*) and yatevo (*G. trinii*)". bbox [-56.5, -28.5, -39.0, -13.0] (my estimate). — [Lees et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/ecology-and-evolution/articles/10.3389/fevo.2021.624959/full); [Purple-winged ground dove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purple-winged_ground_dove)
- Last documented wild record: 15 January 1985 (female specimen, Rio de Janeiro); unverified sightings continue, most recently 2017 in Argentina. Status: Critically Endangered, possibly extinct; if extant, 50–249 birds. — [Lees et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/ecology-and-evolution/articles/10.3389/fevo.2021.624959/full); [Purple-winged ground dove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purple-winged_ground_dove)
- Recording: ML273900, obtained from aviculturist Carlos Keller, 1992, four calls of captive birds. Keller said the call "sounded strange" as if the tape was "running at low velocity", and sounded more accurate pitched up by two semitones. — [Lees et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/ecology-and-evolution/articles/10.3389/fevo.2021.624959/full)
- Acoustic detail: calls ascend in pitch, last about 0.5 s and lie below 500 Hz; the sympatric violaceous quail-dove's call "has a longer duration (about 1 s), descends in pitch and occupies frequencies above 500 Hz". — [Lees et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/ecology-and-evolution/articles/10.3389/fevo.2021.624959/full)
- Model for phrasing: sister species maroon-chested ground dove — "a series of low-pitched, slightly rising bisyllable coos 'cuWOOP.....cuWOOP.....cuWOOP...'". — [Maroon-chested ground dove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maroon-chested_ground_dove)
- Context: kept and bred by Brazilian aviculturists from the 1970s to late 1980s (over 150 captive birds at peak); the captive stock died out by the mid-1990s after regulatory changes dispersed it. Wikipedia compares its bamboo-mast dependence and collapse to the passenger pigeon. — [Purple-winged ground dove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purple-winged_ground_dove)
- Tier: RECORDED (captive, one low-quality tape, pitch uncertain by about two semitones).

#### 4.7 Bahama nuthatch — *Sitta insularis*
- Range and habitat: pineyards of Grand Bahama only (Pleistocene fossils on Abaco and Long Island); since the 2000s all observations in or around Lucaya Estates (~13,000 ha of mature Caribbean pine with bracken, poisonwood, silver palm). bbox [-78.95, 26.48, -77.85, 26.80] (my estimate). — [Bahama nuthatch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahama_nuthatch); [Levy & Cox 2020](https://www.ace-eco.org/vol15/iss2/art15/)
- Decline: locally common in 1969 and 1978 surveys; 2 birds in 1993; about 23 in 2007; seven individuals at three sites in 2012; none in January 2018 after Hurricane Matthew, then a few sightings (including two birds together) in spring–summer 2018; Hurricane Dorian (Sept 2019; 24 hours of 295 km/h winds, saltwater flooding of the pineyards) probably finished it. Status CR, "very likely extinct". — [Bahama nuthatch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahama_nuthatch); [Levy & Cox 2020](https://www.ace-eco.org/vol15/iss2/art15/)
- Acoustic detail (two-syllable "rubber ducky" call, Bahama n=1 vs continental n=10): peak frequency 6.1 kHz first syllable and 6.4 kHz second (continental 4.8 ± 0.6 and 4.6 ± 0.6 kHz); total length 0.27 s; first syllable 0.16 s; slurred descent through the first phrase spanning 4.5 kHz (continental 2.0 kHz); 0.04 s gap between phrases. Bahama males responded to Bahama calls three times as often (0.83) as to Florida calls (0.25), "often creeping down the bole of the tree to within 1 m of the playback device". — [Levy & Cox 2020](https://www.ace-eco.org/vol15/iss2/art15/)
- Further call types: described as having "a unique warbling call"; a search-result summary of the species account states it has at least five vocalizations homologous to but 2–3 kHz higher than brown-headed nuthatch, including a rapid high "warble". — [Bahama nuthatch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahama_nuthatch); [Birds of the World account (via search snippet)](https://doi.org/10.2173/bow-on.100278)
- Model: brown-headed nuthatch — "a sharp whee-hyah that sounds very similar to a 'rubber duck' toy and is notably loud for a bird of its size"; cooperative groups of 3–5 adults. — [Brown-headed nuthatch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brown-headed_nuthatch)
- Tier: RECORDED (2012 video audio; June 2018 recordings).

#### 4.8 Grand Cayman thrush — *Turdus ravidus*
- Voice: "Only Savage English (1916:26) published any record of this bird's vocalizations. He states: 'Its song was very subdued, recalling the warble of a Budgerigar ... the second time it showed itself it flew across the road, giving a 'thrush' chatter as it flew. This, like its song, was only just audible.'" — [Johnston 1969](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://sora.unm.edu/sites/default/files/journals/condor/v071n02/p0120-p0128.pdf)
- Habitat: seen 21 January 1914 in "a tangle of knife-edged coral-rock, swamp, and mangroves, with patches here and there of the poisonous manchineel tree and of climbing cactus"; same spot 27 January and 16 February. — [Johnston 1969](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://sora.unm.edu/sites/default/files/journals/condor/v071n02/p0120-p0128.pdf)
- Last seen 1938 (C. Bernard Lewis); Johnston's 1960s searches with taped red-legged thrush song found nothing. Described by Cory 1886; largest series collected by W. W. Brown Jr., April–July 1916. bbox [-81.42, 19.25, -81.08, 19.40] (my estimate; eastern and northern interior). — [Johnston 1969](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://sora.unm.edu/sites/default/files/journals/condor/v071n02/p0120-p0128.pdf)
- Tier: DESCRIBED (one observer, a few lines). Model: red-legged thrush (Johnston used its song as the playback lure).

#### 4.9 Semper's warbler — "pied-blanc" — *Leucopeza semperi*
- Voice: "The song remains undescribed. The only documented calls consist of tuck-tick-tick-tuck noises, as well as chattering sounds made when an individual is alarmed." — [Semper's warbler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semper%27s_warbler)
- Range and habitat: St Lucia; undergrowth of lower montane rainforest and elfin woodland; most records from the Barre de l'Isle ridge between Piton Flore and Piton Canaries; forages near the ground and may nest on it. bbox [-61.08, 13.70, -60.87, 14.11] (my estimate, whole island). — [Semper's warbler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semper%27s_warbler)
- Dates (conflicting): locally common in the 19th century; last collected 1934 on Piton Flore; sighting March 1947; last confirmed sighting 1961; unconfirmed 1965, 1989, 1995, 2003. Search for Lost Birds uses 1934; REPAD cites 1961, 1960s, 1972 and "1995?" from different authors; the Wikipedia list says "1970s?". Status CR. Mongoose introduction (1884) is the likely cause. — [Semper's warbler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semper%27s_warbler); [Search for Lost Birds](https://www.searchforlostbirds.org/birds); [REPAD](https://recentlyextinctspecies.com/passeriformes-passerine-birds/leucopeza-semperi)
- Tier: DESCRIBED (calls only). No close living relative (monotypic genus).

#### 4.10 Kinglet calyptura — *Calyptura cristata*
- Voice: "Its call has been described as brief, hoarse, and disagreeable, as well as surprisingly loud for a bird of its size"; no recordings on xeno-canto or Macaulay as of early 2025. — [Kinglet calyptura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinglet_calyptura)
- Range and habitat: Rio de Janeiro state; humid Atlantic Forest to about 900 m, where it was thought somewhat common in the 19th century; 1996 birds in secondary forest at about 550 m; normally in pairs, climbing lianas for insects and small berries, exploring bromeliad rosettes; believed to move seasonally in elevation. bbox [-43.3, -22.7, -42.0, -21.8] (my estimate). — [Kinglet calyptura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinglet_calyptura)
- Dates: at least 55 nineteenth-century specimens; two birds seen 27–30 October 1996 on the edge of Serra dos Órgãos National Park; nothing reliable since. (The Wikipedia article's distribution section says "October 2016" for these sightings, contradicting its own history section — 1996 is correct per Birds of the World.) Search for Lost Birds gives 1860 as last documentation. Status CR, 1–50 birds estimated. — [Birds of the World intro](https://birdsoftheworld.org/bow/species/kincal1/cur/introduction); [Kinglet calyptura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinglet_calyptura); [Search for Lost Birds](https://www.searchforlostbirds.org/birds)
- Tier: DESCRIBED (thin).

#### 4.11 Jamaican wood rail — "water partridge" / red rail — *Amaurolimnas concolor concolor*
- Voice (Gosse): "As it roams, it utters at intervals of a few seconds, a cluck, like a hen." — [Gosse 1847](https://archive.org/details/birdsofjamaica00goss)
- Habitat and behaviour: "fresh-water morasses, and secluded streams, rather than saline swamps, and is found even on the mountain acclivities"; flight "slow, heavy, and laboured". Wikipedia: swamps, jungle undergrowth and streambeds to fairly high altitudes. bbox [-78.4, 17.7, -76.15, 18.55] (my estimate, all Jamaica). — [Gosse 1847](https://archive.org/details/birdsofjamaica00goss); [Jamaican wood rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamaican_wood_rail)
- Last specimens 1881, shortly after mongoose introduction in 1872. — [Jamaican wood rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamaican_wood_rail)
- Model: mainland uniform crake — song "series of 6–20 upslurred 'tooee' whistles"; contact "clear, but not loud, whistled 'tooo' notes"; alarm "a sharp, nasal 'kek'". — [Uniform crake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_crake)
- Tier: DESCRIBED (one sentence) plus INFERRED.

#### 4.12 Tristan moorhen — *Gallinula nesiotis*
- Flightless; once abundant on Tristan da Cunha, rare by 1873, extinct by the end of the 19th century (hunting, rats, cats, pigs, fire). In 1956 the Gough moorhen was introduced to Tristan in the belief it was a reintroduction; Groenenberg et al. (2008) propose treating the two as subspecies. On Tristan the Gough moorhen is classed as non-native and unprotected. bbox [-12.36, -37.17, -12.20, -37.05] (my estimate). — [Tristan moorhen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tristan_moorhen); [Gough moorhen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gough_moorhen)
- Tier: INFERRED. Model: Gough moorhen (now living on Tristan itself).

#### 4.13 Cuban ivory-billed woodpecker — "carpintero real" — *Campephilus principalis bairdii*
- Voice and sounds (Dennis 1948, Cuchillas de Moa): "As to the Ivory-bill's voice, I have nothing to report from my notes. Mr. Crompton, however, took pains to record the exact interpretation of the calls he heard. He describes one note by the female only as a *hump* repeated slowly at intervals. The characteristic note, given by both sexes, he describes as a *hant* repeated six times." Tapping in threes and fours, scarcely louder than a downy woodpecker; "the only noisy tapping we heard was three or four taps given at slow intervals"; double raps "occasionally heard"; no drumming. — [Dennis 1948](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://sora.unm.edu/sites/default/files/journals/auk/v065n04/p0497-p0507.pdf)
- Dates: Dennis found a few birds in 1948; Lamb found six territories in 1956; last accepted sighting 1987 (a female, Ojito de Agua, hilly pine forest, eastern Cuba); searches in 1991 and 1993 failed; calls "reportedly heard in 1998" in the Sierra Maestra. bbox [-75.6, 20.2, -74.4, 20.7] (my estimate, Moa–Baracoa mountains). — [Cuban ivory-billed woodpecker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuban_ivory-billed_woodpecker)
- Tier: DESCRIBED. Model: the (already implemented) American ivory-bill recordings.

#### 4.14 Imperial woodpecker (implemented) — voice accounts, for refinement
- Nelson, near Nahuatzin, Michoacán, 1892: "just before sunrise, my curiosity was aroused by a succession of queer, nasal, penny-trumpet-like notes from the summit of a rounded hill ... The calls continued at short intervals until a little after sunrise and were the only sounds audible in the otherwise silent forest." "For so powerful a bird their notes are weak, and have the peculiar nasal tone that is characteristic of the notes of Sapsuckers, but with a penetrating quality that renders them distinct for a long distance. I am certain they were frequently heard at a distance of a mile; yet when the birds were nearby they did not sound very loud." Drumming: "the strokes are much louder and slower than those of the other species." Groups of 5 to 8–10; called at sunrise, flew to feeding grounds between eight and nine. — [Nelson 1898](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://sora.unm.edu/sites/default/files/journals/auk/v015n03/p0217-p0223.pdf)
- Lumholtz (1902): "plaintive trumpet sound"; A. A. Allen: the single bird he heard "sounded like an American Ivory-bill". Names: "pitoreal"; Nahuatl "cuauhtotomomi"; Tarahumara "cumecócari" (from a letter by C. W. Pennington). — [Tanner 1964](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://sora.unm.edu/sites/default/files/journals/auk/v081n01/p0074-p0081.pdf)
- Tier: DESCRIBED; no recording (only a 1956 amateur film). — [Imperial woodpecker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_woodpecker)

#### 4.15 St Kitts bullfinch — "mountain blacksmith" — *Melopyrrha grandis*
- By 1880 only in the montane forest of Mount Liamuiga (then Mount Misery), "not uncommon"; one specimen July 1929 (Bartsch); possible sightings 1993, 2012 (a call "similar to that of a Puerto Rican bullfinch high in the tree canopy", heard by a Puerto Rican naturalist) and a 2021 sonogram by the St Kitts Department of the Environment "that could potentially be" this species. IUCN keeps it CR pending analysis of "recent sound recordings". bbox [-62.87, 17.20, -62.60, 17.42] (my estimate). — [St. Kitts bullfinch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Kitts_bullfinch)
- Tier: INFERRED (model: Puerto Rican bullfinch), with an unverified possible recording.

#### 4.16 Black-capped petrel, "diablotin", on Dominica / Guadeloupe / Martinique (regional extirpation)
- Formerly nested on at least five islands (Jamaica, Hispaniola, Guadeloupe, Dominica, Martinique); "presumed extirpated from Martinique, Dominica, and Guadeloupe". — [Birds of the World intro](https://birdsoftheworld.org/bow/species/bkcpet/cur/introduction); [USGS Diablotin biography](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70154814)
- Dominica: once abundant; last confirmed nesting 1862 (EPIC: historical evidence suggests persistence to the 1930s); in January 2015 radar and night-vision surveys recorded 968 petrels flying over Dominica's mountains, "supplemented by detection of vocalizations". World population about 1,000–2,000 pairs. — [BirdsCaribbean 2015](https://www.birdscaribbean.org/2015/07/rediscovery-of-black-capped-petrels-on-dominica/); [EPIC](https://epicislands.org/press_item/researchers-confirm-presence-of-endangered-diablotin-black-capped-petrel-on-dominica-raising-hopes-of-finding-nesting-area/)
- Voice and name: "Diablotín, means 'little devil' because of its night-time habits and odd-sounding mating calls, which suggested to locals the presence of evil spirits in the dark." Peaks in Dominica and Haiti are still named for it; EPIC notes traditional songs and poems mention it. Eggs January, hatching March, fledging June–July. bbox Dominica [-61.50, 15.20, -61.24, 15.65]; Guadeloupe Basse-Terre [-61.81, 15.95, -61.55, 16.36] (my estimates). — [Black-capped petrel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black-capped_petrel); [EPIC](https://epicislands.org/press_item/researchers-confirm-presence-of-endangered-diablotin-black-capped-petrel-on-dominica-raising-hopes-of-finding-nesting-area/)
- Tier: RECORDED (living species) for a lost island soundscape.

#### 4.17 White-necked crow on Puerto Rico and St Croix (regional extirpation)
- Last confirmed sighting on Puerto Rico 1963, Sierra de Luquillo; crows had been shot in the Luquillo and Río Abajo areas; extirpated from St Croix too; survives on Hispaniola (Vulnerable). bbox Puerto Rico [-67.3, 17.9, -65.6, 18.5] (my estimate). — [USFWS 5-year review](https://ecosphere-documents-production-public.s3.amazonaws.com/sams/public_docs/species_nonpublish/2308.pdf); [White-necked crow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White-necked_crow)
- Voice: "quite remarkable and unusual for a corvid, described as sounding more like a parrot ... a series of liquid bubbling sounds, squawking, and babbling, mixed with sweet and harsh notes, including some that sound like the common raven"; imitates rooster crowing. Recordings on xeno-canto. — [White-necked crow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White-necked_crow)
- Tier: RECORDED (living species).

#### 4.18 Thick-billed parrot in Arizona and New Mexico (regional extirpation)
- First documented in Arizona 1583; last reliable US reports 1935 and 1938, Chiricahua Mountains; accounts describe regular visits and over-wintering; shooting is blamed. 1980s reintroduction abandoned 1993 (last bird 1995); a new attempt was announced in 2020. bbox [-111.0, 31.3, -108.2, 33.5] (my estimate; core Chiricahuas about [-109.5, 31.6, -109.1, 32.1]). — [Arizona Highways](https://www.arizonahighways.com/article/thick-thin-0); [Thick-billed parrot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thick-billed_parrot); [NPS Chiricahua](https://www.nps.gov/chir/learn/nature/thick-billed-parrot.htm)
- Voice: "resembles a high-pitched macaw and includes a variety of harsh, rolling calls described as similar to human laughter"; "piercing calls". — [Thick-billed parrot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thick-billed_parrot)
- Cultural sensitivity: thick-billed parrot bones occur in ritual burials at Wupatki and Chaco Canyon, and the bird is painted at Pottery Mound; a feather was found in a medicine fetish in Colorado. Mexican names "guacamaya", "cotorra serrana". — [Thick-billed parrot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thick-billed_parrot)
- Tier: RECORDED (living species in Mexico).

#### 4.19 Galápagos rail — "pachay" — on Floreana (lost 1835–2025; now refound)
- Confirmed 27 February 2025 by "six acoustic records, two visual confirmations, and one photograph" at three transition-zone sites (guava-overgrown grassland); last recorded by Darwin in 1835; a 1987 playback survey at 150 points found none. Researchers think it "was never truly extinct but remained undetected". bbox [-90.55, -1.38, -90.34, -1.22] (my estimate). — [Charles Darwin Foundation](https://www.darwinfoundation.org/en/news/all-news-stories/galapagos-rail-rediscovered-on-floreana/)
- Voice: at least five vocalizations — "cheeping, a *chichichichiroo* call, a *chirr* call, a chatter call, and a rattle call". — [Galapagos crake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galapagos_crake)
- Tier: RECORDED. Note it is no longer a lost voice; it fits only as a "returned" voice.

#### Minor candidates (one line each; all INFERRED unless stated)
- Bermuda petrel / cahow (historic clamour; living): Strachey — "in the darkest nights of November and December ... they would come forth, but not fly far from home, and hovering in the air and over the sea, made a strange hollo and harsh howling"; named "of the cry which it maketh". Dense, noisy colonies; up to 4,000 taken in a night (Ramirez 1603); thought extinct by the 1620s, 18 pairs refound 1951, 132 pairs by 2019. bbox [-64.90, 32.24, -64.64, 32.40] (my estimate). — [Strachey](https://www.virtualjamestown.org/TR%20modern.doc); [Bermuda petrel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bermuda_petrel)
- Dominican green-and-yellow macaw (hypothetical): "a more disagreeable, harsh noise" than the parrot; "easily discovered by their loud chattering noise, which at a distance resembles human voices" (Atwood 1791). — [Dominican green-and-yellow macaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominican_green-and-yellow_macaw)
- Lesser Antillean macaw (catalogued earlier): Du Tertre 1654 — "Their voice is loud and piercing, and they always cry when flying. If one imitates their cry, they stop short"; Labat 1742 — "its voice is strong and distinct". — [Lesser Antillean macaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lesser_Antillean_macaw)
- Puerto Rican parakeet (Mona Island): very gregarious, "loud, continuous calling"; last specimen 1882 per its page (list says 1890s). Model: Hispaniolan parakeet "kree" with "a grating noisy sound quality". — [Puerto Rican parakeet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puerto_Rican_parakeet); [Hispaniolan parakeet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hispaniolan_parakeet)
- Jamaican golden swallow: cockpit country glens and high central limestone range (Gosse gives no voice); bred June–July; last confirmed 1989. Model: Hispaniolan subspecies, "a soft, two-note call ... *tchee-weet*". — [Gosse 1847](https://archive.org/details/birdsofjamaica00goss); [Golden swallow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_swallow)
- Isle of Pines solitaire: extinct 1930s. Model: Cuban solitaire, song "high-pitched and flute-like ... melodious and varied", likened to rubbing a wet finger on glass. — [Cuban solitaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuban_solitaire)
- Grand Cayman oriole (last 1967). Model: Jamaican oriole, whistled "you cheat" or "cheat-you". — [Jamaican oriole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamaican_oriole)
- Samaná and Gonâve chat-tanagers. Models: eastern chat-tanager ("among the finest [singers] on Hispaniola", sings at dawn, "swerp, swerp, chip, chip, chip") and western ("wee-chee-chee-chee"). — [Eastern chat-tanager](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_chat-tanager); [Western chat-tanager](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_chat-tanager)
- Virgin Islands owl. Model: Puerto Rican owl — "a short, relatively deep, somewhat guttural, toad-like quavering trill", plus "a loud coo-coo" that gives the name "cuckoo bird". — [Puerto Rican owl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puerto_Rican_owl)
- San Benedicto rock wren (extinct in the 1 Aug 1952 Bárcena eruption). Model: rock wren — males have 69–119 syllables, each song a 1–2.5 s trill of repeated syllables with 3–5 s between songs. — [Rock wren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_wren)
- Goldman's yellow rail (Lerma marshes, 2,500 m, last 1964 — the same marshes as the implemented slender-billed grackle). Model: yellow rail, calling at night like "two stones being clicked together 'tik-tik tik-tik-tik'". — [Yellow rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellow_rail)
- Socorro elf owl (last recorded 1931/32). Model: elf owl, "high-pitched whinny or chuckle", 5–7 notes "similar to the sound of a young puppy", most active at dusk and before dawn. — [Elf owl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elf_owl)
- New Mexico sharp-tailed grouse (1952). Model: lek display — feet stamped "about 20 times per second", rattling tail feathers, "cooing" calls; leks average 8–12 males. — [Sharp-tailed grouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharp-tailed_grouse)
- Bahia rufous-vented ground cuckoo. Model: "a low, drawn out, moaning coo", "a muffled, barking woof call", sharp "kchak!". — [Rufous-vented ground cuckoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rufous-vented_ground_cuckoo)
- Alejandro Selkirk firecrown (1908; one author doubts a firecrown ever lived on Selkirk). Model: "a medley of high-pitched squeaky notes, dry gravelly trills and descending chatters". — [Juan Fernández firecrown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juan_Fern%C3%A1ndez_firecrown)
- Bogotá bearded tachuri. Model: "see sisi-krrak" with wing-buzzing flight display. — [Bearded tachuri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bearded_tachuri)
- St Helena hoopoe. Model: Eurasian hoopoe, soft trisyllabic "oop-oop-oop". — [Eurasian hoopoe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurasian_hoopoe)
- Ascension crake. Possible model: Inaccessible Island rail (once thought related; "a highly vocal species", long trills) — but the relationship is now considered more distant. — [Ascension crake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ascension_crake); [Inaccessible Island rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inaccessible_Island_rail)
- Chinijo stonechat (Alegranza, Montaña Clara). Model: Fuerteventura stonechat, "a ticking call like a pebble hitting another, and a high twittering song". Eastern Canary chiffchaff (Lanzarote, 1986?). Model: Canary Islands chiffchaff, song harsher, shorter, faster than common chiffchaff. — [Canary Islands stonechat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canary_Islands_stonechat); [Canary Islands chiffchaff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canary_Islands_chiffchaff)
- Cape Verde kite. Model: red kite, "a thin piping sound ... less mewling than the common buzzard". — [Red kite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_kite)
- Socorro dove (catalogued; EW since 1972): advertising call "Coo-oo, OO, OO, OO, Coo-oo". Cozumel thrasher (catalogued): song "a rich varied warbling, slightly scratchy with little repetition". — [Socorro dove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socorro_dove); [Cozumel thrasher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cozumel_thrasher)

### Inferences
- (Uncited, my reading — verify before use.) Palmer's caracara display — neck stretched out then bent back until the head rests on the back, with a "gabbling" noise — matches the head-throwback rattle display of the living crested caracara, so the crested caracara's rattle is a defensible model for that sound, with a harsher eagle-like scream for alarm.
- (Uncited, my reading.) Bryant's flicker vocabulary maps onto standard northern flicker call types: "scythe-whetting notes" and "wake-up" correspond to the rhythmic two-syllable "wicka" series; the "rapid prelude of monosyllables" to the long "wik-wik-wik" call; "quit-tu" was Bryant's own whistled imitation or the bird's — the sentence is ambiguous.
- (Uncited, my reading.) Bryant's storm-petrel transcription — a hurried four-syllable flight call answered by a two-syllable call from a bird at rest — implies a duet between a flying bird and a bird on the ground or in a burrow, in a pine forest at night, only in full darkness; the rhythm resembles the chatter call of Leach's-type storm petrels.
- The towhee and kinglet differ from their mainland models in documented ways that can be synthesised: towhee = one bluebird-like "chuck" immediately before the trill; kinglet = final disyllable repeated 3–6 (up to 8–10) times, sung through winter.
- Guadalupe's whole dawn chorus of 1886 (kinglet, towhee, flicker, junco, house finch, rock wren in cypress grove; petrels at night on the pine ridge; mostly silent caracaras at carcasses) could be built as one place-specific scene from a single source, which is rare for an extinct island fauna.
- For the purple-winged ground dove the paper's two-semitone uncertainty means absolute pitch should be treated as approximate (below ~500 Hz, perhaps ~12% higher than the tape).

### Gaps
- No measured frequencies, durations or spectrograms exist for any Guadalupe taxon, Grand Cayman thrush, Semper's warbler, kinglet calyptura, Jamaican wood rail or Cuban ivory-bill.
- Gough moorhen voice, Puerto Rican bullfinch song, red-legged thrush song, Leach's storm petrel and crested caracara calls were not described on the pages retrieved; the report writer will need a field-guide or archive source for those models.
- Whether "mountain blacksmith" refers to a hammering quality in the St Kitts bullfinch's voice is unknown; no source explains the name.
- Local or Indigenous names for the purple-winged ground dove, kinglet calyptura and Grand Cayman thrush were not found in the sources reached.
- Labat's own description of diablotin hunting and calls on Guadeloupe (1696) was not retrieved; extirpation dates for Guadeloupe and Martinique were not found.
- Seasonal and time-of-day singing data are available only where quoted above.

## 5. Ranking

### Takeaway
Ten A candidates (four truly lost with recordings or rich descriptions, three Guadalupe additions, three regional extirpations), about ten B, and the rest C or not recommended.

### Cited Findings

A — recorded or richly described; clearly worth adding
- Purple-winged ground dove — recorded 1992 in captivity (ML273900), measured: ascending, ~0.5 s, <500 Hz. — [Lees et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/ecology-and-evolution/articles/10.3389/fevo.2021.624959/full)
- Bahama nuthatch — recorded 2012 and 2018; 6.1/6.4 kHz peaks; lost around Hurricane Dorian 2019. — [Levy & Cox 2020](https://www.ace-eco.org/vol15/iss2/art15/)
- Guadalupe storm petrel — "here's-a-letter ... for you", darkest nights only. — [Bryant 1887](https://archive.org/details/bulletinofcalifo021887cali)
- Guadalupe caracara — eagle-like scream, "gabbling" with head thrown back; otherwise silent. — [Ridgway 1876](https://archive.org/details/bulletinofunited21876geol)
- Guadalupe flicker, Guadalupe towhee, Guadalupe kinglet — detailed first-hand notes, each with a living model. — [Bryant 1887](https://archive.org/details/bulletinofcalifo021887cali)
- Diablotin over Dominica/Guadeloupe; white-necked crow on Puerto Rico; thick-billed parrot in Arizona — living species with recordings, strong place-specific loss stories. — [USGS](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70154814); [USFWS](https://ecosphere-documents-production-public.s3.amazonaws.com/sams/public_docs/species_nonpublish/2308.pdf); [Thick-billed parrot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thick-billed_parrot)

B — described thinly
- Grand Cayman thrush (budgerigar-like warble, "only just audible"). — [Johnston 1969](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://sora.unm.edu/sites/default/files/journals/condor/v071n02/p0120-p0128.pdf)
- Cuban ivory-billed woodpecker ("hant" ×6; "hump"; soft tapping, double raps). — [Dennis 1948](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://sora.unm.edu/sites/default/files/journals/auk/v065n04/p0497-p0507.pdf)
- Semper's warbler (calls only). — [Semper's warbler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semper%27s_warbler)
- Kinglet calyptura (brief, hoarse, loud). — [Kinglet calyptura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinglet_calyptura)
- Jamaican wood rail (hen-like cluck every few seconds). — [Gosse 1847](https://archive.org/details/birdsofjamaica00goss)
- Bermuda cahow as a historic night clamour (Strachey), using living recordings. — [Strachey](https://www.virtualjamestown.org/TR%20modern.doc)
- Dominican macaw and Puerto Rican parakeet (generic loud-parrot descriptions). — [Dominican green-and-yellow macaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominican_green-and-yellow_macaw); [Puerto Rican parakeet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puerto_Rican_parakeet)
- Galápagos rail on Floreana (recorded, but refound 2025 — a returned rather than lost voice). — [Charles Darwin Foundation](https://www.darwinfoundation.org/en/news/all-news-stories/galapagos-rail-rediscovered-on-floreana/)
- Stresemann's bristlefront (recorded; last seen 2020; not yet declared lost). — [American Bird Conservancy](https://abcbirds.org/news/stresemanns-bristlefront-get-additional-habitat-protection/)

C — inferred only
- Tristan moorhen, St Kitts bullfinch, Isle of Pines solitaire, Jamaican golden swallow, Grand Cayman oriole, Hispaniolan chat-tanagers, Virgin Islands owl, Antiguan and Guadeloupe burrowing owls, Martinique and Guadeloupe house wrens, San Benedicto rock wren, Goldman's yellow rail, Socorro elf owl, New Mexico sharp-tailed grouse, Bahia ground cuckoo, Selkirk firecrown, Bogotá tachuri, Niceforo's pintail, St Helena hoopoe and rails, Ascension crake, Bermuda flicker/towhee/hawk/owl, Madeiran wood pigeon, Chinijo stonechat, Lanzarote chiffchaff, Cape Verde kite, Brace's emerald, New Providence yellowthroat. — [Wikipedia list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_extinct_bird_species_since_1500)

Not recommended
- Invalid or doubtful taxa: hooded seedeater, Tumaco seedeater, Rio de Janeiro antwren, Texas Henslow's sparrow, Amak song sparrow, Townsend's dickcissel, painted vulture, Cabo ani. — [Yellow-bellied seedeater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellow-bellied_seedeater); [Henslow's sparrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henslow%27s_sparrow); [Wikipedia list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_extinct_bird_species_since_1500)

- Extant: cherry-throated tanager, blue-eyed ground dove, Táchira antpitta, Santa Marta sabrewing, Belem curassow, Azores bullfinch. — [Cherry-throated tanager](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cherry-throated_tanager)

### Inferences
- Within C, the three with the best story-to-effort ratio are Tristan moorhen (its stand-in now calls on the same island), San Benedicto rock wren (an extinction timed to the minute) and Goldman's yellow rail (stone-clicking at night in the marsh where the implemented slender-billed grackle lived).
- Among C candidates, Isle of Pines solitaire has the most distinctive model voice (glass-harmonica-like Cuban solitaire).
- The Guadalupe additions are low-cost: same island and bbox as two taxa already catalogued, one shared source.

### Gaps
- Rankings rest on sound evidence only; I did not assess synthesis feasibility against the artwork's engine.
- Cultural permissions were not investigated. Flags from the sources: Pueblo ritual use of thick-billed parrots; Rarámuri (Tarahumara) and Nahuatl names for the imperial woodpecker that came through a private letter and a 1957 paper; Kalinago/Island Carib parrot keeping and trade behind the Lesser Antillean parrot accounts; the diablotin's place in Dominican song and place-names. None of the bird candidates here depends on Indigenous-informant knowledge in the way the earlier-skipped Choiseul pigeon did, as far as the sources show.
